# 2. stacks

Blake chose the carrel because it had a wall at its back and a clear line to both doors.

The library at night was a different building. The students who came after curfew were the kind who did not want to be asked what they were doing, and there were few of them: a boy asleep on his arms in Periodicals, two second-years whispering over a diagram, a woman in a cardigan going down the aisles with a cart. The librarian at the desk was a heavy man who ate oranges. He peeled each one in a single spiral and laid the peel on a saucer, and when he had finished he laid the saucer on a shelf behind him, where there were already nine. He had not looked at Blake once. She liked him a great deal.

She came because the dorm had three heartbeats in it.

She could hear them all, each at its own pace, Ruby's quick and Yang's slow and Schnee's, in the bunk across the room, so regular that it sounded like it was being kept on purpose. She could hear the sheets. She could hear someone's stomach, three nights running. It was not a thing she could explain to anyone, and she had been in the room for eleven days.

In the library there was only the building. Pipes, the high note of the lights, the librarian's thumbnail going into rind.

She wore her gloves. They were black wool, cut off at the first knuckle, and the stacks were cold. She wore the bow, too. After an hour the bow began to ache at the hinge of each ear, a dull pinch that moved up toward the temple, and she would press her thumbs there through the ribbon and wait for it to pass. It did not pass. It only got used to being ignored.

Around midnight on the third night, when the woman with the cart had gone through to the far wing, Blake took her book into the last stack, where the shelves ran out against the wall and the poetry was, and loosened the knot and let them stand. Twenty minutes. Back against the shelf. The air was cold on them and it was good.

She heard the door before she heard the feet. She had the bow tied before the feet were halfway down the aisle.

It was only Schnee.

* * *

Schnee came in at eleven, most nights. She carried an armload of folders and a thermos and put them down on the table by the east window, the one with the good lamp, and then she sat and took off her shoes.

She did it under the table. Blake knew because of the small sound the leather made on the floor. After that Schnee crossed her left foot over her right and opened the first folder and put a pencil in her mouth, and for the next three hours she was a person doing arithmetic.

The folders had a letterhead. Blake could read it from the carrel, because she could read most things from most places. A snowflake in a ring.

She had seen it stamped on crates. She had seen it on the sides of freight cars, on tarpaulins, once on a hand towel in a shack in the lower district where nobody had been able to explain to her how it got there. She knew it the way you know a smell. She looked away from it at once, at the page in front of her, and turned the page. It was the page she had been reading.

She sat with her gloved hands flat on the table until they were warm again.

Then she watched, because it was what she did. She was not proud of it. She watched in the same manner she checked doors.

Schnee mouthed numbers when she added. She sighed through her nose at footnotes. She underlined with the ruler. She had a way of pressing her thumbnail into the pad of her finger when something didn't balance, and she did it now, and now. At one in the morning she put her head on her fist; at a quarter past she lifted it again and began, quietly, to hum.

It was nothing much. Three notes, and a rest, and a phrase that climbed and came back. Eight bars. Then three notes. Schnee did not know she was doing it. Her face did not change.

Blake's ears, under the ribbon, turned toward the sound.

She felt them go. It was a small tightening at the root, as if a hand had tipped her head. She pressed her palm flat on top of the bow and held it there.

It was not the first time they had gone somewhere without asking. It was the first time they had gone toward a Schnee.

* * *

On the fifth night Schnee fell asleep at her table.

It was past two. The librarian was gone, the saucers left in their rows. Blake had given up on her page an hour before and was listening instead. At some point the pencil had stopped, and then the humming, and then there was only the breath, slower, with a catch at the top of each one, like a drawer that wasn't quite shut.

The lamp was an old brass thing on a hinged arm. It had been angled at the page. It had slipped, a little at a time, as they do, until it pointed directly at Weiss's face.

Blake sat for a while.

Then she got up and crossed the floor. She was good at floors. She knew which boards in the east wing would speak and which would stay quiet, and she took the quiet ones, and stopped beside the table with her heart going at its ordinary rate.

Weiss's mouth was a little open. Her hair had come out of its tie on one side. There was a mark on her cheek where the edge of a folder had pressed. The scar over her left eye looked paler in this light than it did in the day, and that was an odd thing to know.

Blake put one finger on the lamp's neck and turned it, two inches, down. Away from the eyes. Onto the paper.

The top folder lay open. She did not mean to read it. The first line was a heading in capitals — *Q3 HOUSING AND WAGE DEDUCTIONS, MANTLE OPERATIONS* — and her eye took that much before she shut it, by effort, the way you shut a hand.

She went back to her carrel by the quiet boards. She did not look at the table again. She listened to the breath until it was time to go, and went out before it woke.

* * *

"You moved the lamp."

It was the next night, a little after eleven. Blake was in the doorway of the reading room with her book against her chest and she stopped there, because there was nothing else to do.

Weiss was at her table with the shoes already off. She had not looked up.

"What lamp?"

"The one on my table. When I sat down last night, it was pointed at the window. When I woke it was pointed at the page."

"The cleaners."

"At three in the morning."

"They're very dedicated."

The pencil kept moving. After a moment Weiss said, "Thank you. Whoever it was."

Blake stood in the door. The building hummed. At the desk, the librarian split an orange with his thumbs, and the smell crossed the room, sharp and bright.

"The light's better over here," said Weiss, to the folder. "If you're going to loiter."

"I'm not loitering. I'm reading."

"From over there. By one bulb."

"It's low," said Blake.

The pencil stopped.

"Yes," said Weiss. "I recall."

Blake crossed the floor. She took the table opposite, two chairs down from the end, so that there was a whole chair between them and the window at her shoulder. The lamp here was better. It was on a hinge. She did not touch it.

She opened the book where the waiver was. She read the same sentence four times.

Across the table, a pencil began again, and a page, and a long time later the humming, very quietly, the three notes and the rest and the phrase that climbed. Blake kept her ears where they were by main force. It felt like holding a door shut against weather.

Around four the librarian turned off the lights in the east wing, one bank at a time. They went on reading by the two lamps until he came and stood in the doorway and said, "Ladies," and nothing else.

She was in the corridor, buttoning her coat, before she found she had one glove.

She stood and looked at her hand for some time. Then she kept walking.
