# 19. epiphany

The dog had stood at the door since noon.

He had not barked. He stood with his nose to the crack at the bottom, rigid, with the one ear up and the other down and his tail held low and moving, slowly, from side to side, like a man testing a floor. When she spoke to him he did not turn. When she set a bowl beside him he looked at it as at a thing from another life.

"It's only the wind," said Weiss.

It was. It had been blowing for three days. It came down off the hill in long hard sheets and struck the hut with a flat sound, like a hand on a table, and went over the roof and along the camp and out of the valley with a howl that had a note in it, a low one, and held. The window had gone white. She could not see the bunkhouse, or the water-tower, or the tram. She had lit the lamp in the sill at noon, and she could not have said why.

She went back to the desk. She picked up the pencil and put it down.

At four the light went. At five she lit the second lamp, and fed the stove, and set the kettle on, and stood over it with her arms folded, listening to it begin. She heard the wind. She heard the stovepipe tick. She heard the dog's nails shift on the boards, and stop.

And then he barked.

Once. A short, hollow, upward bark, as at a person known. There was a pause. He barked again.

She turned round.

* * *

She took the rapier. It was in her hand before she had thought about it; she had it off the peg and out of its sheath, and she crossed the room with the point low. She did not know what she was expecting. A trapper; a lost man; a thing the wind had brought. In the weeks of the camp she had imagined, now and then, in the dark, all the ways in which someone might arrive.

The dog was frantic. He was scratching at the wood with both forepaws. A strange high noise was coming out of him, not a whine, a sort of pleading chatter, the sound of an animal that cannot say a thing in its own language.

She lifted the bar.

The wind took the door out of her hand. It came in with a roar and a wall of white, and the lamp on the sill went flat and sprang up again, and the cold struck her across the face like a board. She got her shoulder to the door. She held it a hand's width. Snow whirled in over the sill and settled on the boards, and the dog went out through the gap and was gone.

She stood with the point of the rapier raised, and looked into the dark.

There was a shape in it.

It stood at the edge of the light, where the lamp's yellow gave out, about eight yards from the door. It was white to the knees. Above that it was grey, and tall, and bulky, with a high rough head. For some seconds she thought it was not a person. It was the wrong size. It had two peaks at the top of its head, and she thought, with perfect clarity, *Beowolf,* and set her feet.

The dog had reached it. He stood against its legs. He was leaning his whole weight into the knees, and the shape put down a hand into the white and rested it on his head.

It was a hand. In a glove. The glove had no fingers. Where the fingers came out they were red.

The head turned, a little, toward the lamp.

There was a scarf. It was wound many times round the skull, thick, and crusted with ice, and under the scarf, pushing up the wool in two peaks, were two shapes with a rim of snow on each, as there is on a roof. The snow had settled and held. It lay along the top of each in a thin white cap.

She understood the ears before she understood anything else.

And then, in the lamplight, from somewhere under the wool, two small lights came on. They were round and gold and perfectly steady. They looked at her out of the storm, as a thing looks out of a wood, and did not blink.

* * *

She did not move.

It is hard to say what she did. She was holding a rapier, in a doorway, in a gale, and her hand did not shake and her feet did not shift, and every part of her had gone quiet in the way a hall goes quiet when someone steps onto a stage. She was aware of the cold on her face. She was aware of the dog. She was aware that the lamp was behind her and that her shadow went out over the snow, a long thin one, and stopped at the other's boots.

Nothing in her training covered it.

She had imagined it. She had imagined it in forty-nine ways, one for each of the days; at the stove, at the desk, on the track to the river. She had imagined sentences. She had had a good many. They were in very fine order. In none of the forty-nine had there been snow.

The figure lifted its head.

The scarf slipped. It was a small movement. It uncovered a mouth, and a jaw, and a strip of face with the colour gone out of it, grey-white and cracked at the lips; and the voice, when it came, was so hoarse and so low that the wind took half and she had to watch the lips to have the rest.

"He's dead."

That was all. It was the first thing. She had not expected a first thing.

She knew who. There was only the one *he.* It was in the voice. It had been in everything the voice had ever not said.

She stood. The point of the rapier had dropped, by some inches, and she did not know when. She heard her own heels, on the boards, shift. She thought, with a great stillness, as of someone who finds a coin on the road, *That is what she came to say.*

The wind blew.

It blew in at the door past her shoulder and across the room and the kettle began to scream on the stove, thin and high, a long way off. The dog was whining. Snow was coming in over the sill and lying down in the lamplight, in a drift, in a white tongue, across the floor.

Weiss opened her mouth.

She had forty-nine sentences. She said the one that came.

"Come in. You're letting the heat out."

* * *

The figure stood a moment longer.

Then something went across the face, quickly, so quickly that she could not have said what it was, and was gone. The head ducked. A foot came up out of the white and was put down on the sill.

Weiss stepped back.

She stood against the wall with the rapier in her hand. The other came in past her, stooping under the lintel, and the cold came with it, a clean hard radiance that smelled of ice and wool and smoke and something else, faint and sweet and unplaceable, like an orange peel in a coat. Snow slid from the shoulders onto the boards. It was tall. It was not tall at all. It stood in the middle of the floor, in the lamplight, with the scarf half off, and breathed.

The dog came in last. He went round the legs twice, in a tight circle, and sat down on the boots.

Weiss put her shoulder to the door, and it closed. She dropped the bar.

The roar went out of the room as if a hand had been laid across it. In the sudden quiet she could hear the stove, the kettle, the small tick of the lamp wick. Their breathing. Her own.

She turned round. She had the rapier still in her hand, and she looked at it as at a thing she had not seen before; then she set it, carefully, on the desk, point toward the wall.

Blake stood in the middle of the floor, with her ears bare, and snow melting on them.
