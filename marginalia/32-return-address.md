# 32. return address

She wrote four letters, and the fourth took eleven days.

The first was to her mother. It was short, and she wrote it in a morning. *I am well. I'm sorry about the dinner. I am thinking of you. I hope you are eating.* She read it twice. She added, after a moment, *Please do not tell Father where this came from,* and then struck that out, and wrote, *Tell him if you like,* and sat for some time looking at it. It was the first honest thing she had ever said to Willow, and it was plain that it would be understood.

The second was to Ruby, who had a right to more. It was four pages. She wrote it at the desk in the afternoons, with the window open and the thaw dripping in the lean-to, in her good hand, and it was a poor letter, long and anxious and full of crossings-out, and at the end of it she wrote, *I know. I said I would write. I'm writing,* and signed it with her whole name.

The third was to Winter.

She did that one three times. It had been written, as she'd told Blake, three weeks before, and lain in a box, and every line of it was true; and she read it over on the morning after the first drip and found that it said *I* eleven times and *we* none. She took a clean sheet. The second draft said *we* twice. She sat with her pen over the third, in the cold bright light, and wrote, in a steady hand:

*I am at Camp Four. I am not alone. I have a question to ask you, and I am going to ask it directly, because I have lately found out that this is possible.*

She asked.

It was the fourth that took the time.

* * *

"May I read it?"

"Yes."

Blake had come in from the river. She stood at the desk with her sleeves pushed up and a smell of water on her. She picked up the sheet. It was in Weiss's hand, but not her good hand; it was a smaller, harder, more upright one, which Blake had seen once on a roster, copying names.

*To the Council of Atlas.*

*I am Weiss Schnee, daughter of Jacques Schnee. I write from Camp Four, Hollin Gap, which is where I am.*

*I enclose a copy of the log of Mine No. 4. It records that the roof of Level 3 was reported to the foreman on the thirty-first, thirty-sixth, thirty-eighth, thirty-ninth and fortieth days of winter. I enclose a copy of the roster of those who died. I enclose a copy of a letter I signed in the summer, and a copy of the page of it that I did not read.*

*The Company regrets.*

*I do not ask for anything. I ask that it be read.*

She read it twice. She laid it down.

"Cut *regrets,*" she said.

"Why?"

"It's their word." Blake tapped the sheet. "It's the plate on the wall. *The Company regrets.* It isn't yours."

Weiss looked at it.

It was a long look. Then she took the pen, and drew a single clean line through the sentence, from the first letter to the full stop, and blotted it, and looked at what was left.

"It reads better," she said.

"It does."

"I hadn't heard it."

"You'd copied it." Blake sat on the edge of the desk. Her ears were forward, and the light from the window ran along them. "You've been reading it for five months."

"Yes." Weiss turned the pen over in her fingers. "Would you read it again? I should like a second reader."

"Yes."

"Slowly."

"I'm always slow."

"You are a wonderful reader," said Weiss. "I should like that noted."

Blake read it again.

She went down it with her lips just moving, as she did with a hard page, and Weiss, watching her, found that her own hands had gone cold. It was not the letter. It was the next thing, the thing at the foot of it. She had left a space, a line and a half, and she had left it on purpose, and she did not know whether she could fill it.

"The address," said Blake.

"Yes."

"You've put it at the top."

"I've put it at the top." She heard her own voice. "It says *Camp Four, Hollin Gap.* It's the truth. It will go to Atlas by the post, on a Schnee seal, and it will be opened by a clerk, and read by another clerk, and there will be a copy made, as there always is. My father has a man in every office that counts. Within a fortnight he will know to the mile where I am."

"Yes."

"I had thought it would matter more."

"Does it?"

"I think it does." She took a breath. "I think it matters a great deal, and I think I'm going to put it anyway. Is that—" She stopped. "Is that a thing a person can do?"

Blake considered.

"I did it," she said. "On a strand. I think it's most of what it is."

* * *

Tove came on the Thursday, with the fish.

She came up the track in the wet, on foot, with the sled dragging behind her on bare stones where the snow had gone, and her ears dark with sweat at the roots; and she stood in the door of the hut, panting, and looked at the three envelopes on the desk, and the fourth, with its seal.

"Hm," she said.

"I'd like you to take them."

"To the depot."

"To the post. To Mantle. They'll go from there." Weiss had her hands folded. "There is one more thing. I should like to ask you whether you'd put your name to this. You needn't."

She held out the copy of the Council letter. The old woman took it, in her brown hands, and read it close, with her lips moving. She read all of it. At the end she looked up, and her pale eyes went over Weiss's face.

"Neat hand," she said.

"Yes."

"You've put your address."

"Yes."

"He'll find you."

"I know."

"Hm." Tove folded the sheet, and unfolded it, and ran her thumb along the crease. "It matters, you know. You've only decided to let it."

"Yes."

"That's not the same."

"No."

"It's better," said Tove Dahl.

She did not say anything else. She sat down on the bench by the stove and pulled the pen toward her across the desk, and unscrewed the cap, and found the place at the foot where the line and a half had been left. She wrote, slowly, in the square rough hand that pressed hard on the capitals:

*Tove Dahl, Hollin Gap. Widow of Halvard Dahl, Miner Grade II, No. 4, twenty-two years.*

She capped the pen.

"I'll get the others," she said. "Tomorrow. There's nine would sign. There's two would sooner see you hang." She looked at Weiss. "I'll tell them it's your hand, and not your father's. They'll know the difference."

* * *

It came back with nine names.

They were on a single sheet, folded in thirds, and Tove laid it on the desk like a card on a table. They were in nine hands, in pencil, in ink, once in what looked like soot. *We the undersigned have read this.* There was an *X.* There was an old man's wavering copperplate, and a boy's round print, and, at the bottom, an unlettered mark in grey lead, the size of a thumbprint, quite delicate.

It was a bird.

It had a round body and a short tail and a small hooked beak, drawn in a dozen sure strokes, with a dot for an eye. Nobody had said whose.

"The thin one's sister," said Tove. "She's got no letters. She says it's all she needs."

Weiss looked at it.

She did not say anything. She stood with her hand at the edge of the desk, and looked, and the stove ticked, and in the lean-to the water ran off the roof in a long steady fall; and she thought of a margin in a log, and a little bird drawn there by a man who had liked the temperature to be right, and who had told a foreman five times that the roof was going to come in.

"Thank you," she said.

"Not me."

"Thank them."

"I did," said Tove. "I told them you said it." She tugged her cap. "They said: *good.*"

Weiss read the whole sheet twice, as she did. She read the names, one at a time, in their different hands. Then she signed.

It was not a long signature. It was *W. Schnee,* upright, clear, and a little too large. She did it in ink, under the nine, with the pen she had had since she was twelve.

She sealed the letters with her own seal, a small cold stamp of a snowflake in a ring, which she had had in the bottom of the hatbox, and which she pressed into the wax and held, a count of five, until it had taken. She looked at it for a time. It was an exact mark. Then she took the point of the penknife and drew a single line through the centre, from rim to rim, so that the ring was split.

"That's defacing a seal," said Blake, from the door.

"Yes."

"Is it legal?"

"It's mine," said Weiss. "I'll find out."

* * *

The waiting was long.

It was a spring in the way that it is a spring in the north, which is to say it did nothing for days, and then rained for one night and was in its sixth week. The roofs dripped. The river went brown and loud. The ice at the bend broke up in great grey plates and went off down the valley with a noise like furniture. Each morning she woke to a new sound, and each night the stars were closer.

She learned to say it.

*Staying?* was the first thing, in the morning, over the stove, in whichever voice came; and *Today* was the answer; and the question did not get easier, and the answer did not get easier, and she had found, to her slight surprise, that this was the point. It was a thing like the tune. You went through it every time, from the bottom, and arrived at the top, and held it a little too long.

She stood a good deal at the window.

She watched the track. It had gone from white to grey to a sort of thin black ribbon, running between drifts, and she watched it as one watches a kettle, with the same grave, unreasonable conviction that if she looked away it would not come. Blake, at the table behind her, mended a boot. She did it very badly. Neither of them said anything about the track.

On the eleventh day the dog got up and went out.

He went without being asked. He went to the end of the yard, and stood with his one ear up and the other down, and looked down the valley with the air of a creature that has heard its own name said, in a far room, by someone it likes; and a quarter of an hour later, round the shoulder of the spur, small, there came a sled.

* * *

It was Brand. He had come as far as the foot, and Tove had gone down to meet him, and she came up the last of the track with a sack on her back and a second in her arms, bent double, with her ears flat against the strain and a look of grim and magnificent annoyance.

"He says it's the first post since the ninth," she said. "He says it's *irregular.*" She dropped the sacks on the desk. "He says there's a woman at the depot has been asking for the Schnee girl by name. He says he told her he'd never heard of one."

"Thank you."

"I'd have done it free."

"I know."

She went through the sack with her hands not steady. There was a good deal. There was a thick envelope in four colours of ink, with a small rose in the corner, which she put on the desk, unopened, and laid her palm on, as one settles a child. There was a postcard with a drawing on it of an orange, ferociously shaded, and on the back, in a large square hand, *A MONTH IS UP. —Y.* There was a note, two lines, in a hand she did not know, from someone who signed himself *Klein,* which said that Mrs. Abney sent her regards and that the cook wished it known that the bread had been very stale.

And at the bottom, in a cream envelope, with the crest of a House stamped in the corner and her own name in a hand like a ruled line: a letter.

She knew the hand.

She stood at the desk and held it. It was thin. It weighed almost nothing. It was a single sheet, folded in three; and under her thumb she could feel, through the paper, the faint ridge where a pen had pressed.

"Do you want me to go?" said Blake.

"No."

"I can."

"I'd like you to stay." She heard it as she said it, and did not take it back. "I'd like you to read it. After. If you'd like."

"Yes."

She opened it.

> *Weiss,*
>
> *Yours of the ninth reached me on the fifteenth by a route I will not describe. I am glad you are alive. I was angry for eleven days. I find I am not any longer, and I am not certain when it stopped.*
>
> *I have read the log. I have put it in the hands of a person who will read it to the end. I make no promise about what follows. I can promise only that it will not go in a drawer.*
>
> *You ask about the nurse.*
>
> *Her name was Maren Holt. She was with us from before you could walk. Father dismissed her in the spring of your seventh year. The occasion was that she was heard to sing to you at night, in a dialect of the lower city, and he said it was not suitable. The reason was that you cried for her and not for Mother. I was sixteen, and old enough to have said something. I stood in the hall and watched her go down the drive with her case. I said nothing. I have not forgiven myself for it. I have learned that this is a fact about me, and not a punishment.*
>
> *She went to Mantle. I looked for her the first year I had leave. I found a Maren Holt in a boarding house on Lamp Street, who had died the winter before. I could not establish that it was the same woman. It is a common name. I did not look further. I should have, and I did not, and I am sorry that it has taken me until now to say her name to you.*
>
> *As to your eye. You were five. You went through the glass of the east door on the third-floor landing; you were running; it was the first week of summer. Maren found you. She held your head in her apron until the doctor came. It was she who told Father it was glass. He told you a Grimm, I think, because he had decided that a Schnee does not fall through a door.*
>
> *I am told you are with someone. I do not ask who. I note that you used the word* we *four times in a letter of two pages.*
>
> *Eat.*
>
> *W.*

She read it once.

She read it again. She read it with great care, from the first line to the last, in the thin clear light of the window. At the second reading she came to *glass,* and stopped there.

*A light. A white light, close.*

She stood still. A door, in summer, at noon. The sun through it. A great flat sheet of white, a thing she had run at, as a child runs, with arms out, thinking that it was air.

*She held your head in her apron.*

Her hand had begun to shake. She put the letter down on the desk, flat, with her palm on it, to keep it from fluttering.

"Weiss."

"I'm all right," she said. "No. I'm — it's not that." Her voice was perfectly level. "It's that I've got a name."

She turned her head. Blake was beside her, a foot off. She had not moved to touch her. She stood with her hands at her sides and her ears forward, and her face, in the clear light, was open all the way down.

"Maren," said Weiss.

She said it aloud.

It was easy. It came out of her mouth like any word. It was a short soft name, with a round vowel in the middle, and it went into the air of the hut, among the stove and the cups and the sound of the water, and did not break.

"Maren Holt."

"Maren," said Blake.

The dog, at the door, lifted his head.

"Maren," said Weiss, a third time, to the kettle. And it came to her, in a long cold tremor that went the whole length of her, that it was a thing one could do. One could say the name. It could be said in the open, in a plain voice, in an ordinary room, to a person who was listening, and the person would say it back.

She sat down in the chair with one arm.

She took the marbled notebook from the shelf, the one with the thirty-one, and turned to the last page, the clean one. She uncapped the pen. She wrote in her best hand, upright, round-vowelled, unhurried, as one writes a name on a door:

*Maren Holt.*

She looked at it. She did not draw a line under it, or add a date. After a moment, under it, in a smaller hand, she wrote the only other word the page wanted.

*Ask.*

"Lamp Street," said Blake.

"Tove will know."

"Tove will know."

"Tomorrow," said Weiss.

"Tomorrow." Blake put her hand on the back of the chair. She did it slowly, in plain sight, with a small deliberate pressure; it lay along the wood an inch from Weiss's shoulder. "May I?"

"Yes."

The hand came down.

It was warm. It was heavy, a little, and rough at the knuckles, and entirely steady. It rested on her shoulder, through the wool, for a long time, and neither of them said anything at all.
