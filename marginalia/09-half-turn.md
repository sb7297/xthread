# 9. half-turn

She had not worn the dress her father's tailor sent.

It had come in a long white box in a nest of tissue, with a card: *For the Vytal Festival. Appropriate.* It was white satin with a great deal of lace at the collar and the sleeves, and she had held it up against herself in the mirror and seen, with great clarity, a girl in a drawing of a girl. She put it back in the box. She went into Vale on a Saturday and bought a different one, with her own money, in a shop whose assistant had not known her name. It was plain, and the colour of the sky an hour before snow, and the neckline did not do anything it had not been asked to.

She was fairly sure it was the best decision she had made all year.

The ballroom was very full and warm. The delegations from the four kingdoms had come in their best, and the best of Atlas was grey and silver and slightly stiff; the best of Vacuo was loose and bright and had a good deal of gold; and a man from Mistral had been explaining to her for eleven minutes about his lizard. She had said "Indeed" and "How interesting" and "Oh, no" in rotation. She had been asked to dance twice. She had declined, politely, with a smile that was a piece of engineering.

At the far end Ruby was on the floor, doing something that was not a dance and not quite a fight. Jaune and Pyrrha were turning slowly in the centre, with the gravity of two people being careful with something fragile. Yang had a boy by the hand and was leading him around like a dog on a lead, delighted. The orchestra played, in three, and in three, and in three.

Blake was not in the room.

Weiss had known this for a while. She had counted the room twice and had not found her, and had been unable to account for why she was counting.

She said "Excuse me," to the lizard, and went to find some air.

* * *

The terrace ran along the east side, behind long glass doors. It was cold and empty, with a stone balustrade and a view over the dark cliff to the lights of the city. The music came through the glass at a distance, softened, as if the room were being played in another room.

Blake stood at the railing.

She was in black. It was a plain black dress with a high collar, which she had borrowed from Yang and which had been taken in at the waist by someone with a needle and a bad temper, and her arms were bare. She had her back to the doors. She had no ribbon on.

Her ears were up, in the dark, turned out toward the cliff.

Weiss stopped.

She did not make a sound; she was almost sure of it. But one ear swung back, and then the other, and Blake said, without turning round, "It's loud in there."

"Yes."

"I'm not hiding. It's only that it's louder."

"I didn't say you were."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say it's cold."

"It is," said Blake. She turned. She looked at the dress for some time, at the colour of it, and at Weiss in it, and then at the cliff again. "It looks nice."

"Thank you."

"I mean the dress."

"I know what you meant," said Weiss, and discovered that she had gone to stand at the railing a yard from her, which she had not decided to do.

For a while they watched the lights. A long way below there was a train, going into the dark of the forest, with a string of lit windows. The music changed behind them and began a new thing in three-four time, slower, a waltz, with a violin out in front.

"I can waltz," said Weiss.

She had no idea why she said it.

"I can't."

"It's counting."

"Is it."

"It's three. That's all it is. One, two, three. Most people can't count past two, and then they hold it against the dance." She had turned to face her. She was already holding out her hand, palm up. It was an absurd thing to be doing, on a terrace, in a cold wind. "Come here."

Blake looked at the hand. She looked at it for what felt like some while. Then she walked the yard between them, and stopped, and did not put her own hand into it.

"Where."

"Here." Weiss took her right and laid it flat on her own upper arm, above the elbow, over the thin cloth. She took the left and held it in her own, out to the side. It was warm. It was warmer than she had expected, and it was perfectly dry. "Your other hand is — no, I'll — there. My shoulder blade. Lightly. It's a handle, not a ledge."

"Okay."

"Now. Forward, and to the side, and together. And back. That's one box. Count."

"One, two, three."

"Out loud, and *on the beat.*"

They moved. It was badly done. Blake went forward when she should have gone back and then, over-correcting, back when she should have gone forward, and for four bars they were two people carrying a table through a door. Then her heel came down on Weiss's instep.

"Sorry."

"It's fine."

"I stepped on you."

"It is *fine.* Again. One, two, three."

They went round. The wind took the end of Weiss's hair and put it across her face, and she did not move to put it back. She counted. She counted aloud for a time, and then more quietly, and somewhere in the third pass she stopped, and neither of them said anything about it.

It came together all at once, as it does. A wrong foot became a right foot. The box turned into a turn. And with a small shock, like a stair that is not there, Weiss found that she was no longer leading. She was being led, slightly, by a quiet hand on her back, and she was following, and it was easy.

Blake's ears were forward. Both of them. They were pointed at her face, as at a sound, and the tips were just visible against the dark. They did not move.

She was watching Weiss with great attention. She had the look, thought Weiss, of a person reading something in a language she had learned alone, and was now hearing spoken for the first time.

The music went on. The train, below, had gone into the trees.

At some point it changed. The waltz ended, and a quick thing in four began inside, and neither of them changed what they were doing; they went on, in three, against it, turning in the cold in the light that came out through the glass. A couple went by on the other side of the door and looked out. Weiss saw them look. She did not break the step.

Then somebody inside laughed, loudly, on a high note, and the sound was ordinary. It was a party sound.

She stopped.

She did it all at once, with her heels together, and took her hand from the warm hand, and her other hand from the arm, and stepped back, so that the cold came in between them like water into a footprint.

"That was adequate," she said.

"High praise."

"It was meant as such."

"I know," said Blake. Her ears had lowered, a little, by a degree. They stayed where they were.

Weiss turned and went in. She went in rather fast. She did it with her chin up and her hand on the glass door, which was heavy, and she shut it behind her and stood a moment with the noise and the heat and the scent of lilies and the lizard man bearing down on her from the left.

She took a glass of something from a tray. Her hand was not steady. She noticed it from a long way off. She drank half and set the glass down on a pillar.

* * *

From the table by the window you could see the terrace through the doors.

She had taken a chair there without having decided to. She sat straight with her hands in her lap. The lizard man had been replaced by a woman from Vacuo who wished to discuss the price of Dust, and Weiss had said "Yes," and "I couldn't say," and "I'm afraid that's a matter for my father."

Out on the terrace, in the square of light, there was one person.

She had put her hands down. She was standing with her feet together. Then, in the middle of nothing, with her ears up and her head slightly bent, she took a step forward and to the side, and one together, and one back.

She was counting. Weiss could not hear it. She could see it, in the mouth. *One, two, three.*

Forward. Side. Together. Back.

The woman from Vacuo was saying something about tariffs.

Under the table, in the shadow of the cloth, Weiss's foot had begun to move. She noticed. She did not stop it. She sat with her hands in her lap, and her chin up, and her eyes on the glass, and without knowing she did it she began, almost soundlessly, to hum.
