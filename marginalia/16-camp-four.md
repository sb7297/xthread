# 16. camp four

The sled driver left her at the foot of the gap, where the track gave out.

He was a man called Brand, with a face the colour of a boot. He had said eleven words since the depot, six of them about the weather. He lifted her case off the runners and set it in the snow, and stood a moment holding the reins, and looked up the valley.

"There's nothing up there, miss."

"I know."

"Nobody's been up since the spring." He chewed something. "They've a wall on it."

"I know."

"I come by the thirtieth, to the foot, if you've not changed your mind."

She paid him from the envelope. He counted it with a thumb, and nodded, and clicked to the dogs, and the sled went away down the white track, getting smaller, the bells on the harness going thin and then going out. Then there was no sound at all.

She stood in the snow in the coachman's coat. She had a case, a bundle of bread, a rapier, and a hatbox on a strap. She looked at the valley, and began to climb.

* * *

It was a shelf of land above a frozen river.

She came on it in the last of the light. The track bent round a spur of rock and there it was, laid out below the cliff like a thing put down and not picked up again: a long low roof, a squarer one, a row of smaller huts under their burden of snow, a tangle of black poles, and a dead tram on a spur of rail that went into a drift and did not come out. A flag-pole without a flag. A water tower. The chimneys were all cold.

And at the back, against the face of the hill, a square of darkness cut in the rock. It was the width of two carts. It had been closed. Fresh stone, fitted close, the colour of new bread, filled the whole of the opening and ran a little way out on either side; across it, nailed through a timber, was a sign.

She did not go near it.

She stood on the track and looked at the camp for some time. She had been in quiet places. She had stood in the Emerald Forest at dawn, and in the Hall of Banners when it was empty, and once at the bottom of the east well at home, where the air was cold and the walls close. This was a different kind. It was the quiet of a place that had been left. It had a smell, even in that cold: a faint iron smell, and of old coal, and of the sweet thing that a house gives off when no one has lit a fire in it for a very long time.

The key was on a wire round her neck. The tag said *No. 4 — Insp.* in Klein's small copperplate.

The inspector's house was the squarer roof. It stood a little apart, at the head of the camp, with its door to the wind. The lock was stiff. She put her shoulder to it. It opened with a noise like a long breath drawn in, and the dark inside came out to meet her, and a smell of cold ash and mouse.

* * *

Two rooms and a stove. Klein had been precise.

The first room was an office. A desk, a chair with one arm, a steel cabinet with its drawers pulled out and empty. A map of the workings on the wall, curling, in the cabinet's shadow. On a shelf by the window, a radio set the size of a small trunk, with a handset on a coiled cord, and a row of dials, and a layer of dust like felt. The second room had a cot with a grey blanket, folded, and a washstand, and a shelf. That was all.

The stove stood in the middle of the floor, a black iron barrel on four legs, with a pipe going up.

She had never lit one.

She was clear on the principle. She had read of it. She found paper in the cabinet, and a box of long matches, and a crate of split wood in the lean-to outside, and she built it in the manner she supposed correct: paper at the bottom, small wood on that, large wood on that. She struck a match and held it to the corner. The paper caught, and flared, and went out. She did it again. She did it eight times. By the ninth the box was half empty and the room was no warmer, and her hands, in the two gloves, had gone numb, and through the window the last of the light had gone.

She stood up.

She went to the case, and took Myrtenaster from its sheath, and turned the chamber until she found a crystal of the lowest red. It was meant for an emergency. She held it a moment, with a feeling of faint shame, as a woman might who has been defeated by a jar. Then she shook one grain of it out onto the paper and struck the match.

The wood went up with a *whump.*

She stepped back. The fire roared in the barrel and the pipe began, alarmingly, to tick. Heat came out at her in a wave, and she stood there in the coachman's coat with her face lit orange, and she realised that she was laughing, in the empty office, in a voice she did not use, quite quietly, and could not stop.

She sat down on the floor.

The room filled. It was warm in an hour. It was hot in two, and she opened the window and put her head out, in the dark, and breathed the cold; and below her the long roofs of the camp lay under the stars, unlit, in rows, like a field of something sown.

She ate Mrs. Abney's bread and the heel of the cheese. She slept in the coat on the cot.

* * *

The log was in the drawer of the desk.

She found it on the second morning, looking for a pencil. A thick book bound in grey canvas, the corners worn through to the board, with a label gummed on the cover in capitals: *RADIO & WEATHER — No. 4.* There was a string tied round it, knotted in a bow, and a pencil on the end of the string.

She opened it at the middle.

It had been ruled by hand, in columns. *Date. Wind. Temp. Remarks. Init.* The entries ran down the pages in a dozen different hands, in pencil, in a cramped hasty style, with the careless abbreviations of people who had a great deal to do and a short time to do it in.

*12th. NNE light. −9. Pay late again. A.V.*

*13th. NNE. −11. Pay Thursday, they say. D.Q.*

She read them as one reads an unfamiliar road, one sign at a time. Wind, cold, pay, a stove pipe cracked. A man named P.O. had been, on the 17th, "sick of beans." A man called E.V. wrote the temperature to a decimal and underlined it. On the 22nd someone had drawn, in the margin of the Remarks, a small and recognisable bird.

She turned the page.

*31st. N mod. −19. Props on 3 gone soft, east drift. Told Mr. H. A.V.*

*36th. N. −21. Noise in the roof on 3. Told Mr. H. He says Thursday. D.Q.*

*38th. N. −22. Thursday. Nothing. Told Mr. H. P.O.*

*39th. Calm. −24. Told Mr. H. E.V.*

*40th. Calm. −25. Roof noise on 3. Told Mr. H. Again. E.V.*

That was the last. Under it the ruled lines went on, clean, down to the bottom of the page, and over, and through the rest of the book. They were all empty.

She read it twice. She read it a third time.

She was aware of the stove ticking behind her. She was aware of a bird, somewhere, outside, making a small unmusical noise in the cold. She stood at the desk in the grey light with the book in both hands, and she noticed that she was holding it very carefully, the way one holds something that is still warm.

Mr. H. She knew him. *Harrow, T., Foreman.* She had copied it herself in the best of her hands.

*Again.*

There was no one to tell. There was a report in a black leather binder, with a table on page nineteen, and a determination that the engineers had found no negligence. It was dated nine days after the fortieth. She thought, with great calm, that it was a very good document. It was laid out in columns.

She sat down in the chair with one arm. She untied the string, and drew the pencil to her, and turned to the first blank line.

*Date.* She did not know what to put. She wrote, after some thought, *Day 1.*

*Wind.* She went to the window and looked at the snow on the sill. *NW, light.*

*Temp.* There was a thermometer on the frame, a brass one, with a glass tube. She read it. *−20.*

*Remarks.* She sat for a long time with the point on the paper. In the end she wrote what was so.

*Arrived. Stove lit (at the ninth attempt, with assistance).*

*Init.*

She wrote *W.S.* in her best hand, and underlined it, and then, because it was a log, and a log is for others, she looked at it and put a full stop after each letter.

* * *

On the third day she went up to the wall.

She did not mean to. She had a bucket in her hand and was on her way to the river to break the ice, and her feet went by themselves, up the slope, along the length of the long roof, past the dead tram, until she was standing in front of it in the snow with the bucket hanging off her arm.

It was bigger close to. The stone was clean. The mortar, between the blocks, was pale and had not yet weathered. At the top, nailed through a baulk of timber, a metal sign, stamped, black on yellow: *DANGER. NO ENTRY. PROPERTY OF S.D.C.* Below it, on a little brass plate screwed neatly into the stone at the height of a man's chest, someone had cut four lines.

*MINE No. 4. CLOSED IN THE INTERESTS OF SAFETY.*

*THE COMPANY REGRETS.*

She read it. She had a feeling like having something pulled slowly out of the middle of her.

She had the roster by heart. She had had it since the third night. She stood there, with the wind coming down off the hill, and she opened her mouth to say it. The first name was *Anselm Voe.* It was a good name, short, easy to say. She had said it a thousand times in her head.

Nothing came.

She tried again. She shut her mouth, and opened it, and put her tongue to the roof of it. She knew exactly how to do it. She could take a breath from below the ribs and put a high E-flat into the roof of a hall and hold it for eleven seconds with her eyes on the back wall. She could not say a man's name to a wall.

"I'm sorry," she said, to the sign.

It was a poor thing, and she heard it. It was the sort of thing a girl says who has dropped a plate.

She stood there another minute. Then she turned, and went down to the river, and broke the ice with the heel of the bucket, and filled it, and carried it back.

She was halfway across the yard before she knew she was humming. It came up quite easily, out of her chest, into the thin air: three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed. She waited for it to be stopped.

Nobody stopped it.

She went on across the yard in the coachman's coat with the bucket slopping at her knee, humming, and the sound went out over the snow and the long roofs, and into the gap of the valley, and did not come back.
