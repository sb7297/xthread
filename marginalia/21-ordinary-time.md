# 21. ordinary time

*Rest.*

She slept for fourteen hours the first night, and eleven the second, and on the third day Weiss left a covered dish at the bunkhouse door, and hummed, and went away.

It was the porridge. It was better. She had learned that if you stirred it, it did not form a crust, and that the thing had a tendency, if left, to become a sort of grey cement. She stood in the yard with her hands in the pockets of the coachman's coat, and looked at the closed door, and went back to the hut and chopped forty-one logs.

On the afternoon of the third day the door opened.

Blake came out into the white with the dog at her knee. She stood on the step with her hand over her eyes, as a person does who has been a long time in the dark, and her ears went up and swung and settled. Weiss, at the woodpile, had stopped with the axe in the air.

"I'd like to do the water tomorrow," said Blake.

"Of course."

"I'll do it with you."

"You needn't—"

"I'd like to."

It was said quite mildly, from across the yard. Weiss lowered the axe. She stood with her hands on the haft, and the breath going up from her mouth in a plume, and found that she was nodding, and could not at once stop.

"Right," she said. "I'll revise the schedule."

She did it that night. She took a fresh sheet, ruled it, and wrote the names in two columns. Under *Blake,* in the first row, she wrote *Water,* and then looked at it, and then, because the square looked bare, put in brackets: *(with W.)* It was better. She left the rest of the column blank, as an offer.

In the morning she found that it had been filled in. Not in her hand. In a small upright pencil, with no corrections, down the whole of the sheet, in a clear pleasant diagonal: *Water. Wood. Traps. Stove (night). Radio (alt.).* She read it twice. It was a good schedule. It was fairer than hers.

She pinned it up in the office, where they would both see it, and did not alter a word.

* * *

*Wood.*

"You're fighting it."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The log." Blake was sitting on the chopping block with her elbows on her knees and the dog against her shin, watching. She had been watching for ten minutes. "You're hitting it as if it were an argument."

"It's a log."

"It knows."

Weiss set her feet again. She raised the axe, brought it down, and it went into the wood three inches off the line and stuck there, humming, and the log rolled off the block and lay on its side with a look of reproach.

Blake got up.

She did not say anything else. She took the axe, which Weiss gave her, and stood the log on its end, and looked at it for about a second. Then she lifted the head and let it fall, without effort, from the shoulder, on a line that might have been drawn there beforehand; and the two halves came apart with a sound like a book dropped on a table, and lay in the snow, white, wet, open to the heart.

"Let the weight do it," she said.

"That is not a technical instruction."

"It's the only one."

She held out the axe. It was an ordinary thing, an axe, thirty inches of ash and a pound or two of iron; and the handle was warm where she had held it. Weiss took it. She stood a log up. She raised the axe, and let it fall, and did not push.

It went through.

She looked at the two halves. They lay as the others had lain.

"Oh," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"It's quite easy."

"It's not. You've just stopped arguing."

They did it for an hour. They did not talk much. The sun came out low and hard over the shoulder of the hill and made the whole shelf of the camp glitter, every roof and pole and rail, and the split wood piled up between them in two white heaps, one on either side, going up. At some point Weiss became aware that she was humming, and Blake was not, but was moving her head very slightly in the time of it, and she let it go on.

* * *

*Fish.*

She had not known that Blake could fish. She had not known that anyone could.

It was done through a hole in the ice. Blake cut it with the axe at the bend of the river where the water ran dark and slow under a shelf, a neat round hole the width of a dinner plate, and sat down beside it on a folded sack with a line wound on a stick and a scrap of rind for bait, and was perfectly still.

She was still for forty minutes. Weiss, on the bank, with a book she was not reading, watched. She watched the line and the dark water and the hands, which did not move at all. She watched the face. It had the look of a cat in a window, half shut-eyed, fixed, absorbed; it was a look of enormous, patient, purposeless attention, in which there was no one at home at all except the part that was waiting.

Then the line jerked.

The hands moved. It took perhaps two seconds. Something silver came up through the hole in a spray of water and lay on the snow, thrashing, a foot long, with a dorsal fin like a sail; and the dog went mad.

"Grayling," said Blake.

"It's enormous."

"It's average."

She killed it with a thumb behind the head. She gutted it with a knife in four movements, on the sack, and flung the offal to the dog, who caught it in the air; and Weiss, who had watched all this with the expression of a woman at an operation, found that she was leaning forward.

They cooked it on the stove, flat, in a pan, with salt. It was tender. It fell off the bone in white flakes with a taste like cold water and smoke, and she ate it with her fingers because Blake did, with her eyes closing, and a small unplanned noise in her throat.

"That is good."

"Of course it's good," said Blake.

She said it perfectly flat. She said it without looking up, over a fishbone, in a voice exactly the colour of dry toast.

Weiss stopped chewing.

It was her own line. It was the very one. She had said it over a pot of tea in a dormitory on the second floor, with her chin raised and the Dust crystal ticking in its bowl, in another life; and it came back to her across the table, intact, with a small clean edge on it, the first thing in a year that Blake had said that was meant to be a joke.

She tried not to. She put her hand up. It did no good at all.

It came out through her nose. It was the ugly laugh, the one she hated, the snort, short and loud, and she clapped a hand over her mouth and her eyes went wide above it, and across the table Blake looked up, and looked at her, and for a second her whole face was open.

Then she looked down at the fish. She bent over it. Her shoulders did something.

"Don't," said Weiss, through her fingers.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was," said Blake, to the fish. "I was going to say it was average."

* * *

*Ursa.*

They came at dusk on the sixty-third day. Two of them, old Ursa, out of the north wood where the track went in, big as a cart, hunched, with the white bone plates grown out of their backs in a rack. The dog had given the warning. He stood at the edge of the yard with his hackles up, and Blake was out of the bunkhouse before the second bark with the long blade in her hand.

Weiss had the rapier. She did not remember taking it.

They did not speak. There was nothing to say. She went left, and Blake went right, and a glyph went down in the snow under the other's feet, a white one, a quick flat disc, and Blake's boot landed on it in the middle of a leap and was thrown twenty feet into the air over the nearer beast's head, turning, with the ribbon out. The Ursa swung at nothing. A shadow was standing where she had been. It took the paw. It came apart in black smoke.

The real one came down behind it. The blade went in at the nape.

Weiss had the second. She had it in a cage of ice before it knew; she was inside the cage, the point at the join of the plates, and she did not look round for a half second, and did not need to. She knew where Blake would be. It was the oddest thing. It was a feeling like having stepped onto a stair in the dark and found it where it ought to be.

They stood in the smoke of the bodies when it was done. It rose off the snow in black threads and went out. Neither of them said anything. Blake was breathing through her mouth. Her ears were up, forward, and her eyes in the last light were bright and gold and rather wild; and Weiss, with the rapier hanging at her side and her hair come down, thought: *We have not done that in a year.*

"Left," she said.

"What?"

"I went left. You went right. We didn't say."

"No."

"It's a good arrangement."

Blake's mouth did the thing at the corner. It lasted a second. "Adequate," she said, and walked away across the yard to wipe her blade, and left Weiss standing among the dissolving bones, with her hand at her own wrist, feeling for a pulse she could find perfectly well without.

* * *

*Thursday.*

Tove came with the fish and a sack of flour and the news that the river was going to freeze harder, and she stood in the doorway of the hut and looked at the two of them for some time without comment. Blake had risen. Her ears had gone back, a little, without her permission. The old woman noted it.

"Belladonna."

"Yes."

"The cat."

"Yes."

"Hm." Tove put the flour on the desk. She was, apparently, about to say a number of things and thought better of it, as one does with a great many things. "You eat?"

"Yes."

"Good. She cooks like a hammer." She jerked her head at Weiss. "Does she let you do anything?"

"She's learning," said Blake.

Weiss, at the stove, kept her back to the room. She was stirring something. She did not turn around, and her shoulders were extremely straight, and she began, quite unconsciously, as she stirred, to hum.

Tove stopped.

She had been taking off one glove. It hung from her fingers, half off. Her head had come up, and her ears, long and white and tipped with black, both swung round together toward the stove, and held there, rigid, like the ears of an animal that has heard its name.

"Do that again."

Weiss turned. "I'm sorry?"

"What you were doing."

"I was — humming. I'm sorry. It's a habit."

"Where'd you get it?"

"I don't—"

"Do it."

She did it. She stood with the spoon in her hand, in the middle of the floor, in front of two people and a dog, and hummed eight bars. Three notes. A rest. A phrase that climbed and was held on the top a little too long, and let fall.

The old woman's face did something. It was a very slow thing. It did not crumple. It simply, from the eyes outward, emptied, as a house empties when the family has gone out.

"That's a pit song," she said.

Nobody spoke. The kettle on the stove ticked.

"We sing it for the ones going down." Her voice had dropped, and was hoarse, and flat as a plank. "At the cage. And for babies. It's the same tune." She cleared her throat. She did not sing it, at first. She said it, in the voice of a woman reading a notice, in the rough short cadence of a shift-song. "*Lamp's gone low and the shift is done.*"

Blake had stopped breathing. Weiss could hear that she had.

"*The cage comes up to the morning sun. Tell the dark you're not back yet—*" The old woman's chin lifted. The pale eyes were fixed on something a long way beyond the wall of the hut, and the rest of it came out of her not as a song at all but as a sort of order, a thing said to a man at a door.

"*Come up, come up. The kettle's set.*"

The silence was complete.

On the stove, behind Weiss, the kettle began, softly, to sing.

"Who taught you that?" said Tove.

Weiss did not answer. She stood with the spoon in her hand. She had gone the colour of the snow, from the throat to the hairline, and the spoon had begun to tremble, very slightly, and she looked at it, and set it down on the stove.

Her right hand was at her side. The thumb was moving across the first two fingers, slowly, back and forth.

"A woman," she said. "Someone who looked after us. When we were small." Her voice was perfectly clear. "She — had a notch. In her ear. Here." She touched the rim of her own, high on the left, with a fingertip. "I used to put my finger in it. She let me."

Tove said nothing.

"She sang it when I couldn't sleep. And then she — went. I was seven." She stopped. "I don't remember her name."

"Mm."

"I don't remember her *name.*"

It was said in the same tone, level, and it went into the room and broke there.

The old woman looked at her for a long time. She did not say that it was all right. She did not say anything of the kind. She pulled the glove back on, finger by finger, and her ears lowered, slowly, and she said, in the dry cracked voice of a person giving directions:

"Could be anyone. Half the nurses in Atlas came out of Mantle." She looked at Weiss's hands. "You'd have to ask."

"Ask whom?"

"Whoever remembers."

"My sister," said Weiss.

"Then ask her."

She picked up her sack. She went out, as she did, without ceremony, and they heard her skis go off down the track in the cold, and the dog, which had been lying under the desk, got up and went out after her as far as the edge of the yard. He stood there. Then he came back.

* * *

*Evening.*

Weiss sat at the desk with her hands flat. The lamp was lit. Neither of them had said a word for an hour, and she had been at it the whole time, trying to put her hands in some order, and could not.

Across the table, Blake mended a sock.

She did it badly. She did it with the concentration of one who is not used to it and does not intend to be defeated. The wool was grey and the needle was blunt and her lips moved slightly as she counted. After a time she began, very quietly, under her breath, to hum.

Weiss's head came up.

It was the tune. It was the three notes, the rest, the phrase that climbed. It was a little flat in the middle. It was being hummed by a woman who did not know she was doing it, to a sock.

"That's mine," said Weiss.

Blake looked up. Her ears flicked. "What?"

"The song."

"I know."

"You — know?"

"I've been humming it for a year." She put the needle down. "I got it in the library. I didn't know what it was. My mother said it was a good tune."

"It isn't mine."

"No."

"It never was." It came out low. "It was hers. And theirs. I'd — thought I'd made it up." She looked at the lamp. "I've been humming it at the table all my life, and they were—" She stopped. "Someone in my father's house sang it to me, and then he sent her away, and I have been keeping it. Without knowing whose it was."

The lamp ticked.

"It can be both," said Blake.

"How?"

"It was given to you. That's still yours." She picked up the sock. She frowned at it. "That's how it works with — most things."

Weiss looked at her.

Blake did not look up. She had gone back to the sock with a ferocious air, and her ears, which were turned a little toward the desk, had gone from their ordinary pitch to something a few degrees further forward, and a few degrees pinker, at the tips, in the lamplight.

"We could find out," she said, to the wool. "Who she was."

"Could we?"

"People can be found."

"You found me."

"I followed a dog," said Blake.

Weiss opened her mouth. She shut it. She picked up the pen from the desk, and uncapped it, and drew the log toward her; and in the column marked *Remarks,* under the date, in her best hand, she wrote:

*Ordinary day.*

She looked at it. It was true. She did not know when she had last been able to say so.
