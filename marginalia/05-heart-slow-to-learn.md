# 5. heart slow to learn

> *Pity me that the heart is slow to learn*
> *What the swift mind beholds at every turn.*
>
> — Edna St. Vincent Millay

The radiator in their room had two settings: off, and the surface of the sun. Maintenance had been informed. Maintenance had said Thursday, and then the Thursday after.

They slept with the window open and the blankets kicked down. Ruby and Yang slept on top of their sheets in as little as decency allowed. Blake slept in her clothes, as she did, curled to the wall. Weiss slept in a long nightdress with the sleeves pushed to the elbow, and badly, and on the fourth night of it she woke at ten past two with a throat like paper.

She took the glass from her desk and filled it at the tap in the corner. She drank half. She turned around with the other half in her hand.

Blake was on the windowsill.

She sat with one knee up and her back to the room, looking out. The moon was low and big and the window held it like a frame. The ribbon was in her hand. She held it loosely, as one holds something one has picked up and has not yet decided about.

On her head, in the moonlight, were two ears.

They were black. They were covered in short fur, close and fine, with the pelt going to a pale lining where they faced the light. They stood a little taller than Weiss would have guessed. Her first thought was that they were a hat. Her second was *no*. She did not have a third. What she had was a kind of silence in the place where a third should go.

The left one turned.

It turned by itself, toward Weiss, the whole ear swivelling on its base while the head held still. The sound did it; her feet on the boards, her breath. Blake went rigid. The ear stayed pointed at the room.

Then she looked over her shoulder. Her eyes, in that light, were nearly white.

Weiss stepped back.

It was less than a step. It was the half of one. Her heel found the leg of the desk and the glass tipped in her hand and the water went over her wrist and down her nightdress and onto the floor. It made a loud sound. Neither of them looked at her. They both looked at the boards, where it spread and darkened and found the crack between two planks and ran along it.

"Oh," said Weiss.

"Yes," said Blake.

"I see."

"Yes."

She felt her face arrange itself. It was a thing she had been trained to do, and it did it without consulting her: the chin, the mouth, an expression of gracious composure she had worn at forty dinners. It came down over everything like a lid.

"That's quite all right," she said.

She heard it as it left her. It was already in the room.

"Thank you," said Blake.

Her voice was perfectly level. She lifted the ribbon and tied it in four movements, fast, in the dark, without a glass, with the practice of ten thousand mornings. The ears went flat under it, folded down, and the bow settled on top of them like a small lie. She slid off the sill and crossed to her bunk and lay down facing the wall.

She did not look at Weiss. She did not say goodnight.

Weiss stood where she was. After some time she took the towel from the hook, the one she'd used on her hair, and knelt and wiped the floor. She did it thoroughly. She wiped along the crack between the planks. She hung the towel up straight, and drank what was left in the glass, which tasted of nothing, and got into bed.

* * *

She lay on her back.

Her right hand was on the blanket. The thumb was moving across the first two fingers, slowly, back and forth, as if testing the weave of something. She noticed this and stopped it. After a while it started again.

She made a list. She composed it in the dark, in order, the way she composed all lists.

*One. Blake is a Faunus.*

*Two. This is not a fact about Blake's character. Blake is quick, and dry, and reads in the cold, and taped my wrist.*

*Three. The ears are ears.*

*Four. I stepped back.*

She went back to one and ran it again. It was a good list. It had no flaws in it. It arrived at four every time, and at four it stopped, and she kept putting her foot down for a fifth.

Across the room the lamp was off. It was the first night it had been. In the dark, beyond the wardrobe, she could hear the particular quality of someone not sleeping.

* * *

In the morning she was perfect.

She said Blake's name twelve times before lunch. She held the door. At the dining hall she stood aside and said "After you," which she had not said to anyone since the first week of term, and which, when it came out of her, sounded like a thing said by a footman. She asked if Blake would like the window seat. Blake said, "No, thank you." She asked if Blake would like the other window seat. Blake said, "No, thank you," in precisely the same tone, and it went by like a train that does not stop at your station.

"What did you do?" said Yang.

They were at the end of the table. Blake had gone to refill her tea. Ruby was in the middle of a story about a boarhound and a mailbox.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're being nice."

"I am frequently nice."

"You're being nice like a nurse." Yang waved her fork in a small circle. "Like somebody's got a thing, and you don't want to say."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Uh-huh," said Yang, and put the fork in her mouth, and looked at her for a long time.

* * *

It happened again in the afternoon.

It was Professor Port's lecture, and Port was explaining the habits of the Ursa to a room that had stopped listening an hour ago. Weiss was taking notes. Blake was three seats down, and in the corridor outside a door slammed, and Blake's head turned toward it.

The bow moved.

It moved by itself, a short sharp tilt, the way a flag does in a gust. Under it the ears had swung toward the sound, and for a half second the ribbon rode up on one side, and the shape of what lay beneath it showed through, plain as a hand under a cloth.

Weiss's shoulder jerked.

It was nothing. It was an inch. She turned it into a reach for her pen, and picked up the pen, and the pen shook.

Blake's eyes came around from the door. They went to the shoulder, and from the shoulder to the pen, and then they went back to the front of the room and stayed there. She wrote something in her notebook. She wrote it very evenly.

After the bell she was gone before Weiss had closed her bag.

* * *

In the washroom, at four, with no one there, Weiss stood at the mirror and said it.

"It doesn't matter to me."

That was wrong. It sounded like a hand being waved.

"I don't mind."

Worse. It supposed there was something to mind.

"It's entirely your business."

A form letter. She could have been declining an invitation.

"I'm sorry."

She looked at it for some time. She could not make the sentence say what for. She would be apologising for a thing she had done with her ankle. She would be saying, to a person she had hurt, *I know that you were hurt,* and the person would say, correctly, *then why did you.*

She did not know why. She knew, perfectly well, the reasons it was wrong. She could have given a lecture. She could have given two, with footnotes. But somewhere below the place where the lecture was kept there was a part of her that had learned it differently, and earlier, and from other teachers, and it had not been told.

She ran the tap. She put her hands under it, and held them there, and the water was cold, and she watched it go over her wrists until her fingers stopped feeling like something they were trying to hold.

In the mirror the scar showed over her left eye. She looked at it as she always did, without recognising anything.

* * *

Back at her desk she took out her notebook and found the list. She had written it out fair, after breakfast, in ink. It had four lines.

Under the fourth she wrote a fifth.

*Practise.*

She pressed so hard that it came through to the page beneath.
