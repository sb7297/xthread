# 3. form

Goodwitch drew the pairs from a glass bowl, which Weiss considered a poor way to assess anything.

"Rose and Arc," said Goodwitch, and the room made a sympathetic noise. "Nikos and Winchester." A different noise. "Xiao Long and Valentin." Yang said "Yes," quietly, like a woman receiving good news at a bank. "Schnee."

Weiss sat up.

"Belladonna."

Across the amphitheatre, in the third row, Blake closed her book on her finger and looked at the ceiling, as if checking it for weather.

Weiss had thought about this. She would not have said so, but she had. She had watched Blake in the Emerald Forest and in the corridors and at the long table in the dining hall, where she ate with her back to the wall and her eyes on the doors. She had watched a good deal. She had arrived at no conclusion except that Blake was quick, and that she did not seem to mind being watched, which was interesting, since she so clearly minded everything else.

The glove was in her coat pocket. It had been there two days. She had picked it up from the library table with the intention of returning it, and then there had been a day, and then another. It was a very small glove. It was absurd to be thinking of it now.

She went down the steps to the floor.

* * *

"Begin," said Goodwitch.

Weiss did not wait. She never did. Three glyphs went down in a line and she was through the first with the rapier already extended, and the point went where Blake was and Blake was not.

There was no sound when the shadow came apart. That was the first thing. She had cut through an after-image, a smear of black air, and it went out like a lamp, and the real Blake was already to the left with the long blade drawn back. Weiss turned the strike on the guard. The ring of it went up through her arm and into the stands.

Again. Another thrust, another shadow. It fell into pieces and was not there.

Blake did not talk when she fought. She did not do anything for show. She came in low and sidelong and withdrew before the answer could arrive; she used the ribbon to take her own weight, swinging, and once she took a length of the front railing with it, by accident, and said "Sorry" to no one. Weiss drove her back across the floor with a line of ice, and Blake ran along it, and the ice shattered where she had been.

She was very good. This was an annoying thing to discover in someone who had said six words at dinner.

Weiss cut through a third shadow. It came apart in the same silence.

She stopped.

She was breathing hard. In the stands, someone was calling for her. The lights were bright and hot and there were forty people shouting, and every one of them was a sound she knew, because she had grown up with sound, she had been taught it as a child the way other children were taught to read. She stood still with the point of the rapier low and she did what she would have done on a stage, in the wings, the moment before the entrance: she stopped watching, and let the room go past her, and listened to a single instrument.

Shoes on the floor. Not those. The crowd. Not those. A pulse in her own ears, and under it, to the left and behind, a breath being taken through the mouth.

She turned and put the point at Blake's throat.

Blake's blade was against her ribs. They had both stopped in the same instant. Weiss could feel the flat of it through her bodice, cold.

"Draw," said Goodwitch. "Both of you. Very good."

Neither of them moved.

"You heard me," said Blake.

"You breathe through your mouth," said Weiss, "just before you go."

"Do I."

"Yes."

Blake lowered the blade. Her eyes had gone to Weiss's, and stayed. They were gold, a very pale gold in the lights. Something moved at the top of her head. The bow shifted, once, as if there were a draft.

Weiss glanced at the air. The air in the arena was perfectly still.

* * *

In the locker room, afterward, she sat on the bench and looked at her wrist.

It had taken the guard badly. It was not broken; she knew what broken was. She was trying to roll the tape from the first-aid kit one-handed and it was going wrong in a way that she could see in advance and could not stop.

"I'll do it," she said.

Blake had been standing by the lockers, unwinding her ribbon. She did not say anything. She sat on the other end of the bench and waited, with her hands on her knees.

"I can manage."

Blake waited.

Weiss held out the arm.

She had good hands, Blake. They were quick, and they did not hurry. She turned the wrist over once in her palm, to see how it moved, and then began to wind the tape: twice around, and across, and around. Weiss looked at the top of her head. The bow was perfectly square.

"You hold your breath," she said, "while you do that."

"Do I?"

"Yes."

Blake let it out. She did not look up. At the side of her head, under the hair, something moved and was still.

Weiss looked away at the wall.

"Does this hurt?" said Blake.

"Yes."

"Good."

"That is not how you reassure someone."

"It's how you tape a wrist." She tore the end with her teeth. "If it didn't hurt it'd be loose."

She smoothed the last edge flat with her thumb. It took one second. Weiss became aware that she had been holding her own breath, and that she was a person who had just criticised another person for it.

Blake stood and picked up her bag. At the door she stopped, and her free hand went to her coat pocket and then to the other, and then she looked at her bare right hand for some time, and put it away.

Weiss's own hand was in her coat. It was closed around something wool.

"I—" she said.

Blake turned her head. "Yes?"

It would have taken four words.

"Nothing," said Weiss.

Blake nodded and went out. The door swung and settled. Weiss sat in the empty room with the glove in her fist, and told herself that she had forgotten, which was a lie, and so small a lie that she did not feel the need to correct it.

* * *

Blake was in the corridor, ten feet along, when she called after her.

"Again."

Blake stopped. "What?"

"Tomorrow. Six. The north court."

"Why six?"

"I'm up at six."

"I know," said Blake. "You fold the blanket in thirds."

Weiss opened her mouth. Thirds were correct. There was no other way to fold a blanket. There was no reason on earth a person should have noticed it from across a dark room, unless the person was lying awake in it, listening.

"Yes," she said. "Well."

"Okay," said Blake.

"Okay?"

"Six."

She went on down the corridor. She did not look back. Her bow was perfectly straight and her hands, in the pockets of her coat, were — Weiss assumed — cold.
