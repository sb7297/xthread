# 10. evacuation

Afterward she could not put the night in order. It came back in pieces, and the pieces did not touch.

The sky was the wrong colour. It was red where it should have been black, and the light at the top of the tower had gone out.

Heat on her face. The smell of burnt hair, and under it something sweeter. A roar from the north like a train in a tunnel, that did not get any nearer and did not stop.

Yang's voice: *Left! Blake, left!* She went left.

The courtyard, with the statue in three pieces. A boy sitting against the plinth with his hands over his ears, not hurt, only sitting.

Then she saw the mask.

It was on the wall above the east steps. White, with the red lines, and under it a man leaning on a sword the way a man leans on a gatepost. He was not looking at the fire. He was not looking at anything that was happening. He was looking at her, and he had been looking for some time, with the patience of someone who had never once had to hurry.

He said her name.

He said it in the voice he had used when they were alone. She heard it under the roar, over it, as if there had been no other sound in the city. Her ears went flat. There was a sharp small pull at the back of her head, and she never saw the blade move, and the ribbon was gone. It fell in the ash. She did not look at it.

Yang stepped in front of her.

Yang had always stepped in front. She had a habit of it; it was the first thing Blake had learned about her, in the first week, and it had been so reliable that she had stopped noticing it, as one stops noticing a floor. Yang stepped in, with her fists up, grinning at him, and there was a sound that Blake had not heard before. Then she heard it again, and went on hearing it.

Yang sat down.

She sat on the flagstones, quite slowly, with a look of great surprise. Her left sleeve was a different colour than it had been.

The arithmetic was very quick. She had learned it in cellars. He had come for her. He would have her, and then he would have anyone who stood nearest, one after another, until there was no one nearer; and she knew how that sum ended, because she had seen the working.

But he would follow. That was the other half. He always followed. If she went, he would come after her, and he would leave the rest.

She thought of the sheet by the door, taped to the wall at the height of someone's eyes. *12. No leaving without notice.* The ink was very black. She thought, *This is an emergency.* She thought, *That was struck out.*

She put what she could into the arch. It was little. It was a shadow of her, upright, with the blade drawn, wearing her face, standing in the way between Yang and the dark.

Then she ran.

She did not look back. Behind her, Yang's voice was saying her name. It said it three times, hoarse, close to laughing. The shadow, in the arch, held the light for the length of a breath, and went out.

Then Yang said it once more, in a different voice.

* * *

The field was west of the school, beyond the lower gate, and by morning there were four thousand people in it.

There were blankets. There were the kind of tents that go up in an hour and come down in less. There was a very long line for water. The smoke lay over the whole of it, grey, flat, and low, and under the smoke a sound that went on in the same register all day: a murmur, with no words in it, like the noise in a seashell.

Weiss sat on an upturned crate with a blanket on her shoulders. She did not know who had put it there.

She had a cut on her scalp which someone had cleaned and she had not asked to be cleaned. Her hands were black to the wrist. She had counted her fingers, early on, in the way one does, and had found them present. Myrtenaster lay across her knees. She had wiped it down. She had wiped it three times.

Ruby was on the next crate along. She had not said anything in two hours. She was holding Yang's other hand, the right, which lay on a stretcher beside her. Yang's left side was wrapped in somebody's coat to the shoulder, and the coat was a colour it had not been, and she was awake, and looking up at the smoke.

"Where's Blake?" said Weiss.

She had said it before. She said it the way she said all things, clearly, in the voice of a person reading from a card.

Yang's eyes came down. They stayed on her.

"She ran."

"She — what?"

"It was a shadow." Yang's voice was dry. It cracked at the end and she let it. "In the arch. I thought it was her. I was yelling at it. It just — went."

"Went where?"

"I don't know."

"You don't *know*—"

"Weiss," said Ruby, quietly.

Weiss stopped. She looked at her own hands. They were very black. She wiped the left one on the blanket and it made no difference.

"I need to go and look at the dormitory," she said.

* * *

The east wing had gone down at the middle.

The roof on that side was a shape; the other side had a wall. She went up the stair that was left, over a floor that gave under her boots with a noise like an animal settling. There was a door, and a frame, and no room. There were the beams of the next floor, laid across what had been theirs. She had to go in under them on her hands.

It was very quiet in there. The grey light came in from above, through a hole.

The wall beside the door was standing.

It was the one piece. There was a stretch of plaster, with a hook on it, and a strip of dusty tape, and on the tape, held up by four corners, a sheet of cream paper. A little singed. A little curled. Eleven rules, a twelve, a zero, a half.

She stood for some time and read it. She read it twice.

Then she peeled it off. The tape pulled a skin of paint from the wall. She folded the sheet in three, once, then once more, along the old creases, and put it in her coat.

The beams had come down on Blake's side. The bunk was under them, flat. She dug. She did it carefully, one board at a time, with her ruined gloves, and every board she lifted she thought *I am not looking for anything,* and found it was a lie. Under the fifth she saw a corner of green.

It was dusty. The cloth spine had split. She turned it over in her hands. The pages were warped from the damp, but they were all there. It fell open by itself at page 112, and a piece of paper folded in four slid out onto her wrist: pencil, three names, and a fourth in ink.

She looked at it for a time. She put it back, at the place it had come from, and flattened the fold with her thumbnail.

She closed the book. She held it against her chest.

* * *

They came for her at noon.

The airship came down in the next field, on its great quiet lamps, and she saw the white sign on the side of it from a long way off. A snowflake in a ring. A line of men crossed the grass in grey coats. And at the head of them, with her back as straight as a rule and her face as white as a plate, was a woman in the uniform of the Specialists, with her hair in a tight knot.

"Weiss."

"Winter."

Her sister stopped a yard away. She did not hold out her arms. She looked her over once from the cut on the scalp to the boots, in the way she looked at a weapon that had been returned from the field, and something in her jaw worked and was still.

"Father has sent for you."

"I can't. My team—"

"Your team has been dispersed. General Ironwood has the evacuation. Weiss." She said it again, lower. "It's an order from the Council, and it is also not."

Weiss looked past her at the field. At the crates, the stretcher, the dark head bent over it. Ruby was looking at her. Her face was small and white and very serious. She nodded once, as someone nods at a train.

"I'll write," said Weiss.

"Okay," said Ruby.

She went with her sister across the grass. The men in grey made a corridor. At the foot of the ramp she stopped, because she had remembered, and turned, and looked at the field, the smoke, the four thousand people, the blankets, the long grey ridge of the school against the sky.

She had a rule about this.

She stood and looked for someone to give notice to. There was no one it would reach.

"I'm going," she said.

She said it aloud, to the field, for the record. Nobody heard. She went up the ramp with the book held to her chest in both arms, as one carries something that has been handed over, and the doors closed behind her, and the lamps came up.
