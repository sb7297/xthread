# 1. house rules

The rules went up on the first night.

Weiss had drafted them on the airship, in pencil, on the back of her enrollment packet. She copied them out fair at the desk while the others argued about the beds. Eleven rules, black ink, cream paper, a ruler for the margins. She taped the sheet to the wall beside the door at the height of her own eyes. It was low for Yang, who read it bent at the waist, like someone inspecting a plant.

"'Lights out at ten,'" Yang read. "'Dust is stored in the locker. Not under the bed, not in a drawer, not near the radiator.' Who puts Dust near a radiator?"

"No one," said Weiss.

Ruby became interested in the ceiling.

"'Shower times are posted. They are not suggestions.'" Yang ran a finger down the grid beneath it. Four names, four colours of ink. "You gave yourself second-best."

"I gave everyone an hour that suited them."

"Second-best, though."

"It was close," said Weiss.

She had spent some time on it. She did not say so.

Ruby read the whole sheet the way she did everything, with her entire face. When she reached the bottom she looked at Weiss as if she were about to ask something, and Weiss said, "No," and Ruby said, "Okay," and went back to her suitcase. A minute later there was a small sound of crayon.

Weiss did not turn around. She was putting her shoes in the wardrobe, heels to the back wall, and it was a job that required attention.

When she had finished there was a line at the foot of the sheet in red wax. *11½. Cookies.* And above the title, in marker, in a hand that leaned as if it were running somewhere, a zero.

"What is that?"

"Rule zero," said Yang. "Overrides all other rules."

"That isn't how rules work."

"That's exactly how rules work."

Weiss looked at the crayon. She looked at the marker. Then she went and sat on her bed with her hands folded and did not remove either one.

* * *

The room was smaller than her room at home and had four people in it. Four beds, four desks, four lockers, one window. She had not slept in a room with another person since she was seven. She had thought about this on the airship, briefly, and had stopped.

Ruby had the bunk above hers. Yang had the one across the room, and below Yang, with her back against the wall, sat Blake.

Her boots were under the bed, toes out, laces loose. It was how you left boots if you might want them quickly. Weiss's were in the wardrobe, heels to the wall. She noticed the difference and put it somewhere.

Blake had a paperback with a cracked spine and she held it close, in both hands, and turned the pages at long, even intervals. She had said six words since dinner. Three were *pass the salt.*

She wore a black ribbon in her hair, tied in a bow. She had worn it at dinner. She was wearing it now.

At five to ten Weiss stood up and said, "Lights out in five minutes." Nobody argued, which she found suspicious. Yang climbed into her bunk with a groan that went on for some time. Ruby brushed her teeth for what felt like the length of a short opera.

At ten, Weiss turned off the overhead.

Blake's lamp stayed on. It was a small clip lamp, black, fixed to the shelf above her pillow. She had not moved. A page turned.

"It's ten," said Weiss.

"I'm aware."

"Rule one."

"Rule eleven." Blake did not look up.

Weiss looked at the sheet, which she could not read in the dark and did not need to. *Any rule may be waived by unanimous consent of the occupants, in writing.*

"I wrote that so that no one would have to—"

She stopped. She had written it because the rules at home had no such clause. She did not say that.

Blake had already torn a page from the back of her book. Weiss watched her do it, one small tidy rip against the spine. She took a pencil stub from her shirt pocket and wrote for half a minute, and then held the page out toward Yang's bunk without being asked. An arm came down from above. The page went up. There was a pause, and a snort, and a scrawl, and it came back down and across the room to Ruby, who signed with a flourish that took two lines.

It arrived at Weiss's bed. Blake did not look at her while it did.

*The undersigned consent to the waiver of Rule 1 as it applies to one (1) lamp. Low.*

Three names. B. Belladonna's was the neatest.

Weiss read it twice. She always read things twice.

"It says low."

"It's a low lamp."

"It doesn't specify wattage."

"It's a low lamp," said Blake, and for the first time Weiss looked at her face, and her face was entirely serious.

Weiss took the pen from her desk. It was the one she had used for the rules and it was in her hand before she had decided to reach for it. She signed under Ruby. *W. Schnee.* Then she held the page out across the dark, at arm's length, and a hand took it. A quick hand. It did not touch hers.

Blake folded the waiver once and slid it into the book to keep her place, and went on reading.

* * *

Weiss lay on her back with her eyes open.

The lamp made a small yellow room at the far end of the dark. Above her, Ruby breathed in a way that suggested she was being chased in a dream, mildly. Yang snored like a low engine. Weiss had expected to mind all of it, and had spent the hour since ten waiting to mind.

Across the room Blake read, and then, at some point, did not. Weiss could tell by the pages. They had been turning once a minute and a half, which was reading. Then they turned every forty seconds, then every thirty, evenly, the way a person turns pages when she wants something to do with her hands while she listens to a room.

Weiss lay still. She knew that pace. It was how she herself had sat through dinners.

She slowed her breathing, in through the nose, the way you do when you want to be taken for asleep. After a while the pages stopped.

She turned her head. Blake lay on her side with her knees drawn up, facing the door. The book was open on the blanket. The ribbon was in her hair.

It had been there at dinner. It had been there at the washbasin. People took ribbons off to sleep; there was the knot to think of, and the pins, and the pillow. Blake's was tied exactly as it had been at noon.

It was not Weiss's business. She noted it, as she had noted the boots, and set it aside to be thought about later, or never.

She closed her eyes. She slept with the lamp on. It was easier than she had expected.

* * *

She woke at six, as she did, before the others. The window was grey. One of Ruby's arms hung over the edge of the bunk above her, palm up. Across the room the lamp was off and the book lay shut on the shelf, and Blake was asleep, or a good imitation of it, curled to the wall with her back to the room.

Weiss crossed to the door. The sheet was where she had left it: eleven rules, a zero, a half. She took the pen from the desk and uncapped it, and under Rule 1, in the strip of margin she had left for nothing, she wrote:

*(Excepting one lamp, low. See waiver.)*

She capped the pen.

She had done it in ink.

# 2. stacks

Blake chose the carrel because it had a wall at its back and a clear line to both doors.

The library at night was a different building. The students who came after curfew were the kind who did not want to be asked what they were doing, and there were few of them: a boy asleep on his arms in Periodicals, two second-years whispering over a diagram, a woman in a cardigan going down the aisles with a cart. The librarian at the desk was a heavy man who ate oranges. He peeled each one in a single spiral and laid the peel on a saucer, and when he had finished he laid the saucer on a shelf behind him, where there were already nine. He had not looked at Blake once. She liked him a great deal.

She came because the dorm had three heartbeats in it.

She could hear them all, each at its own pace, Ruby's quick and Yang's slow and Schnee's, in the bunk across the room, so regular that it sounded like it was being kept on purpose. She could hear the sheets. She could hear someone's stomach, three nights running. It was not a thing she could explain to anyone, and she had been in the room for eleven days.

In the library there was only the building. Pipes, the high note of the lights, the librarian's thumbnail going into rind.

She wore her gloves. They were black wool, cut off at the first knuckle, and the stacks were cold. She wore the bow, too. After an hour the bow began to ache at the hinge of each ear, a dull pinch that moved up toward the temple, and she would press her thumbs there through the ribbon and wait for it to pass. It did not pass. It only got used to being ignored.

Around midnight on the third night, when the woman with the cart had gone through to the far wing, Blake took her book into the last stack, where the shelves ran out against the wall and the poetry was, and loosened the knot and let them stand. Twenty minutes. Back against the shelf. The air was cold on them and it was good.

She heard the door before she heard the feet. She had the bow tied before the feet were halfway down the aisle.

It was only Schnee.

* * *

Schnee came in at eleven, most nights. She carried an armload of folders and a thermos and put them down on the table by the east window, the one with the good lamp, and then she sat and took off her shoes.

She did it under the table. Blake knew because of the small sound the leather made on the floor. After that Schnee crossed her left foot over her right and opened the first folder and put a pencil in her mouth, and for the next three hours she was a person doing arithmetic.

The folders had a letterhead. Blake could read it from the carrel, because she could read most things from most places. A snowflake in a ring.

She had seen it stamped on crates. She had seen it on the sides of freight cars, on tarpaulins, once on a hand towel in a shack in the lower district where nobody had been able to explain to her how it got there. She knew it the way you know a smell. She looked away from it at once, at the page in front of her, and turned the page. It was the page she had been reading.

She sat with her gloved hands flat on the table until they were warm again.

Then she watched, because it was what she did. She was not proud of it. She watched in the same manner she checked doors.

Schnee mouthed numbers when she added. She sighed through her nose at footnotes. She underlined with the ruler. She had a way of pressing her thumbnail into the pad of her finger when something didn't balance, and she did it now, and now. At one in the morning she put her head on her fist; at a quarter past she lifted it again and began, quietly, to hum.

It was nothing much. Three notes, and a rest, and a phrase that climbed and came back. Eight bars. Then three notes. Schnee did not know she was doing it. Her face did not change.

Blake's ears, under the ribbon, turned toward the sound.

She felt them go. It was a small tightening at the root, as if a hand had tipped her head. She pressed her palm flat on top of the bow and held it there.

It was not the first time they had gone somewhere without asking. It was the first time they had gone toward a Schnee.

* * *

On the fifth night Schnee fell asleep at her table.

It was past two. The librarian was gone, the saucers left in their rows. Blake had given up on her page an hour before and was listening instead. At some point the pencil had stopped, and then the humming, and then there was only the breath, slower, with a catch at the top of each one, like a drawer that wasn't quite shut.

The lamp was an old brass thing on a hinged arm. It had been angled at the page. It had slipped, a little at a time, as they do, until it pointed directly at Weiss's face.

Blake sat for a while.

Then she got up and crossed the floor. She was good at floors. She knew which boards in the east wing would speak and which would stay quiet, and she took the quiet ones, and stopped beside the table with her heart going at its ordinary rate.

Weiss's mouth was a little open. Her hair had come out of its tie on one side. There was a mark on her cheek where the edge of a folder had pressed. The scar over her left eye looked paler in this light than it did in the day, and that was an odd thing to know.

Blake put one finger on the lamp's neck and turned it, two inches, down. Away from the eyes. Onto the paper.

The top folder lay open. She did not mean to read it. The first line was a heading in capitals — *Q3 HOUSING AND WAGE DEDUCTIONS, MANTLE OPERATIONS* — and her eye took that much before she shut it, by effort, the way you shut a hand.

She went back to her carrel by the quiet boards. She did not look at the table again. She listened to the breath until it was time to go, and went out before it woke.

* * *

"You moved the lamp."

It was the next night, a little after eleven. Blake was in the doorway of the reading room with her book against her chest and she stopped there, because there was nothing else to do.

Weiss was at her table with the shoes already off. She had not looked up.

"What lamp?"

"The one on my table. When I sat down last night, it was pointed at the window. When I woke it was pointed at the page."

"The cleaners."

"At three in the morning."

"They're very dedicated."

The pencil kept moving. After a moment Weiss said, "Thank you. Whoever it was."

Blake stood in the door. The building hummed. At the desk, the librarian split an orange with his thumbs, and the smell crossed the room, sharp and bright.

"The light's better over here," said Weiss, to the folder. "If you're going to loiter."

"I'm not loitering. I'm reading."

"From over there. By one bulb."

"It's low," said Blake.

The pencil stopped.

"Yes," said Weiss. "I recall."

Blake crossed the floor. She took the table opposite, two chairs down from the end, so that there was a whole chair between them and the window at her shoulder. The lamp here was better. It was on a hinge. She did not touch it.

She opened the book where the waiver was. She read the same sentence four times.

Across the table, a pencil began again, and a page, and a long time later the humming, very quietly, the three notes and the rest and the phrase that climbed. Blake kept her ears where they were by main force. It felt like holding a door shut against weather.

Around four the librarian turned off the lights in the east wing, one bank at a time. They went on reading by the two lamps until he came and stood in the doorway and said, "Ladies," and nothing else.

She was in the corridor, buttoning her coat, before she found she had one glove.

She stood and looked at her hand for some time. Then she kept walking.

# 3. form

Goodwitch drew the pairs from a glass bowl, which Weiss considered a poor way to assess anything.

"Rose and Arc," said Goodwitch, and the room made a sympathetic noise. "Nikos and Winchester." A different noise. "Xiao Long and Valentin." Yang said "Yes," quietly, like a woman receiving good news at a bank. "Schnee."

Weiss sat up.

"Belladonna."

Across the amphitheatre, in the third row, Blake closed her book on her finger and looked at the ceiling, as if checking it for weather.

Weiss had thought about this. She would not have said so, but she had. She had watched Blake in the Emerald Forest and in the corridors and at the long table in the dining hall, where she ate with her back to the wall and her eyes on the doors. She had watched a good deal. She had arrived at no conclusion except that Blake was quick, and that she did not seem to mind being watched, which was interesting, since she so clearly minded everything else.

The glove was in her coat pocket. It had been there two days. She had picked it up from the library table with the intention of returning it, and then there had been a day, and then another. It was a small glove. It was absurd to be thinking of it now.

She went down the steps to the floor.

* * *

"Begin," said Goodwitch.

Weiss did not wait. She never did. Three glyphs went down in a line and she was through the first with the rapier already extended, and the point went where Blake was and Blake was not.

There was no sound when the shadow came apart. That was the first thing. She had cut through an after-image, a smear of black air, and it went out like a lamp, and the real Blake was already to the left with the long blade drawn back. Weiss turned the strike on the guard. The ring of it went up through her arm and into the stands.

Again. Another thrust, another shadow. It fell into pieces and was not there.

Blake did not talk when she fought. She did not do anything for show. She came in low and sidelong and withdrew before the answer could arrive; she used the ribbon to take her own weight, swinging, and once she took a length of the front railing with it, by accident, and said "Sorry" to no one. Weiss drove her back across the floor with a line of ice, and Blake ran along it, and the ice shattered where she had been.

She was good. This was an annoying thing to discover in someone who had said six words at dinner.

Weiss cut through a third shadow. It came apart in the same silence.

She stopped.

She was breathing hard. In the stands, someone was calling for her. The lights were bright and hot and there were forty people shouting, and every one of them was a sound she knew, because she had grown up with sound, she had been taught it as a child the way other children were taught to read. She stood still with the point of the rapier low and she did what she would have done on a stage, in the wings, the moment before the entrance: she stopped watching, and let the room go past her, and listened to a single instrument.

Shoes on the floor. Not those. The crowd. Not those. A pulse in her own ears, and under it, to the left and behind, a breath being taken through the mouth.

She turned and put the point at Blake's throat.

Blake's blade was against her ribs. They had both stopped in the same instant. Weiss could feel the flat of it through her bodice, cold.

"Draw," said Goodwitch. "Both of you. Very good."

Neither of them moved.

"You heard me," said Blake.

"You breathe through your mouth," said Weiss, "just before you go."

"Do I."

"Yes."

Blake lowered the blade. Her eyes had gone to Weiss's, and stayed. They were gold, a pale gold in the lights. Something moved at the top of her head. The bow shifted, once, as if there were a draft.

Weiss glanced at the air. The air in the arena was perfectly still.

* * *

In the locker room, afterward, she sat on the bench and looked at her wrist.

It had taken the guard badly. It was not broken; she knew what broken was. She was trying to roll the tape from the first-aid kit one-handed and it was going wrong in a way that she could see in advance and could not stop.

"I'll do it," she said.

Blake had been standing by the lockers, unwinding her ribbon. She did not say anything. She sat on the other end of the bench and waited, with her hands on her knees.

"I can manage."

Blake waited.

Weiss held out the arm.

She had good hands, Blake. They were quick, and they did not hurry. She turned the wrist over once in her palm, to see how it moved, and then began to wind the tape: twice around, and across, and around. Weiss looked at the top of her head. The bow was perfectly square.

"You hold your breath," she said, "while you do that."

"Do I?"

"Yes."

Blake let it out. She did not look up. At the side of her head, under the hair, something moved and was still.

Weiss looked away at the wall.

"Does this hurt?" said Blake.

"Yes."

"Good."

"That is not how you reassure someone."

"It's how you tape a wrist." She tore the end with her teeth. "If it didn't hurt it'd be loose."

She smoothed the last edge flat with her thumb. It took one second. Weiss became aware that she had been holding her own breath, and that she was a person who had just criticised another person for it.

Blake stood and picked up her bag. At the door she stopped, and her free hand went to her coat pocket and then to the other, and then she looked at her bare left hand for some time, and put it away.

Weiss's own hand was in her coat. It was closed around something wool.

"I—" she said.

Blake turned her head. "Yes?"

It would have taken four words.

"Nothing," said Weiss.

Blake nodded and went out. The door swung and settled. Weiss sat in the empty room with the glove in her fist, and told herself that she had forgotten, which was a lie, and so small a lie that she did not feel the need to correct it.

* * *

Blake was in the corridor, ten feet along, when she called after her.

"Again."

Blake stopped. "What?"

"Tomorrow. Six. The north court."

"Why six?"

"I'm up at six."

"I know," said Blake. "You fold the blanket in thirds."

Weiss opened her mouth. Thirds were correct. There was no other way to fold a blanket. There was no reason on earth a person should have noticed it from across a dark room, unless the person was lying awake in it, listening.

"Yes," she said. "Well."

"Okay," said Blake.

"Okay?"

"Six."

She went on down the corridor. She did not look back. Her bow was perfectly straight and her hands, in the pockets of her coat, were — Weiss assumed — cold.

# 4. a reasonable position

The screen above the serving line was supposed to show the schedule for the week's matches. Someone had changed it to the news.

Blake saw it a half second after the others, because she was cutting her fish. A train on a siding. Steam, or smoke, and a reporter with a microphone held tight to her chin, and behind her the long boxcars with their doors torn off. The picture was good. She could see the stencil on the nearest car. A snowflake in a ring.

"—the fourth such attack on a Schnee Dust Company freight this quarter. A company spokesman said losses were—"

"Huh," said Yang.

"Do they say who?" said Ruby.

Weiss didn't turn around. She was sitting straight with her back to the screen, and her tea in front of her, and she had not stopped eating; she cut a piece of chicken and put it in her mouth and chewed it, and it was clear from the sound of her jaw that she had heard every word.

"It's the White Fang."

"They didn't say that," said Ruby.

"They never say. It's always the White Fang."

"Always?" said Yang.

"Four trains. The same method. The same cut on the couplings. Does anyone at this table honestly think it's pirates?"

"I mean," said Ruby, "it could—"

"It isn't."

Blake finished cutting the fish. She laid the knife across the edge of the plate. It was quiet, the knife, on the china.

"I'd like to say something before somebody says it for me," said Weiss. She put her fork down. "I am not saying anything against Faunus. My family employs more than four thousand of them. I know a great many. Some of them looked after—" She stopped. She went around it, in a small neat arc, the way you go around a puddle. "I know them. They are perfectly decent people. What I'm saying is that an organisation that sets a charge on a train with guards inside it is not a political movement. It is a gang with a slogan."

"There were guards on it?" said Yang.

"Two in the second car. One of them is still in hospital."

Nobody said anything. Over by the windows a first-year dropped a tray and the whole room clapped, the way a room does.

"Maybe they've got reasons," said Yang. She said it lightly, looking at her bread.

"Everyone has reasons," said Weiss. "Having a reason isn't the same as being right. It's a perfectly reasonable position."

Blake had put both hands in her lap. She noticed it, in the way you notice something you are doing at the far end of the table. Under the ribbon her ears were lying flat to her skull. They did it without being told, as they had always done, and they ached at the root with the effort of it, and in them she could hear her own pulse, slow and loud, like a clock on the other side of a wall.

She knew the number. Four thousand two hundred and twelve, as of the spring report. She could have given the average weekly wage, and the housing deduction, and what was left. She had once read it aloud to forty people in a cellar in Kuo Kuana, and they had listened to the last figure in silence, and then a man in the back had laughed and kept laughing for some time.

She could say it now. All of it. It would be easy to say, and it would be correct, and it would cost her the room.

"May I be excused," she said.

Weiss looked at her for the first time. "You haven't finished."

"I'm not hungry."

She stood, and picked up her tray. She walked at an even pace to the return, and set the tray in the slot, and did not run.

* * *

The woman at the dish window had small, round ears, set high, dark with a fringe of pale fur. They stuck out from her hairnet like two buttons. She took the tray and looked at the fish, and then at the ribbon, and then at the fish.

"Not hungry?"

"No."

"It's good fish."

"I know."

The woman said nothing else. She wrapped the fillet in a square of waxed paper, folded the ends as neatly as a letter, and slid it across the steel. Blake took it. It was warm in her hand, like something alive.

"Thank you."

"Mm," said the woman, and was already turning to the next tray.

* * *

She ate it on the east stair, in the dark, where the steps ran out against the wall and there was a ledge no wider than a book. It was cold. She ate with her fingers. It was good fish.

She tried to think about the train, and thought instead about the stencil on the car. It was a clean stencil. Whoever had made it had cut it carefully, with a steady hand, and it had been used a great many times without the edges getting soft. Someone had been paid to do that. Someone had a job making that mark.

*Four thousand,* she thought. *I know a great many.*

It was true, probably. The woman was not a liar. She would have met the housekeeper and the cook and the man who polished the cars. She would have learned their names. She would have gone on thinking they were what she had met.

And the other thing was also true, which was that Blake had stood on a roof like that one, in the dark, with a cutter in one hand and a lantern in the other, and had watched the same sort of train come in. She had never done a thing to a guard that she could not have defended in the moment. Whether she could defend it now was something she kept in a drawer.

So she did not argue. She was not sure that was cowardice. She was not sure that it wasn't.

She sat on the ledge until the bells rang eleven and then twelve.

* * *

At ten past three, the door opened. Blake was on her bunk, with the lamp on, and the book on her knees, not read. Yang and Ruby slept. In the doorway, Weiss stood in her coat with her shoes in her hand, carried by the heels so as not to make a noise.

They both stopped.

"You're awake," said Weiss.

"The lamp's on."

"It's low."

"Yes."

Weiss crossed the room and put her shoes in the wardrobe, heels to the back wall. She hung up her coat. She stood with her back to the room and her hand flat on the wardrobe door, for what was longer than it took to close a wardrobe door.

"At dinner," she said.

"Mm."

"Did I say something?"

Blake looked at the back of her head. The tie had come loose, and a white-gold thread of hair lay along the nape of her neck. There was a smudge of ink on the collar.

"No."

"Because it seemed—"

"It's fine."

Weiss's hand moved a little on the door. It looked as if she had a sentence ready and had run it through, and found it wanting, and was now looking for a different one in the wardrobe.

"All right," she said.

She got into bed. She lay on her side, turned toward the wall. After a time she said, in a small clear voice, to the plaster, "Goodnight, Blake."

"Goodnight."

The lamp stayed on. Blake lay on her back and listened to the other heartbeat across the room until it slowed, which took forty minutes. She had never listened to anyone's so closely. She did not know whether it was a kindness or a theft.

She did not turn the lamp off.

# 5. heart slow to learn

> *Pity me that the heart is slow to learn*
> *What the swift mind beholds at every turn.*
>
> — Edna St. Vincent Millay

The radiator in their room had two settings: off, and the surface of the sun. Maintenance had been informed. Maintenance had said Thursday, and then the Thursday after.

They slept with the window open and the blankets kicked down. Ruby and Yang slept on top of their sheets in as little as decency allowed. Blake slept in her clothes, as she did, curled to the wall. Weiss slept in a long nightdress with the sleeves pushed to the elbow, and badly, and on the fourth night of it she woke at ten past two with a throat like paper.

She took the glass from her desk and filled it at the tap in the corner. She drank half. She turned around with the other half in her hand.

Blake was on the windowsill.

She sat with one knee up and her back to the room, looking out. The moon was low and big and the window held it like a frame. The ribbon was in her hand. She held it loosely, as one holds something one has picked up and has not yet decided about.

On her head, in the moonlight, were two ears.

They were black. They were covered in short fur, close and fine, with the pelt going to a pale lining where they faced the light. They stood a little taller than Weiss would have guessed. Her first thought was that they were a hat. Her second was *no*. She did not have a third. What she had was a kind of silence in the place where a third should go.

The left one turned.

It turned by itself, toward Weiss, the whole ear swivelling on its base while the head held still. The sound did it; her feet on the boards, her breath. Blake went rigid. The ear stayed pointed at the room.

Then she looked over her shoulder. Her eyes, in that light, were nearly white.

Weiss stepped back.

It was less than a step. It was the half of one. Her heel found the leg of the desk and the glass tipped in her hand and the water went over her wrist and down her nightdress and onto the floor. It made a loud sound. Neither of them looked at her. They both looked at the boards, where it spread and darkened and found the crack between two planks and ran along it.

"Oh," said Weiss.

"Yes," said Blake.

"I see."

"Yes."

She felt her face arrange itself. It was a thing she had been trained to do, and it did it without consulting her: the chin, the mouth, an expression of gracious composure she had worn at forty dinners. It came down over everything like a lid.

"That's quite all right," she said.

She heard it as it left her. It was already in the room.

"Thank you," said Blake.

Her voice was perfectly level. She lifted the ribbon and tied it in four movements, fast, in the dark, without a glass, with the practice of ten thousand mornings. The ears went flat under it, folded down, and the bow settled on top of them like a small lie. She slid off the sill and crossed to her bunk and lay down facing the wall.

She did not look at Weiss. She did not say goodnight.

Weiss stood where she was. After some time she took the towel from the hook, the one she'd used on her hair, and knelt and wiped the floor. She did it thoroughly. She wiped along the crack between the planks. She hung the towel up straight, and drank what was left in the glass, which tasted of nothing, and got into bed.

* * *

She lay on her back.

Her right hand was on the blanket. The thumb was moving across the first two fingers, slowly, back and forth, as if testing the weave of something. She noticed this and stopped it. After a while it started again.

She made a list. She composed it in the dark, in order, the way she composed all lists.

*One. Blake is a Faunus.*

*Two. This is not a fact about Blake's character. Blake is quick, and dry, and reads in the cold, and taped my wrist.*

*Three. The ears are ears.*

*Four. I stepped back.*

She went back to one and ran it again. It was a good list. It had no flaws in it. It arrived at four every time, and at four it stopped, and she kept putting her foot down for a fifth.

Across the room the lamp was off. It was the first night it had been. In the dark, beyond the wardrobe, she could hear the particular quality of someone not sleeping.

* * *

In the morning she was perfect.

She said Blake's name twelve times before lunch. She held the door. At the dining hall she stood aside and said "After you," which she had not said to anyone since the first week of term, and which, when it came out of her, sounded like a thing said by a footman. She asked if Blake would like the window seat. Blake said, "No, thank you." She asked if Blake would like the other window seat. Blake said, "No, thank you," in precisely the same tone, and it went by like a train that does not stop at your station.

"What did you do?" said Yang.

They were at the end of the table. Blake had gone to refill her tea. Ruby was in the middle of a story about a boarhound and a mailbox.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're being nice."

"I am frequently nice."

"You're being nice like a nurse." Yang waved her fork in a small circle. "Like somebody's got a thing, and you don't want to say."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Uh-huh," said Yang, and put the fork in her mouth, and looked at her for a long time.

* * *

It happened again in the afternoon.

It was Professor Port's lecture, and Port was explaining the habits of the Ursa to a room that had stopped listening an hour ago. Weiss was taking notes. Blake was three seats down, and in the corridor outside a door slammed, and Blake's head turned toward it.

The bow moved.

It moved by itself, a short sharp tilt, the way a flag does in a gust. Under it the ears had swung toward the sound, and for a half second the ribbon rode up on one side, and the shape of what lay beneath it showed through, plain as a hand under a cloth.

Weiss's shoulder jerked.

It was nothing. It was an inch. She turned it into a reach for her pen, and picked up the pen, and the pen shook.

Blake's eyes came around from the door. They went to the shoulder, and from the shoulder to the pen, and then they went back to the front of the room and stayed there. She wrote something in her notebook. She wrote it very evenly.

After the bell she was gone before Weiss had closed her bag.

* * *

In the washroom, at four, with no one there, Weiss stood at the mirror and said it.

"It doesn't matter to me."

That was wrong. It sounded like a hand being waved.

"I don't mind."

Worse. It supposed there was something to mind.

"It's entirely your business."

A form letter. She could have been declining an invitation.

"I'm sorry."

She looked at it for some time. She could not make the sentence say what for. She would be apologising for a thing she had done with her ankle. She would be saying, to a person she had hurt, *I know that you were hurt,* and the person would say, correctly, *then why did you.*

She did not know why. She knew, perfectly well, the reasons it was wrong. She could have given a lecture. She could have given two, with footnotes. But somewhere below the place where the lecture was kept there was a part of her that had learned it differently, and earlier, and from other teachers, and it had not been told.

She ran the tap. She put her hands under it, and held them there, and the water was cold, and she watched it go over her wrists until her fingers stopped feeling like something they were trying to hold.

In the mirror the scar showed over her left eye. She looked at it as she always did, without recognising anything.

* * *

Back at her desk she took out her notebook and found the list. She had written it out fair, after breakfast, in ink. It had four lines.

Under the fourth she wrote a fifth.

*Practise.*

She pressed so hard that it came through to the page beneath.

# 6. reading list

Blake came to the north court at six. She came at two minutes past, which was how Weiss knew she had stood outside the gate for two minutes first.

"Good morning."

"Morning."

That was all. They fought for an hour. It was the best they had fought. Weiss noticed that, and did not know what to do with it, and went on noticing it through the whole of her cooldown and the walk back and the shower, which was in her allotted slot, and which she took at a third of her usual speed.

Four mornings of that. On the fifth she tried again.

"Did you sleep?"

"Fine."

"It's still quite warm in the—"

"Yes."

On the sixth morning she did not say anything, and Blake did not say anything, and the silence was no better, but it was not worse, and she thought that might be how it was going to go.

* * *

The library was empty at that hour on the east side. She had not been in it for a week. She had avoided it, if she was honest, because the chair opposite her usual table was a chair, and she knew who had sat in it.

She took the list to the catalogue desk and asked the librarian for a thing she had never asked for before.

He looked at her for a time. He had an orange half peeled in one hand. Then he wrote three call numbers on a slip and added a fourth from memory, and slid it across, and returned to the orange.

She read for six days.

*The Faunus Question,* in three volumes, by a man named Tull, who was against them and said so civilly. *Hearth and Hardship,* which was about households, and had drawings. A short history of the Faunus rights movement with a green cover, written by a professor who clearly meant well. An account of the war, the old one, eighty years back, which she had been taught in school as a story about a king. It was a different story when told from the other side of the table.

She was a good reader. She took notes. She was halfway through the second volume of Tull on the fourth night before she noticed that she had stopped taking them.

The footnotes had caught her. At first they were only footnotes: a ledger, a treaty, a series of dates. Then there was one that said *S.D.C., wage records, first quarter,* and one that said *S.D.C., Mine No. 2, contract labour,* and one that said *N. Schnee, letter to the Board, on the matter of housing,* and she stopped, and read it twice, and then she turned back and read the paragraph it hung from.

*...the Company's founder, who in this respect was no worse than his peers and in others rather better, nonetheless saw no contradiction between a charitable disposition and a deduction for rent...*

Nicholas Schnee was her grandfather. He had died before she was born. There was a portrait of him in the east hall at home. He wore a grey coat and looked as if he were about to say something reasonable.

She closed the book. She looked at the cover, as if she might have picked up the wrong one, and then she put it down square to the edge of the table and sat with her hands on either side of it.

After a time she began again, and read to the end.

* * *

On the seventh day she took four of the books to the dormitory and left them on Blake's pillow, squared into a stack, with a note on the top. She had written the note three times. The version she left said:

*Background. —W.S.*

It looked like the label on a file.

She went out before Blake came back. She sat through two lectures without hearing either. At supper she watched Blake not look at her.

In the morning there was a stack on her own desk.

It was her stack. She knew it by Tull, who had been on top. But there were things in it now: small slips of white paper, pushed into the books at various depths, flagged with a pencil in a hand she knew. Upright, small, educated. Quick.

*Written by a mine owner.* That was the green one, and it was stuck at the front.

*Written by a mine owner's nephew.* Tull.

*Good.* That was stuck in *Hearth and Hardship,* at a drawing of a kitchen.

*Wrong. See p. 212. They didn't leave. They were moved.*

*Be careful of this man.*

And on the top of the stack, folded once, a single sheet.

*You didn't have to.*

Weiss stood with the page in her hand. Nothing happened on her face, which was the usual arrangement. She straightened the stack against the desk, squared the corners, and then took the sheet and read it again. It was five words. She had thought it would take longer.

She put it in the inside pocket of her bag, flat, between two sheets of notepaper.

* * *

For the next several days she practised.

She did it as she did everything. She set a time. In the evenings, after supper, when the others were out, she sat at her desk with her homework and Blake sat in the window with the ribbon off. This was a thing that had begun on its own, on the second night after the books, without any announcement: the ribbon coming off, quietly, as one removes a shoe, and the ears coming up, and the page turning. Neither of them said anything.

Weiss did her sums.

And every so often a sound would come from somewhere, the radiator knocking, someone on the stair, and one of the ears would swing toward it. And something in her would go to jump.

She held still. That was all there was to it. She held still and let the thing in her go to jump and arrive at nothing, and the ear came back to centre, and she wrote down the next figure. She practised it like scales: slowly, and without admiration. The first night, she held still eleven times. The second night, nine. The fourth night she was well into a long division, and looked up to find that the sky had gone dark and the lamp was on, and that she had not once thought about it for what must have been an hour.

She went back to the division. She did not tell anyone. Nothing had happened that anyone could have been told.

"You can look," said Blake.

It was the sixth night. Ruby and Yang were at a party on the fourth floor that had been described, by Yang, as an "event." Weiss had a pencil in her mouth.

"I have been looking."

"At the wall."

"At the wall behind you."

"That's a kind of looking."

Weiss took the pencil out. "Yes."

"It's rude."

"I know."

Blake had not turned her head. She was reading, or was holding a book in front of her face, in the lamp. Her ears were up and slightly forward, and Weiss, who had been looking at the wall behind them for a fortnight, put the pencil down and looked at them.

They were not like anything. They were ears. The left was pitched a little further forward than the right. The fur at the tip was shorter, and she could see the grain of it, the small whorl at the base, where it met the hair of the head. It moved a fraction. It had been listening to the pencil.

She looked for what she imagined was a long time. Then she looked at the rest of Blake, who was looking at her with a face that gave away nothing at all.

"May I ask something?"

"You may ask."

"Does it hurt, the ribbon?"

"At the hinge." Blake touched, with two fingers, the place where the ear met the skull. "After a few hours."

"Then why—"

"People look."

"They would look at the ears."

"They would look at the ears. And then they would look at me differently."

Weiss said, "I did."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry I stepped back."

Blake closed the book. She did it slowly. It took, Weiss thought, the longest time she had ever seen anyone take to close a book.

"I'd have stepped back."

"No, you wouldn't."

"I might. In your place."

"That's generous."

"It's not generous." The ear on the left turned a degree, away, and returned. "I've been looking at people's faces for six years when they find out. Yours was one of the less bad ones."

"You did."

"It was unforgivable."

"It was fairly unforgivable."

"Don't say that one."

"Which?"

"*All right.* You said it then."

"*Okay.*"

"That isn't formal."

"Good," said Blake.

Weiss was silent for some time. In the corridor someone laughed and the laugh went away down the stairs. The ear on the left swung toward it and back.

And nothing in her moved.

"Okay," she said.

Blake looked at her a moment longer. Then she opened the book again, and Weiss picked up her pencil, and neither of them said anything at all.

# 7. not finished

"Hendry died this morning," said Weiss.

She said it to her desk. She was reading something on her scroll, a company bulletin, one of the ones her father's office sent every day at seven, and she read the line once and then said it aloud in the voice of a person reading it back to herself, to check.

Blake was on her bunk with a book. Ruby was in the shower. Yang was out.

"Cornelius Hendry. Forty-one. He had three children." Weiss turned the scroll face down. "I didn't know his name until Tuesday. I looked it up. He drove a regional line for eleven years."

There was nothing to say to that and so Blake didn't say it. She turned a page she had not read.

That night, at the washbasin, she took the pencil from her pocket and tore a leaf from the back of the book. She wrote four words and a letter. She looked at it for a time. Then she folded it once and left it on Weiss's desk, under the lamp, where it would be found.

*Roof, after lights. If you're willing. —B.*

* * *

The roof of the east wing was reached by a ladder, a hatch, and a long cold stretch of gravel with a lip at the edge. The moon was broken, as it always was, a big pale disc with a bite out of one side, and the cliff was somewhere past the lip, and past that the lights of Vale in a spill.

She had taken off the ribbon. Her ears were out in the wind and the wind was the worst of it. It was unbearable and it was clean. She sat with her back against the housing of the ventilation shaft and her knees up and her hands clasped around them, and she waited, and did not know if she was waiting for a person.

The hatch opened at a quarter past midnight.

Weiss climbed out in her coat. She stood. She had her hands in her pockets and her chin up, and in the half-light she looked like a person who had come to inspect a property. She did not come any nearer than the hatch.

"I got your note."

"Thank you for coming."

"I'm not sure that—" She stopped. "Go on."

Blake looked at the lights. It was easier than looking at the person.

"I should tell you something. Before you're kind to me anymore."

"I was not aware that I—"

"The books."

"Ah."

"I was in the White Fang."

The wind took it, and brought it back.

She had thought about this for three years, off and on, and had run through it in the dark of other rooms in other cities, and in none of the versions had it been this short. It was one sentence. It was all there was.

"Six years. Since I was twelve. My father started it and he left when it changed, and I stayed. I was—" She heard her own voice go level, the way it did, the way it had in the cellars. "I was good at it."

"At what?"

"Getting in. First through a door. I could do a lock in under a minute." She held her hands out in front of her in the moonlight. They were quite still. "The cut on the couplings. You said it was always the same."

"Yes."

"It's a particular cut. It's called a – it's got a name. I taught it to people."

Weiss did not speak.

"I didn't do the one on the news. I'd left by then. But I know who did." She lowered her hands. "I did others. I don't think I killed anyone. I don't *know*. I held doors. I carried things. I stood where I was told. I knew what we were for and I stayed. I stayed for a long time after I knew."

"Hendry," said Weiss.

"I know the name."

"Why did you leave?"

"Because of a train."

It was the whole answer. She had no other.

She had expected something. Shouting; or a hand going to a weapon; or a sound like the sound of the cafeteria when the tray went down. What she got was Weiss standing at the hatch with her hands in her pockets. Her face in the moonlight was white. She was looking at the middle distance between Blake and the sky.

She said, "Excuse me."

She went down through the hatch, and the hatch came down after her, and shut with a small flat noise.

Blake sat on the roof until she could not feel her hands.

* * *

It took four minutes.

She had always been able to do it in four. The duffel from under the bunk; the second pair of boots; the blanket, rolled; the book; the tin with the knife-oil. Not the lamp. Not the ribbon. She left the ribbon on the pillow, and then she thought, *with what, then,* and took it again.

Yang was asleep on her back with her mouth open. Ruby had a sock on her face. Weiss's bunk was empty and made.

She sat on her own bed with the bag at her feet and her coat on and the light off.

She thought it through. It was a skill. Weiss would go to the Headmaster, or to the Dean, or to her father's lawyers; it would be a night, or a day. There would be a woman with a clipboard. They would not arrest her at Beacon. They would put her on a train. She did not think they would put her in a cell. She did not know where the train would go. She tried to think of a city she had not already left.

She listened.

Every sound in the building came to her through the floor. Someone on the third floor with a tap running. A heel in the corridor. When she heard heels her ears went flat against her skull with a small clicking effort, because the one heel she was waiting for would be Glynda's, and it did not come. Every bell she counted. Two. Three.

At four she began to think about the door. She had, over the weeks, come to know the sound of it: the way it stuck at the top, how it gave with a small suck of air. She thought, *She will open it, and that will be the end.* And she thought, *She won't open it,* and her ears went flat again.

At ten past five, someone came up the stairs.

She knew the tread. It was a very upright tread, even and light, a person who walked as if the stairs were being watched. It came along the corridor. It stopped at the door, and stayed stopped for a time, and she thought *Now.*

The door opened. It stuck at the top.

Weiss stood in the doorway.

She was in her coat. The coat was dark at the shoulders and there was frost in her hair, which was down; she had taken it out of its tie, and it hung loose over one shoulder, white and a little wild. Her cheeks were red with the cold. She had been walking for hours and it was written all over her. She was wearing one glove and holding the other.

She looked at the bag.

She looked at it for what felt like a long time. Her face did not change. Then she looked at Blake, who had not moved, and said, quite clearly, in the voice she used to open a recitation:

"I am not finished being angry."

Blake said nothing.

"I should like you to remain until I am."

* * *

She crossed the room and sat on her own bunk, across from Blake, with her hands between her knees.

"I've been walking," she said. "I went to the cliff and back. Twice. I composed several speeches."

"Okay."

"None of them were adequate. They had a good deal of *whereas.*" She was looking at the floor. "I want to be very clear that I am angry. That is the first thing. I'd like it noted."

"Noted."

"I am angry that you didn't tell me. I am angry that I sat there—" She stopped. "I sat there at that table and said *a gang with a slogan* with you eating fish. I'm angry about the train. I'm angry that a man called Hendry had three children and I'd never heard of him. I am very angry that I can't tell you which of those it is. I thought if I walked far enough it would sort itself."

"Did it?"

"No."

"How long?"

"What?"

"How long will you be."

"I don't know. I've never done this before."

She said it with such a lack of humour that Blake did not know, for a moment, whether it was a joke. Then she saw that it was not. It was a plain fact. It was a thing a person said when she had been taught that there was a way of doing it, and had been taught it wrong.

Weiss lifted her head.

"Tell me one thing you did."

"What?"

"One. A specific. I can't be angry at a category. I've been trying since midnight."

Blake looked at the bag.

She had meant to say something general. She had a kind of statement, ready, that she gave to people: *I was involved in activities.* But there was the face in front of her, with its frost-burned cheeks and the red-rimmed eye and the scar, and the pale hair loose, and it was waiting, and it was not going to take the statement.

"In Mistral," she said. "There was a man carrying a crate. It was Dust. We were moving it, and there was a door, and I was nearest. I held the door for him. I knew where the crate was going. It was going to a shop. The man had hired a – it doesn't matter. It was going to be used on him." Her voice stopped, and found itself again. "I held the door. He said, *thanks.*"

"He thanked you."

"I said, *sure.*"

The radiator knocked. It was warm in the room, for once. Yang turned over.

"Thank you," said Weiss.

"For what?"

"For not saying you were sorry."

Blake stared at her.

"If you had," said Weiss, "I believe I should have said something I couldn't take back. I don't want it yet."

"Yet."

"Yes."

She stood, and began to put her gloves on, and found she was already wearing one and took it off again.

"I am going to the library to be angry in there," she said. "You may come, if you like. I'd prefer you didn't speak."

* * *

The library opened at six. The night librarian was still there; he had been there since ten the evening before and showed no sign of leaving. He looked up when they came in. He looked at the clock, and at the duffel Blake had forgotten to put down, and at Weiss's hair. He did not ask.

Weiss took the east table. Blake sat on the other side, two chairs along, with the bag at her feet. Neither of them opened anything.

It was a long time. The light came in at the window, grey, then white. The pipes started up. A boy came in, saw them, and went out again. Blake watched Weiss not read. She did it with great concentration, with a pencil against her lip and her eyes fixed on a point two inches above the page. At some point she began to hum, three notes, a rest, and a phrase, and stopped, and began again, and stopped, and her mouth went thin.

Blake's ears moved. She let them.

At eight, a saucer was set down on the table between them.

It had an orange on it, peeled, and divided into its segments, and laid out in a ring. The librarian did not speak. He went back to his desk. The smell came up from the plate, very strong in the cold room, and Weiss looked at it as if it had been an official document.

After a while she took a segment. She ate it with her whole attention. She wiped her fingers on her handkerchief.

Blake took one. She licked her thumb.

Weiss looked at the wall.

* * *

It was noon before they went back. Yang opened one eye when they came in and said, "Where've you been?" and Weiss said "Library," and Yang said, "Both of you?" and Weiss said "Yes," and Yang said, "At six in the morning," and nobody answered. Blake lay down on her bunk, in her coat, with the bag still at her feet, and was asleep before she had finished thinking that she would not be.

She woke to the sound of a pen.

It was a small sound, one she knew. A nib, on paper, going steadily. She lay still and kept her eyes shut and listened to it go. It went for some time. Then it stopped, and the cap clicked on.

When she opened her eyes, Weiss was standing at the door with the pen in her hand. She was not looking at Blake. She was looking at the sheet on the wall.

Blake got up and went to read it.

*12. No leaving without notice.*

It was beneath the eleventh in the same neat black ink. It was in ink.

"That's not a rule you can enforce," said Blake.

"It is in ink."

"That's not what makes a—"

"It's in ink."

Blake looked at the pen. After a moment, she took the pencil stub from her pocket and wrote, beside the twelfth, in small upright letters: *(Emergencies excepted.)*

Weiss looked at it. She took the cap off the pen. She struck out the parenthesis with a single line, neat as a ruler, and capped the pen.

"I don't want a promise," she said, to the wall.

"That's all?"

"It is a great deal." Her voice was very dry and very careful. "I do not want to be told you'll never go. I wouldn't believe it, and if I did, it would be worse. I'd like notice. If you're going. Tell me you're going."

Blake read the line again. She read the strike-through.

She put the pencil back in her pocket.

"Okay," she said.

Weiss nodded. She put the pen on the desk, squared with the edge, and went out to find breakfast, and it was not until the door had closed that Blake understood she had left her scroll behind, face up, on the desk, with the bulletin still open on it, where anyone could read it.

She did not read it. She turned it face down.

Then she unpacked the bag.

# 8. kettle

*The kettle.*

It was not a kettle. It was a ceramic bowl on Weiss's side of the desk, and a pot, and a tin, and a small red crystal the size of a thumbnail, in a dish of its own.

"That's Dust," said Blake.

"The lowest grade. It is perfectly safe."

"It's a bomb in the shape of a crystal."

"It is a heating element." Weiss dropped the crystal into the bowl of water. It sank, and sat on the bottom, giving off nothing at all, and after a minute a thin line of bubbles began to rise off its edges. "Rule four says not near the radiator. It says nothing about on the desk."

"It says *Dust is stored in the locker.*"

"Stored. This is in use."

She was pouring the water over the leaves by then. The tea was black and smelled of smoke, and of something burnt and sweet underneath, like a field in autumn. She set the lid on the pot, and folded her hands, and began to hum.

Three notes, a rest, a phrase that climbed. The eight bars, through once, and once again.

At the end of the second she lifted the lid.

"It's four minutes," said Blake.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The tune. Twice through. You time it by that."

Weiss looked at the pot. "I have always done it that way."

"What is it?"

"It isn't — I don't know that it's anything. I've always hummed it." She poured two cups, with the strainer, and her hand was entirely steady. "It's just there."

Blake smelled the tea before she drank it, which she did with everything. Then she drank. It was strong and dark and a little bitter, and it was good, and she could not have said why it made her want to sit down.

"It's good."

That was all. It was, she thought, the whole of their marriage. It had been so since she was a child. It was a thing that said *please,* and was answered *no,* in two words, each way, with their names.

"Why were the claims denied?"

"Because the Company was not liable. The roof failed. Mining is a dangerous occupation, and the contracts are very clear. A report was commissioned, and the engineers found no negligence."

"Who commissioned the report?"

"The Company."

"The engineers?"

"The Company's."

"There was a request for shoring on Level 3."

He did not answer immediately. It was a small pause. It might have been a man swallowing.

"You've been talking to someone."

"I have read the Q2 summary. The support budget for Level 3 was reduced by forty per cent last autumn." She had not raised her voice. "It's on page nineteen. In the third table. I noticed it because the line before it had gone up."

Her father looked at her for some time.

He looked at her with an expression she had not seen on him before. It was a kind of attention, direct, undivided, and entirely without hostility; it was the way he looked at a ledger when the figures balanced. A man might have looked at a fine instrument that had turned out to work.

"You read it twice," he said.

"Yes."

"Good."

She felt it go through her like a cold hand. It was *pleasure.* He was pleased. It was the first time in years that he had been pleased with her, and she saw, with great clarity, that she had wanted it, and that she would go on wanting it, all her life, whatever else she did.

"Then you will also have seen," said her father, "that the reduction was made by the Board, on the recommendation of the Director of Operations, on the basis of the Council's allocation, which was reduced in the previous quarter in response to the Vacuan tariff. Each of those persons acted correctly within his office. If you wish to find the man who killed the men at Hollin Gap, you will find that you have a great many to choose from, and that each of them did exactly as he was asked."

"And you?"

"I signed."

"Then—"

"I am the part that signs." He said it gently. "As, in time, will you be. I do not say it to distress you. A company is a method by which a great many persons are enabled to do a small thing each, so that none of them need do a large one. It is a considerable moral achievement, and it has made your life possible. You will find it very restful."

* * *

She sat and looked at it.

It was a complete thing. It had no gap in it. She turned it over, as she turned all problems, searching for the edge. If the order came from one man's will, and the rest only worked it, then the man was answerable and the rest were instruments; and she was one. She had signed. She had a neat hand. *Man does but work his will,* the old tutor had said once, of some other matter, with his small dry cough. It was meant to be a consolation.

And if it came from no one's will, but from the arrangement, the whole slow arithmetic of Board and Council and tariff, then no one was answerable, and she was not, and nor was he; and thirty-one men were dead in a hill with a wall across the door.

She went round it twice. There was no place in it to stand and say *I.*

She saw that this was the point.

"I would like to be excused."

"You haven't finished your fish."

"I would like to be excused."

Her father regarded her. It went on a long time. She watched him decide something, and put it away, and take up his fork.

"You may."

"Thank you, Father."

She rose. She pushed in her chair, squarely. She did not look at her mother, or at Whitley, or at Klein, who was standing at the sideboard with his hands behind him and his long grey face turned, slightly, away.

She went out through the door at the end of the room. It was a long door, and it closed on a spring, and in the hall the light from the chandelier fell on the black and white tiles in great even squares. She walked across them. She heard, faintly, her own heels.

It was not until she was nearly at the stair that she found she was humming.

She did not know what it was she was humming. She did not know when she had begun. It came up out of her chest and into the air of the hall, three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed; she heard it go up under the chandelier and spread.

"Weiss."

He had come to the dining-room door. He stood in it with the light behind him. He had not raised his voice.

"That tune."

"Yes?"

"Please don't."

She stopped.

Something crossed her father's face. She would think about it a good deal, afterward. It was not anger. It was not anything that she had a name for. It was as though he'd heard a sound from the next room, in a house he had been assured was empty, and was waiting, very patiently, for the sound to come again.

"Not in this house," said her father.

* * *

She did not weep. She went up to her room and shut the door and stood in the middle of it for some while.

Then she knelt by the wardrobe and took down the hatbox.

The glove was on top. She put it on her left hand and took it off. Under it lay the sheet, folded in three; under that the marbled notebook, with its thirty-one names, in her best hand; under that the book with its green cloth spine. She turned it over. It was still warped from the damp. It fell open, as it always did, at page 112.

She took the pencil from the desk.

She had not written in it before. She had read it through four times; she had read the margins, and the long unanswered line on page 14 with its hook at the end. She sat on the floor with her back to the bed. She looked for a long while at the strip of white at the edge of the page.

Then she wrote it, small, in her steep light hand, under the last of Blake's notes. She did not write a date. She did not write a name.

*I signed.*

She put the pencil down. She closed the book. She sat there until it was quite dark, and outside the window, in the east garden, the lamps came on one after another along the paths.

# 15. service entrance

The pantry light was on at eleven.

She saw it from the end of the passage, a bar of yellow under the door, and stood in the dark on the runner with her hand on the wall. She had not slept. She had lain on the made bed in her clothes for three hours, with the lamp off, rehearsing; and she had come to a decision, and then been unable to get her feet off the floor.

She knocked.

"Come."

Klein was at the long table with a tray of silver in front of him and a cloth in his hand. It was the good silver. There was no need to polish it. It lay in rows, each piece squared against the next, and he was working on a soup spoon with small, slow, circular strokes, and did not look up.

"Miss Weiss."

"Klein, I need to go to Hollin Gap."

The cloth stopped.

It was all that stopped. His hand went on holding the spoon, and his face, long and soft and grey-moustached, remained turned down toward it, as though the spoon had said something that required thought.

"Miss Weiss," he said, "don't."

"I'm not asking your permission."

"No." He set the spoon in its row. "You're asking for a coat."

She stared at him.

"It's a day by rail to the Mantle depot, and then another by sled. The line beyond was cut in the spring. There'll be a man at the Mantle depot who will drive for a price." He had folded the cloth into a square. He was speaking very mildly, to the tray. "I should say you want a good deal more than a coat. But I'd begin there."

"You'll be dismissed."

"He may try."

"Klein—"

"I'm very old, Miss Weiss. It takes a great deal to move something that old." He looked up for the first time. His eyes were small and wet and perfectly steady. "I have stood at that sideboard for twenty-six years. I've poured every glass in this house. I have been of use in the pantry, and the dining room, and the front hall, in more ways than I would care to enumerate, and I find, upon reflection, that I should like to be of use in another room for once."

She did not say anything. She found that she could not.

He opened the drawer under the table.

* * *

There was a coat. It was a long grey wool one, nine years old, heavy as a rug, with a worn collar and a button replaced with another of a different make. He held it up. It was too large. It would come to her ankles.

"You will look like a coachman."

"Thank you."

"It is warm. It was warmer in the old days, but it is still warm." He laid it over the back of a chair. From the second drawer he took a pair of gloves, brown, thick, lined with fleece, and set them on the coat.

"I have gloves," said Weiss.

She put out her hands to show him.

The right was white silk with a pearl button. The left was black wool, cut off at the knuckle, and rather large, and pulled up under her cuff.

Klein regarded them.

He did it for a considerable time. His moustache did something. It did it once and then it stopped, and his face, otherwise, remained as grave as a church.

"So you have," he said.

He put the brown gloves back in the drawer and closed it.

From the pocket of his waistcoat he brought a small envelope, thick, tied with string. He laid it on the coat. She knew without looking what it was, and put her hand over it.

"I can't."

"It is not a gift."

"Klein."

"It is a loan, Miss Weiss, against the tea. You may repay it," said the butler, "by never mentioning it."

She stood with her hand on the envelope. It was warm from being close to him. There was a clock on the wall of the pantry, a cheap white one with black hands, and in the silence it was possible to hear the second hand moving.

"The house," she said.

"At Camp Four? It was kept up for the inspectors. Two rooms and a stove. The key is on the board, with the others. I have not seen anyone ask for it since the spring." He paused. "No one will look for you there. It has your father's name on every wall."

"That's a poor recommendation."

"It's the best one I have," said Klein.

* * *

She wrote the note at the dining-room table in the dark.

She had her own pen. She could do it by touch. The room was cold and she did not light a lamp. The table went away from her on both sides in a long pale gleam, and at the far end, where the bare place was, the cloth lay smooth and white and unmarked, and the plate in the dark was a round of faint light.

*I am going. I will not say where. I will write when I can. —W.*

She had no one to give it to. There was a rule, and it was in ink, and she did not mean to be the second to break it. She folded the note in three. She laid it on the fifth plate, centred, squarely.

She stood looking at it.

It looked like a place card.

* * *

The kitchen was down a short flight and along a flagged passage that smelled of onions and coal. She had been in it perhaps six times in her life. She had been taken there as a child, for the pleasure of the cook, and had been lifted onto a stool and given a spoon; she remembered the spoon, and the warm wooden table, and a pair of large floury hands, and not the face above them.

There was a light at the far end. A lamp on the range.

A woman stood in front of it, with her back to the door, in a white apron and a cap, stirring a pot.

She was broad. She was perhaps fifty. Her sleeves were pushed above the elbow and her arms were thick and brown, and when she turned her head at the sound of the step the cap shifted, and Weiss saw her ears, which were round and dark and set high, under the cloth.

"Miss."

"Mrs. Abney."

The woman did not stop stirring. She looked at the grey coat, and at the envelope in Weiss's hand, and at Weiss's face. She did not look surprised. She looked as a person looks at a clock to see that it agrees with another.

"Klein's been down," she said.

"Yes."

"Sit."

She sat at the long table. It was scrubbed pale as bone. Mrs. Abney took a bundle from the dresser and set it in front of her, tied in a cloth: a loaf, a heel of cheese, a twist of salt fish, a flask. She put it down with some force.

"I'm not helping you, miss."

"No."

"I'm feeding somebody that's leaving. It's different." She wiped her hands on her apron, one and then the other. "If they ask me, I gave you nothing. If they ask Orla, she was asleep. If they ask Teodor, he'll say he's got a bad back."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do. But it's all right."

Weiss's hand, on the bundle, tightened.

She heard it. It was a phrase the cook had used ten thousand times, to the kitchen boy, to the dog. It was a thing said when a pot was put right. It went by like a bird against a window, and was gone, and the room was exactly as it had been.

She said: "What is your name?"

The cook looked at her.

"Abney."

"Your first name."

It was a long moment. The pot ticked on the range. Mrs. Abney's face, broad and closed and weathered, did a thing Weiss could not read; it was neither pleasure nor its opposite; it was a kind of slow settling, as of a door being tried.

"Dorrit," she said.

"Dorrit."

"It's nobody's business."

"Thank you," said Weiss. "For the bread."

"Mm." The cook turned back to the range. "Mind you eat it."

* * *

The back door was at the end of the passage, down three steps. It was narrow and painted green. She had never used it. She had not known it was green.

Teodor was at the gate.

He stood with his back to the wall in the dark, a thin tall man in a heavy jacket with the collar turned up, and as she came across the yard he took one hand from his pocket and lifted the bar. He did not look at her. His cap sat far back on his head, and above his brow two small antlers, no longer than her thumb, stood against the sky.

"Teodor."

"Miss."

"I'm sorry. I should have asked."

"Asked what, miss?"

"Your name." She stopped. "I have known you since I was six."

"It's all right, miss." He said it simply. He had his eyes on the gate. "It's a cold morning. Mind the ice by the second post."

The gate swung. The road lay beyond it, white, running down between the walls toward the lights of the lower city. A little wind came up it. She stood on the threshold with Klein's coat to her ankles and Mrs. Abney's bundle under her arm and, in her pocket, an envelope, and she heard it again, *all right,* in the thin air, in the voice of a man she had never thanked.

She had thought it would sound different now.

She went through.

It had been snowing. A thin dry fall, an inch or two, lying along the road and over the cobbles, perfectly untouched. The stars were out above it. She stood and looked at it. It was the first time in her life she had left a house by the door the work went out of.

She took a step.

Under her boot, high and small and flat, the snow squeaked.

She stopped. She stamped, once, to be sure.

It squeaked.

"There," said Weiss, to no one.

She walked on down the hill.

# 16. camp four

The sled driver left her at the foot of the gap, where the track gave out.

He was a man called Brand, with a face the colour of a boot. He had said eleven words since the depot, six of them about the weather. He lifted her case off the runners and set it in the snow, and stood a moment holding the reins, and looked up the valley.

"There's nothing up there, miss."

"I know."

"Nobody's been up since the spring." He chewed something. "They've a wall on it."

"I know."

"I come by the thirtieth, to the foot, if you've not changed your mind."

She paid him from the envelope. He counted it with a thumb, and nodded, and clicked to the dogs, and the sled went away down the white track, getting smaller, the bells on the harness going thin and then going out. Then there was no sound at all.

She stood in the snow in the coachman's coat. She had a case, a bundle of bread, a rapier, and a hatbox on a strap. She looked at the valley, and began to climb.

* * *

It was a shelf of land above a frozen river.

She came on it in the last of the light. The track bent round a spur of rock and there it was, laid out below the cliff like a thing put down and not picked up again: a long low roof, a squarer one, a row of smaller huts under their burden of snow, a tangle of black poles, and a dead tram on a spur of rail that went into a drift and did not come out. A flag-pole without a flag. A water tower. The chimneys were all cold.

And at the back, against the face of the hill, a square of darkness cut in the rock. It was the width of two carts. It had been closed. Fresh stone, fitted close, the colour of new bread, filled the whole of the opening and ran a little way out on either side; across it, nailed through a timber, was a sign.

She did not go near it.

She stood on the track and looked at the camp for some time. She had been in quiet places. She had stood in the Emerald Forest at dawn, and in the Hall of Banners when it was empty, and once at the bottom of the east well at home, where the air was cold and the walls close. This was a different kind. It was the quiet of a place that had been left. It had a smell, even in that cold: a faint iron smell, and of old coal, and of the sweet thing that a house gives off when no one has lit a fire in it for a long time.

The key was on a wire round her neck. The tag said *No. 4 — Insp.* in Klein's small copperplate.

The inspector's house was the squarer roof. It stood a little apart, at the head of the camp, with its door to the wind. The lock was stiff. She put her shoulder to it. It opened with a noise like a long breath drawn in, and the dark inside came out to meet her, and a smell of cold ash and mouse.

* * *

Two rooms and a stove. Klein had been precise.

The first room was an office. A desk, a chair with one arm, a steel cabinet with its drawers pulled out and empty. A map of the workings on the wall, curling, in the cabinet's shadow. On a shelf by the window, a radio set the size of a small trunk, with a handset on a coiled cord, and a row of dials, and a layer of dust like felt. The second room had a cot with a grey blanket, folded, and a washstand, and a shelf. That was all.

The stove stood in the middle of the floor, a black iron barrel on four legs, with a pipe going up.

She had never lit one.

She was clear on the principle. She had read of it. She found paper in the cabinet, and a box of long matches, and a crate of split wood in the lean-to outside, and she built it in the manner she supposed correct: paper at the bottom, small wood on that, large wood on that. She struck a match and held it to the corner. The paper caught, and flared, and went out. She did it again. She did it eight times. By the ninth the box was half empty and the room was no warmer, and her hands, in the two gloves, had gone numb, and through the window the last of the light had gone.

She stood up.

She went to the case, and took Myrtenaster from its sheath, and turned the chamber until she found a crystal of the lowest red. It was meant for an emergency. She held it a moment, with a feeling of faint shame, as a woman might who has been defeated by a jar. Then she shook one grain of it out onto the paper and struck the match.

The wood went up with a *whump.*

She stepped back. The fire roared in the barrel and the pipe began, alarmingly, to tick. Heat came out at her in a wave, and she stood there in the coachman's coat with her face lit orange, and she realised that she was laughing, in the empty office, in a voice she did not use, quite quietly, and could not stop.

She sat down on the floor.

The room filled. It was warm in an hour. It was hot in two, and she opened the window and put her head out, in the dark, and breathed the cold; and below her the long roofs of the camp lay under the stars, unlit, in rows, like a field of something sown.

She ate Mrs. Abney's bread and the heel of the cheese. She slept in the coat on the cot.

* * *

The log was in the drawer of the desk.

She found it on the second morning, looking for a pencil. A thick book bound in grey canvas, the corners worn through to the board, with a label gummed on the cover in capitals: *RADIO & WEATHER — No. 4.* There was a string tied round it, knotted in a bow, and a pencil on the end of the string.

She opened it at the middle.

It had been ruled by hand, in columns. *Date. Wind. Temp. Remarks. Init.* The entries ran down the pages in a dozen different hands, in pencil, in a cramped hasty style, with the careless abbreviations of people who had a great deal to do and a short time to do it in.

*12th. NNE light. −9. Pay late again. A.V.*

*13th. NNE. −11. Pay Thursday, they say. D.Q.*

She read them as one reads an unfamiliar road, one sign at a time. Wind, cold, pay, a stove pipe cracked. A man named P.O. had been, on the 17th, "sick of beans." A man called E.V. wrote the temperature to a decimal and underlined it. On the 22nd someone had drawn, in the margin of the Remarks, a small and recognisable bird.

She turned the page.

*31st. N mod. −19. Props on 3 gone soft, east drift. Told Mr. H. A.V.*

*36th. N. −21. Noise in the roof on 3. Told Mr. H. He says Thursday. D.Q.*

*38th. N. −22. Thursday. Nothing. Told Mr. H. P.O.*

*39th. Calm. −24. Told Mr. H. E.V.*

*40th. Calm. −25. Roof noise on 3. Told Mr. H. Again. E.V.*

That was the last. Under it the ruled lines went on, clean, down to the bottom of the page, and over, and through the rest of the book. They were all empty.

She read it twice. She read it a third time.

She was aware of the stove ticking behind her. She was aware of a bird, somewhere, outside, making a small unmusical noise in the cold. She stood at the desk in the grey light with the book in both hands, and she noticed that she was holding it carefully, the way one holds something that is still warm.

Mr. H. She knew him. *Harrow, T., Foreman.* She had copied it herself in the best of her hands.

*Again.*

There was no one to tell. There was a report in a black leather binder, with a table on page nineteen, and a determination that the engineers had found no negligence. It was dated nine days after the fortieth. She thought, with great calm, that it was a good document. It was laid out in columns.

She sat down in the chair with one arm. She untied the string, and drew the pencil to her, and turned to the first blank line.

*Date.* She did not know what to put. She wrote, after some thought, *Day 1.*

*Wind.* She went to the window and looked at the snow on the sill. *NW, light.*

*Temp.* There was a thermometer on the frame, a brass one, with a glass tube. She read it. *−20.*

*Remarks.* She sat for a long time with the point on the paper. In the end she wrote what was so.

*Arrived. Stove lit (at the ninth attempt, with assistance).*

*Init.*

She wrote *W.S.* in her best hand, and underlined it, and then, because it was a log, and a log is for others, she looked at it and put a full stop after each letter.

* * *

On the third day she went up to the wall.

She did not mean to. She had a bucket in her hand and was on her way to the river to break the ice, and her feet went by themselves, up the slope, along the length of the long roof, past the dead tram, until she was standing in front of it in the snow with the bucket hanging off her arm.

It was bigger close to. The stone was clean. The mortar, between the blocks, was pale and had not yet weathered. At the top, nailed through a baulk of timber, a metal sign, stamped, black on yellow: *DANGER. NO ENTRY. PROPERTY OF S.D.C.* Below it, on a little brass plate screwed neatly into the stone at the height of a man's chest, someone had cut four lines.

*MINE No. 4. CLOSED IN THE INTERESTS OF SAFETY.*

*THE COMPANY REGRETS.*

She read it. She had a feeling like having something pulled slowly out of the middle of her.

She had the roster by heart. She had had it since the third night. She stood there, with the wind coming down off the hill, and she opened her mouth to say it. The first name was *Anselm Voe.* It was a good name, short, easy to say. She had said it a thousand times in her head.

Nothing came.

She tried again. She shut her mouth, and opened it, and put her tongue to the roof of it. She knew exactly how to do it. She could take a breath from below the ribs and put a high E-flat into the roof of a hall and hold it for eleven seconds with her eyes on the back wall. She could not say a man's name to a wall.

"I'm sorry," she said, to the sign.

It was a poor thing, and she heard it. It was the sort of thing a girl says who has dropped a plate.

She stood there another minute. Then she turned, and went down to the river, and broke the ice with the heel of the bucket, and filled it, and carried it back.

She was halfway across the yard before she knew she was humming. It came up quite easily, out of her chest, into the thin air: three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed. She waited for it to be stopped.

Nobody stopped it.

She went on across the yard in the coachman's coat with the bucket slopping at her knee, humming, and the sound went out over the snow and the long roofs, and into the gap of the valley, and did not come back.

# 17. bunkhouse

On the twelfth day a dog barked.

She was at the stove, with the damper in her hand, trying to learn what it was for. The sound came across the yard, one flat bark and a pause and another, and she put the damper down and went to the window and wiped a hole in the frost.

A figure was coming up the track on skis.

It was small and broad. It moved with a short, steady, shuffling stride, bent forward over the poles, in a heap of grey wool and sheepskin that made it hard to tell where the coat stopped. Behind it a little sled came sliding on a rope. Beside the sled, trotting, with one ear up and one ear down, went a long lean grey dog with a serious face.

Weiss put on the coat. She took Myrtenaster from the peg, and then put it back. She opened the door.

It was a woman. She had stopped at the edge of the yard to knock the snow from her skis, one and then the other, with great deliberation, and she looked up only when she had finished. Her face was brown and creased and wide-cheeked. Her eyes were pale. From under the sheepskin cap, on either side, rose two long white ears, white as the snow, tipped with black, one of them nicked at the edge by a small old notch.

"You'll be the Schnee."

"Yes."

"Thought so." The woman unstrapped the skis. "Smoke on the second day. Nobody else burns wood like they're angry at it."

"I've been told I'm slow with the stove."

"You're not slow. You're loud." She looked Weiss over, once, from the hair to the boots, and her gaze stopped on the hands. "Your gloves don't match."

"No."

"Hm."

She bent and lifted a bundle from the sled. It was tied in oilcloth. She put it into Weiss's arms without ceremony, and it was heavy, and cold, and smelled of smoke and salt. "Fish. Flour. Lamp oil, a can. Four lien. I'll take it in silver if you've got it."

Weiss found the coins. They changed hands.

The dog had come to sit beside the woman's boot. It looked at Weiss. It looked at the door behind her, from which a little warmth was coming, and then at Weiss again, with an air of reasonable inquiry.

"That's Pike," said the woman. "He isn't staying."

"I didn't ask."

"I'm saying." She tugged her cap straight. "I'm Dahl. Tove Dahl. I got your letter."

* * *

She had known it was coming. She had known for twelve days, and for two hundred miles, and she had rehearsed it in the dark on the cot, and it was nothing like.

"Neat hand," said Tove Dahl.

"Mrs. Dahl—"

"Tove. We'll get on." She was looking not at Weiss but past her, up the slope, at the grey square of stone. "I wrote you. I thought they'd keep it back."

"They did. For a time."

"Did you read it?"

"Yes."

"Better than the other, then."

It was said with no change of tone. Weiss felt it arrive, and stood in the doorway, with the oilcloth in her arms.

"Yes."

"Hm." The woman nodded slowly. "Well. You've been up there."

"Yes."

"Did you say anything?"

"No."

"Don't," said Tove. "It's a wall. It can't hear." She shifted her feet in the snow. "I asked for the words. Have you got them?"

She had had them ready. She had had a good many. They lay in order behind her teeth; she could feel them there, polished, in line, and not one of them could be said.

"I didn't know what the letter said," she said. "I signed it without reading. I was told it was a letter of sympathy, and I was glad to sign. I didn't ask to see the second page. That is not an excuse."

"No."

"I know that it isn't."

"Good."

"They decided not to pay. The Company. My father decided. And I signed it."

"You."

"I signed it."

Tove looked at her. It was the first time she had properly done so, and it went on a long while: the pale eyes in the brown creased face, steady and unhurried and entirely without heat. The dog yawned. In the camp behind them, somewhere, a loose sheet of iron ticked in the cold.

"That's the words," said the old woman. "That'll do."

She turned and began to put her skis on the sled.

"That's all?"

"What did you want?" She did not look round. "A scene?"

"I—" Weiss stopped.

"I've no use for you being sorry. I'll tell you now. Don't be sorry at me. It's no good to me and it's tiring for you." Tove straightened. She put her hands in the small of her back and arched, with a grunt, like a woman of any age. "You burn like an engine. Somebody should chop it. You can chop wood."

"I'm sorry?"

"There. That's what I mean."

* * *

The bunkhouse was the long roof.

She had not been inside. She had passed it forty times on the way to the river and the lean-to, and she had learned its outline as one learns the outline of a thing one will not look at: the low eaves, the door at the gable end with its latch tied shut, the row of small windows with their shutters hanging.

Tove lifted the latch with a thumb.

It was dark. It smelled of cold wood and wool and old tobacco. She stood in the door and let her eyes learn it. Two rows of bunks, twelve a side, in tiers, with a wide aisle down the middle, and at the far end a squat stove gone red with rust. Some of the blankets were still on the beds. Someone had left a boot under the fourth. On a nail by the door hung a coat with the shoulder gone, and a lamp of the old kind, with its glass blackened.

Tove went in. Weiss went after her.

"Look," said Tove.

It was on the posts.

She had thought, at first, that the wood was marked by age. It was marked by hands. Every upright, every rail, every end-board of every bunk, to the height a man could reach lying down, was cut with names. They were cut with knives and nails and the points of spoons. They were carved deep, or scratched shallow, or burnt in with a hot wire. There were dates. There were small drawings: a boat, a tree, a woman's face with large eyes. There were tallies. There were a great many rude jokes.

She went along the aisle with her hand not quite touching.

*A. VOE.* On a lower rail, near the door. Careful, upright capitals.

*D.Q. WAS HERE AND WAS RIGHT.*

*P. OAKES. SICK OF BEANS.*

And on an end-board, at the height of the eyes, in a hand she knew from the log, small and exact, underlined: *−21.4.*

"Anselm," said Tove.

She had come up behind her. She stood at Weiss's elbow with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the rail. Her ears, long and white, stood still.

"Snored. Like a boat on a rope. You could hear it from the cookhouse. He'd a wife in Mantle and sent her every lien. That one is Dara." She nodded at the next bunk. "Cheated at cards. Was no good at it. Lost four shifts' wages in a winter to a man she'd taught." Her eyes moved. "Pettigrew couldn't sing. He did, though. Every night. E.V. there, the surveyor, he wrote the temperature to the decimal. We said he'd write the day he died to the second. He'd have liked that."

She went on.

She did not do it for effect. She said each one in the same dry flat voice, in a tone almost of complaint, as one might go through a list of tools, and each had something. *Left-handed. Owed me a pound. Wept at weddings. Could whistle with two fingers and a thumb. Terrible with children.* They came up out of the dark of the bunkhouse one at a time, the way objects come out of a drawer. They had boots. They had mothers. It was a long room.

Weiss stood and listened with her hand against a post.

She had thirty-one names in a book. She had written them with care. She had written them in a good hand, with a good pen, on good paper, over three nights, in the voice she had begun to hear. She had thought it was the thing to do.

She had thought it was knowing them.

"I have them written down," she said.

"Do you?"

"All thirty-one."

"Let's see."

She had the notebook in her coat. She held it out. Tove took it in two brown hands and opened it close to her face and read, in the half dark, with her lips a little moving. She turned the pages slowly. She went down the list.

"Hm. Neat hand."

"Yes."

"Spelled Quill wrong."

"It's from the roster."

"Roster's wrong. It's Quil. One L." Tove held the book up to the light of the nearest window, and tapped the page with a nail. "She hated the second. Said it was a waste of ink."

Weiss took the pen from her pocket. She looked at the name for a moment. *Dara Quill,* in a good clear hand, in the sixth place down.

She struck out the second *l.* She did it carefully, with a single line, as one strikes out an error in a ledger; and then she wrote the correction in the margin, small and clean. *Quil.*

She stood with the pen over it.

It was a small thing. It was a letter of the alphabet. She was aware of her own hand, steady on the page, and of a sensation behind the breastbone that she could not name and did not try to, as of something heavy being turned slightly over.

"There," said Tove Dahl. She was looking not at the book but at her face. She did not look away. "That's better."

* * *

At the third bunk from the door, on the lower tier, Tove stopped.

It was no different from the others. A grey blanket, folded; a hollow in the straw tick; a name cut into the end-board in wavering capitals, and under it, much smaller, a line that Weiss could not read from there. The old woman stood and looked at it. She did not touch it. After a time she said, in the same voice, "That one's mine."

Weiss said nothing.

"Halvard. Twenty-two years." She said it plainly. "Sang flat. Never looked at the weather. I've been up here every month since, to sit on it. I don't know why. It's a bed."

The dog had gone ahead of them.

It had been ranging the length of the room with its nose to the boards, and now it had come back and gone, without hesitation, to the third bunk from the door. It put its forepaws on the edge. It looked back at them both over its shoulder. Then it climbed in, turned round three times, and lay down in the hollow of the straw with its chin on the grey blanket, and shut its eyes.

"Pike," said Tove.

The dog did not move.

"Pike."

He opened one eye. He shut it.

"Hm," said the old woman.

She regarded him for some time. Then she turned and looked at Weiss, who was standing in the aisle of the bunkhouse with a notebook in one gloved hand and a pen in the other, and there was something in her face for a moment that was not unlike amusement, and not unlike something else.

"He's decided."

"I can't keep a dog."

"You can't send him back either. He'll sort it out." She was already walking to the door. "Thursday, with the fish. You'll want to draw your damper down. You're wasting wood."

"Mrs. Dahl."

"Tove."

"Thank you."

The old woman paused with her hand on the latch. She did not turn. Her ears, against the grey light of the door, long and pale, with their black tips, turned a very little toward the sound.

"For what?" she said.

"For — saying them."

"Somebody should," said Tove, and went out, and the door swung, and she was a small figure crossing the white yard, on skis, with an empty sled.

* * *

She stayed on in the bunkhouse until it was dark.

She sat on the end of the nearest bunk with the notebook on her knee, and a stub of pencil, and wrote by the window. She went down the list. She did it slowly, with her tongue between her teeth. Beside each of the thirty-one names she put, in a small hand, in the margin, what she had been told.

*Snored.*

*Cheated at cards.*

*Couldn't sing. Did.*

She did not have it for them all. She had it for eleven. The others she left, with a clean space beside, and told herself she would ask.

At some point there was a weight on her feet.

She looked down. The dog had come from the third bunk and lain across her boots, heavily, as a coat is laid over a chair. It was warm. It breathed with a small deep whistle, and its flank went up and down against her ankle.

"I'm not keeping you," said Weiss.

The tail moved once on the boards.

She finished the page.

# 19. epiphany

The dog had stood at the door since noon.

He had not barked. He stood with his nose to the crack at the bottom, rigid, with the one ear up and the other down and his tail held low and moving, slowly, from side to side, like a man testing a floor. When she spoke to him he did not turn. When she set a bowl beside him he looked at it as at a thing from another life.

"It's only the wind," said Weiss.

It was. It had been blowing for three days. It came down off the hill in long hard sheets and struck the hut with a flat sound, like a hand on a table, and went over the roof and along the camp and out of the valley with a howl that had a note in it, a low one, and held. The window had gone white. She could not see the bunkhouse, or the water-tower, or the tram. She had lit the lamp in the sill at noon, and she could not have said why.

She went back to the desk. She picked up the pencil and put it down.

At four the light went. At five she lit the second lamp, and fed the stove, and set the kettle on, and stood over it with her arms folded, listening to it begin. She heard the wind. She heard the stovepipe tick. She heard the dog's nails shift on the boards, and stop.

And then he barked.

Once. A short, hollow, upward bark, as at a person known. There was a pause. He barked again.

She turned round.

* * *

She took the rapier. It was in her hand before she had thought about it; she had it off the peg and out of its sheath, and she crossed the room with the point low. She did not know what she was expecting. A trapper; a lost man; a thing the wind had brought. In the weeks of the camp she had imagined, now and then, in the dark, all the ways in which someone might arrive.

The dog was frantic. He was scratching at the wood with both forepaws. A strange high noise was coming out of him, not a whine, a sort of pleading chatter, the sound of an animal that cannot say a thing in its own language.

She lifted the bar.

The wind took the door out of her hand. It came in with a roar and a wall of white, and the lamp on the sill went flat and sprang up again, and the cold struck her across the face like a board. She got her shoulder to the door. She held it a hand's width. Snow whirled in over the sill and settled on the boards, and the dog went out through the gap and was gone.

She stood with the point of the rapier raised, and looked into the dark.

There was a shape in it.

It stood at the edge of the light, where the lamp's yellow gave out, about eight yards from the door. It was white to the knees. Above that it was grey, and tall, and bulky, with a high rough head. For some seconds she thought it was not a person. It was the wrong size. It had two peaks at the top of its head, and she thought, with perfect clarity, *Beowolf,* and set her feet.

The dog had reached it. He stood against its legs. He was leaning his whole weight into the knees, and the shape put down a hand into the white and rested it on his head.

It was a hand. In a glove. The glove had no fingers. Where the fingers came out they were red.

The head turned, a little, toward the lamp.

There was a scarf. It was wound many times round the skull, thick, and crusted with ice, and under the scarf, pushing up the wool in two peaks, were two shapes with a rim of snow on each, as there is on a roof. The snow had settled and held. It lay along the top of each in a thin white cap.

She understood the ears before she understood anything else.

And then, in the lamplight, from somewhere under the wool, two small lights came on. They were round and gold and perfectly steady. They looked at her out of the storm, as a thing looks out of a wood, and did not blink.

* * *

She did not move.

It is hard to say what she did. She was holding a rapier, in a doorway, in a gale, and her hand did not shake and her feet did not shift, and every part of her had gone quiet in the way a hall goes quiet when someone steps onto a stage. She was aware of the cold on her face. She was aware of the dog. She was aware that the lamp was behind her and that her shadow went out over the snow, a long thin one, and stopped at the other's boots.

Nothing in her training covered it.

She had imagined it. She had imagined it in forty-nine ways, one for each of the days; at the stove, at the desk, on the track to the river. She had imagined sentences. She had had a good many. They were in very fine order. In none of the forty-nine had there been snow.

The figure lifted its head.

The scarf slipped. It was a small movement. It uncovered a mouth, and a jaw, and a strip of face with the colour gone out of it, grey-white and cracked at the lips; and the voice, when it came, was so hoarse and so low that the wind took half and she had to watch the lips to have the rest.

"He's dead."

That was all. It was the first thing. She had not expected a first thing.

She knew who. There was only the one *he.* It was in the voice. It had been in everything the voice had ever not said.

She stood. The point of the rapier had dropped, by some inches, and she did not know when. She heard her own heels, on the boards, shift. She thought, with a great stillness, as of someone who finds a coin on the road, *That is what she came to say.*

The wind blew.

It blew in at the door past her shoulder and across the room and the kettle began to scream on the stove, thin and high, a long way off. The dog was whining. Snow was coming in over the sill and lying down in the lamplight, in a drift, in a white tongue, across the floor.

Weiss opened her mouth.

She had forty-nine sentences. She said the one that came.

"Come in. You're letting the heat out."

* * *

The figure stood a moment longer.

Then something went across the face, quickly, so quickly that she could not have said what it was, and was gone. The head ducked. A foot came up out of the white and was put down on the sill.

Weiss stepped back.

She stood against the wall with the rapier in her hand. The other came in past her, stooping under the lintel, and the cold came with it, a clean hard radiance that smelled of ice and wool and smoke and something else, faint and sweet and unplaceable, like an orange peel in a coat. Snow slid from the shoulders onto the boards. It was tall. It was not tall at all. It stood in the middle of the floor, in the lamplight, with the scarf half off, and breathed.

# 20. arrangements

Weiss's heartbeat was not regular.

That was the first thing Blake heard, standing in the middle of the floor with the snow coming off her in sheets. It went fast and then not, with a catch in it, like a drawer that sticks. She had listened to it for forty minutes once, across a dark room, and it had been kept as a clock is kept. This was not that. It was quick and unfinished. It was going on without permission.

She did not say so. She stood where she had been put.

"I'll take your coat," said Weiss.

She had come across the room as one crosses ice. She stopped a yard off. In the lamp, with the wind shut out, she was thinner than she had been, with a face that had gone all bone and eye, and her hair was down. Blake had seen it down once, at dawn, frozen. It hung to the middle of her back, loose, and not quite clean, and it was the first thing in the world to be exactly as she had remembered it.

"May I?"

"Yes."

She stood and let it be done, one button, two, a pause at the third where the cloth had frozen to the thread; and the left hand that worked at it was in a glove.

It was black. It was wool, with the tips cut off at the first knuckle. It was a little large, and pulled up under the cuff.

Blake looked at it.

She looked at it for some seconds. The cook had told her. She had been told in a warm kitchen, over soup, and she had thought she understood, and it was nothing at all like seeing it: the stiff fingers, bare at the ends, going about their business at her collar, in the lamplight, on a hand that was not hers.

The hand stopped.

"It was cold," said Weiss.

"It's snowing."

"Yes."

"I know," said Blake.

"Yes," said Weiss. "She would have."

She saw a girl in a coachman's coat, with her hair coming down, sitting with her hands in her lap and her jaw set, and she saw, at last, that the cheekbones were not only thin. They were sharp as a thing that had been whittled.

* * *

The bunkhouse was a long dark roof with the wind on it.

She went across the yard with the lamp and the dog, who had decided without consultation to come, and a blanket over her arm. It was ten yards. It took a long time. The storm had thinned to a hard fine dry snow, driven flat, and it hissed on the lamp glass and on the dog's back, and above her the sky had cleared in places and showed, through the rents, the sharp blue-black of the cold.

The door was tied with a thong. She untied it.

Twelve beds a side, in tiers. The air stood in the room like water in a well. She went down the aisle with the lamp held up, and as it moved the dark moved back along the walls and showed her what was on them.

Names.

She stopped.

They were on the posts and the rails and the end-boards, as high as her chin and as low as her knee. Cut. Scratched. Burnt. She put out her hand and drew a thumb along the nearest, an *A* and a *V,* with a drift of dust in the grooves. Further down: *D.Q. WAS HERE AND WAS RIGHT.* She held the lamp to it. She read it slowly, in a voice that did not leave her throat.

She had a notebook in her bag. It was ruled in columns.

She stood in the cold with the lamp at the level of her face and looked at the wall, and understood that she had crossed a sea and a continent and climbed a mountain to arrive at a room that was the same as the one she had been carrying. She had not known the lists could be so long.

She chose the bunk at the stove end.

She did not light the stove. There was wood in the bin, split, and a box of matches, and she stood in front of it for some time with her hand on the lid. Then she took her hand away. She spread the blanket on the tick, and lay down on her back in her clothes, with the lamp turned low and her boots on, and the dog came up and settled across her feet.

The ceiling was close. The cold came down through the blanket. She lay and looked at the beams, and she listened to the wind, and under it, across the yard, through two walls, to a heart that had not slowed.

She thought: *I will not sleep.*

She slept almost at once.

* * *

She woke to heat.

It was full dark. The lamp was out. She lay without moving, with her eyes open on the beams, and knew in the first second that the room had changed. The air was soft. There was a ticking at the end of the building, an iron barrel creaking as it took the warmth, and a glow around its door and along the floor in a thin red line.

Someone had lit the stove.

She had heard nothing. That was impossible. She had slept through a door and a stride and a match, who had never slept through a footstep in her life, and she lay and went over it, and came to the end, and it was as plain as a wall. She had been too tired. That was all. She had not been so tired for years.

And then, outside, she heard it.

It was a thin sound. It came from the yard, a long way off, going away: a little thread of tune, hummed low, hardly more than breath. Three notes. A rest. A phrase that climbed. It went across the snow toward the other building and grew fainter and was gone, and she lay with her ears up, and listened to it all the way.

She did not move for a long time.

She was thinking of the way, in the last stack of a library, she had once held a door shut against weather. She was thinking that she had hummed that tune for months, in a country where she knew no one, with her hands busy, and had said she did not know where it was from.

She thought that it was strange for a person to hum, in the dark, in a gale, crossing a yard, when no one was awake to hear.

She did not finish the thought.

The stove ticked. The room was warm. After some time she turned on her side, with her face to the barrel, and the dog shifted, and she slept.

* * *

In the morning there was a knock.

It came at the door. It came after a sound, a little tune, hummed outside in the cold, not far off, and coming nearer, three notes, a rest, a phrase. It went on for exactly eight bars. Then the knock.

"Breakfast," said Weiss, through the wood. "There's porridge. It's not good."

"I'll come."

"I've made a list."

She opened the door.

Weiss stood on the step in the coachman's coat with a sheet of paper in her hand. The cold stood round her in the doorway, bright, shaking with light. She had pinned her hair up. She had not slept, that was plain, but she had been careful about it, and the care sat on her like a coat.

The paper was a schedule.

It was ruled in columns. *Wood. Water. Stove. Radio. Meals.* Across the top, in her best hand, were two names, and under each a series of small clean entries, with times.

Blake took it. She read it down.

Under *Blake,* for the first three days, in every row, in the same neat black ink, there was one word. It was repeated like a stitch.

*Rest.*

She looked at it for some while.

"I thought—" said Weiss.

"It's good."

"It's only for the—"

"It's good," said Blake. "Thank you."

It was not a thing she would have done herself. She noticed that. She stood on the step in the cold with the paper in her hand and she noticed it, as one notices a stone in a shoe, and she put it away. It was a kindness. She was so tired that she could have lain down on the snow.

She folded the sheet, and put it in her pocket, and went to eat.

*Rest.*

She slept for fourteen hours the first night, and eleven the second, and on the third day Weiss left a covered dish at the bunkhouse door, and hummed, and went away.

It was the porridge. It was better. She had learned that if you stirred it, it did not form a crust, and that the thing had a tendency, if left, to become a sort of grey cement. She stood in the yard with her hands in the pockets of the coachman's coat, and looked at the closed door, and went back to the hut and chopped forty-one logs.

On the afternoon of the third day the door opened.

Blake came out into the white with the dog at her knee. She stood on the step with her hand over her eyes, as a person does who has been a long time in the dark, and her ears went up and swung and settled. Weiss, at the woodpile, had stopped with the axe in the air.

"I'd like to do the water tomorrow," said Blake.

"Of course."

"I'll do it with you."

"You needn't—"

"I'd like to."

It was said quite mildly, from across the yard. Weiss lowered the axe. She stood with her hands on the haft, and the breath going up from her mouth in a plume, and found that she was nodding, and could not at once stop.

"Right," she said. "I'll revise the schedule."

She did it that night. She took a fresh sheet, ruled it, and wrote the names in two columns. Under *Blake,* in the first row, she wrote *Water,* and then looked at it, and then, because the square looked bare, put in brackets: *(with W.)* It was better. She left the rest of the column blank, as an offer.

In the morning she found that it had been filled in. Not in her hand. In a small upright pencil, with no corrections, down the whole of the sheet, in a clear pleasant diagonal: *Water. Wood. Traps. Stove (night). Radio (alt.).* She read it twice. It was a good schedule. It was fairer than hers.

She pinned it up in the office, where they would both see it, and did not alter a word.

*Wood.*

"You're fighting it."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The log." Blake was sitting on the chopping block with her elbows on her knees and the dog against her shin, watching. She had been watching for ten minutes. "You're hitting it as if it were an argument."

"It's a log."

"It knows."

Weiss set her feet again. She raised the axe, brought it down, and it went into the wood three inches off the line and stuck there, humming, and the log rolled off the block and lay on its side with a look of reproach.

Blake got up.

She did not say anything else. She took the axe, which Weiss gave her, and stood the log on its end, and looked at it for about a second. Then she lifted the head and let it fall, without effort, from the shoulder, on a line that might have been drawn there beforehand; and the two halves came apart with a sound like a book dropped on a table, and lay in the snow, white, wet, open to the heart.

"Let the weight do it," she said.

"That is not a technical instruction."

"It's the only one."

She held out the axe. It was an ordinary thing, an axe, thirty inches of ash and a pound or two of iron; and the handle was warm where she had held it. Weiss took it. She stood a log up. She raised the axe, and let it fall, and did not push.

It went through.

She looked at the two halves. They lay as the others had lain.

"Oh," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"It's quite easy."

"It's not. You've just stopped arguing."

They did it for an hour. They did not talk much. The sun came out low and hard over the shoulder of the hill and made the whole shelf of the camp glitter, every roof and pole and rail, and the split wood piled up between them in two white heaps, one on either side, going up. At some point Weiss became aware that she was humming, and Blake was not, but was moving her head slightly in the time of it, and she let it go on.

* * *

*Fish.*

She had not known that Blake could fish. She had not known that anyone could.

It was done through a hole in the ice. Blake cut it with the axe at the bend of the river where the water ran dark and slow under a shelf, a neat round hole the width of a dinner plate, and sat down beside it on a folded sack with a line wound on a stick and a scrap of rind for bait, and was perfectly still.

She was still for forty minutes. Weiss, on the bank, with a book she was not reading, watched. She watched the line and the dark water and the hands, which did not move at all. She watched the face. It had the look of a cat in a window, half shut-eyed, fixed, absorbed; it was a look of enormous, patient, purposeless attention, in which there was no one at home at all except the part that was waiting.

Then the line jerked.

The hands moved. It took perhaps two seconds. Something silver came up through the hole in a spray of water and lay on the snow, thrashing, a foot long, with a dorsal fin like a sail; and the dog went mad.

"Grayling," said Blake.

"It's enormous."

"It's average."

She killed it with a thumb behind the head. She gutted it with a knife in four movements, on the sack, and flung the offal to the dog, who caught it in the air; and Weiss, who had watched all this with the expression of a woman at an operation, found that she was leaning forward.

They cooked it on the stove, flat, in a pan, with salt. It was tender. It fell off the bone in white flakes with a taste like cold water and smoke, and she ate it with her fingers because Blake did, with her eyes closing, and a small unplanned noise in her throat.

"That is good."

"Of course it's good," said Blake.

She said it perfectly flat. She said it without looking up, over a fishbone, in a voice exactly the colour of dry toast.

Weiss stopped chewing.

It was her own line. It was the very one. She had said it over a pot of tea in a dormitory on the second floor, with her chin raised and the Dust crystal ticking in its bowl, in another life; and it came back to her across the table, intact, with a small clean edge on it, the first thing in a year that Blake had said that was meant to be a joke.

She tried not to. She put her hand up. It did no good at all.

It came out through her nose. It was the ugly laugh, the one she hated, the snort, short and loud, and she clapped a hand over her mouth and her eyes went wide above it, and across the table Blake looked up, and looked at her, and for a second her whole face was open.

Then she looked down at the fish. She bent over it. Her shoulders did something.

"Don't," said Weiss, through her fingers.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was," said Blake, to the fish. "I was going to say it was average."

* * *

*Ursa.*

They came at dusk on the sixty-third day. Two of them, old Ursa, out of the north wood where the track went in, big as a cart, hunched, with the white bone plates grown out of their backs in a rack. The dog had given the warning. He stood at the edge of the yard with his hackles up, and Blake was out of the bunkhouse before the second bark with the long blade in her hand.

Weiss had the rapier. She did not remember taking it.

They did not speak. There was nothing to say. She went left, and Blake went right, and a glyph went down in the snow under the other's feet, a white one, a quick flat disc, and Blake's boot landed on it in the middle of a leap and was thrown twenty feet into the air over the nearer beast's head, turning, with the ribbon out. The Ursa swung at nothing. A shadow was standing where she had been. It took the paw. It came apart in black smoke.

The real one came down behind it. The blade went in at the nape.

Weiss had the second. She had it in a cage of ice before it knew; she was inside the cage, the point at the join of the plates, and she did not look round for a half second, and did not need to. She knew where Blake would be. It was the oddest thing. It was a feeling like having stepped onto a stair in the dark and found it where it ought to be.

They stood in the smoke of the bodies when it was done. It rose off the snow in black threads and went out. Neither of them said anything. Blake was breathing through her mouth. Her ears were up, forward, and her eyes in the last light were bright and gold and rather wild; and Weiss, with the rapier hanging at her side and her hair come down, thought: *We have not done that in a year.*

"Left," she said.

"What?"

"I went left. You went right. We didn't say."

"No."

"It's a good arrangement."

Blake's mouth did the thing at the corner. It lasted a second. "Adequate," she said, and walked away across the yard to wipe her blade, and left Weiss standing among the dissolving bones, with her hand at her own wrist, feeling for a pulse she could find perfectly well without.

* * *

*Thursday.*

Tove came with the fish and a sack of flour and the news that the river was going to freeze harder, and she stood in the doorway of the hut and looked at the two of them for some time without comment. Blake had risen. Her ears had gone back, a little, without her permission. The old woman noted it.

"Belladonna."

"Yes."

"The cat."

"Yes."

"Hm." Tove put the flour on the desk. She was, apparently, about to say a number of things and thought better of it, as one does with a great many things. "You eat?"

"Yes."

"Good. She cooks like a hammer." She jerked her head at Weiss. "Does she let you do anything?"

"She's learning," said Blake.

Weiss, at the stove, kept her back to the room. She was stirring something. She did not turn around, and her shoulders were extremely straight, and she began, quite unconsciously, as she stirred, to hum.

Tove stopped.

She had been taking off one glove. It hung from her fingers, half off. Her head had come up, and her ears, long and white and tipped with black, both swung round together toward the stove, and held there, rigid, like the ears of an animal that has heard its name.

"Do that again."

Weiss turned. "I'm sorry?"

"What you were doing."

"I was — humming. I'm sorry. It's a habit."

"Where'd you get it?"

"I don't—"

"Do it."

She did it. She stood with the spoon in her hand, in the middle of the floor, in front of two people and a dog, and hummed eight bars. Three notes. A rest. A phrase that climbed and was held on the top a little too long, and let fall.

The old woman's face did something. It was a slow thing. It did not crumple. It simply, from the eyes outward, emptied, as a house empties when the family has gone out.

"That's a pit song," she said.

Nobody spoke. The kettle on the stove ticked.

"We sing it for the ones going down." Her voice had dropped, and was hoarse, and flat as a plank. "At the cage. And for babies. It's the same tune." She cleared her throat. She did not sing it, at first. She said it, in the voice of a woman reading a notice, in the rough short cadence of a shift-song. "*Lamp's gone low and the shift is done.*"

Blake had stopped breathing. Weiss could hear that she had.

"*The cage comes up to the morning sun. Tell the dark you're not back yet—*" The old woman's chin lifted. The pale eyes were fixed on something a long way beyond the wall of the hut, and the rest of it came out of her not as a song at all but as a sort of order, a thing said to a man at a door.

"*Come up, come up. The kettle's set.*"

The silence was complete.

On the stove, behind Weiss, the kettle began, softly, to sing.

"Who taught you that?" said Tove.

Weiss did not answer. She stood with the spoon in her hand. She had gone the colour of the snow, from the throat to the hairline, and the spoon had begun to tremble, slightly, and she looked at it, and set it down on the stove.

Her right hand was at her side. The thumb was moving across the first two fingers, slowly, back and forth.

"A woman," she said. "Someone who looked after us. When we were small." Her voice was perfectly clear. "She — had a notch. In her ear. Here." She touched the rim of her own, high on the left, with a fingertip. "I used to put my finger in it. She let me."

Tove said nothing.

"She sang it when I couldn't sleep. And then she — went. I was seven." She stopped. "I don't remember her name."

"Mm."

"I don't remember her *name.*"

It was said in the same tone, level, and it went into the room and broke there.

The old woman looked at her for a long time. She did not say that it was all right. She did not say anything of the kind. She pulled the glove back on, finger by finger, and her ears lowered, slowly, and she said, in the dry cracked voice of a person giving directions:

"Could be anyone. Half the nurses in Atlas came out of Mantle." She looked at Weiss's hands. "You'd have to ask."

"Ask whom?"

"Whoever remembers."

"My sister," said Weiss.

"Then ask her."

She picked up her sack. She went out, as she did, without ceremony, and they heard her skis go off down the track in the cold, and the dog, which had been lying under the desk, got up and went out after her as far as the edge of the yard. He stood there. Then he came back.

* * *

*Evening.*

Weiss sat at the desk with her hands flat. The lamp was lit. Neither of them had said a word for an hour, and she had been at it the whole time, trying to put her hands in some order, and could not.

Across the table, Blake mended a sock.

She did it badly. She did it with the concentration of one who is not used to it and does not intend to be defeated. The wool was grey and the needle was blunt and her lips moved slightly as she counted. After a time she began, very quietly, under her breath, to hum.

Weiss's head came up.

It was the tune. It was the three notes, the rest, the phrase that climbed. It was a little flat in the middle. It was being hummed by a woman who did not know she was doing it, to a sock.

"That's mine," said Weiss.

Blake looked up. Her ears flicked. "What?"

"The song."

"I know."

"You — know?"

"I've been humming it for a year." She put the needle down. "I got it in the library. I didn't know what it was. My mother said it was a good tune."

"It isn't mine."

"No."

"It never was." It came out low. "It was hers. And theirs. I'd — thought I'd made it up." She looked at the lamp. "I've been humming it at the table all my life, and they were—" She stopped. "Someone in my father's house sang it to me, and then he sent her away, and I have been keeping it. Without knowing whose it was."

The lamp ticked.

"It can be both," said Blake.

"How?"

"It was given to you. That's still yours." She picked up the sock. She frowned at it. "That's how it works with — most things."

Weiss looked at her.

Blake did not look up. She had gone back to the sock with a ferocious air, and her ears, which were turned a little toward the desk, had gone from their ordinary pitch to something a few degrees further forward, and a few degrees pinker, at the tips, in the lamplight.

"We could find out," she said, to the wool. "Who she was."

"Could we?"

"People can be found."

"You found me."

"I followed a dog," said Blake.

Weiss opened her mouth. She shut it. She picked up the pen from the desk, and uncapped it, and drew the log toward her; and in the column marked *Remarks,* under the date, in her best hand, she wrote:

*Ordinary day.*

She looked at it. It was true. She did not know when she had last been able to say so.

The night stove was hers. It said so on the schedule, in her own pencil: *Stove (night).* The bunkhouse was warm when she opened the door.

It had been warm every night. For twelve nights she had come in at eleven, from the last round of the traps, and found the barrel red and the kindling laid for morning and no sign of how. She had not said anything. It was a kindness. She had begun to notice, at about the sixth night, that it was also not hers to refuse.

She put that away. She sat on the bunk at the stove end with the lamp on the rail above her and the dog on her feet, and opened the book.

*Flyleaf.* In her own hand, upright, quick, a thing she had written at the counter of the shop, the day she bought it, with no particular feeling: *B. Belladonna. If found, please do not return.*

Under it, in a lighter hand, held steeper:

*Found. Will return in person. Date to be arranged. —W.S.*

She read it twice. She turned the page.

She knew the book. She knew where every one of her own notes was, and what the weather had been when she made it: a margin at page 9, a line at page 31, the long run at page 103 that she had written on the night after an examination, lying on her stomach with the lamp on her back. They were all there, small, neat, upright, a little dry. She had been, she thought, a plain person. She had thought she was being private.

The other hand began at page 14.

*Disagree.*

*Which part.*

*Attentive.*

She went down it with her thumb, the old argument, the one she had left on the hook. *Birds are not witnesses. Then who's she counting for.* And below it the short pencil line with the curl at the end, which had sat there for a year and a half, unfinished, in the margin of a page.

There was a word beside it now.

*Whoever reads it.*

She sat still.

She looked at the old stroke, and she looked at the new. She had thought, for all that time, that the hook was nothing. It was the stroke of a pencil that had not finished. She saw now where it would have gone. It was the first line of a *W.* Weiss had stopped, in a dormitory, in a lamp's light, on a night Blake could now place, and put the pencil down.

She turned her hand over on the page.

Under it, a little way down, set apart, there was another line. It was in the same pencil and it was by itself. It was extremely small.

*I signed.*

She read it. She read it again.

She did not know what it meant. She had no idea. It lay in the margin like a coin on a table, and she put her fingertip very lightly on the first word and held it there, and did not write anything under it, and did not take the finger away for a long while.

* * *

It took her four hours.

She read it as she would have read a letter. She went slowly. She went back. The stove ticked and the wind got up and went down and the dog, on her feet, gave a long sigh and shifted. She read the Ingram. She had known it by heart; she read it again.

*More snow. I am told by those who have read my letters that I complain of the weather. I do not complain. I record.*

*Atlas weather also not to be complained of. Snow in the east garden since the 3rd. The dry kind. It squeaks.*

*Counted twelve on the scree, and one that was lame. I do not count the lame one with the twelve. I count him by himself, because he is the one I will look for tomorrow.*

Under it, in her own hand, from the year before, *She counts the lame one by himself.* And under that:

*Yes. I noticed this the first time. I thought you had noticed too.*

She breathed.

*A white-out. Stayed in. Kept the stove banked low to make the wood last. It is a mistake to burn it as though the winter were short.*

*True. Day 17.*

*Wrote to my sister. Did not post it. The post comes once a month and I find I prefer the letters I have not sent; they are so much better than the ones that arrive.*

*I have four. They are good. They are all good. —Atlas.*

*Day 31: five.*

*Day 33: six.*

*Day 47: seven. They are getting worse. I think that means I am getting closer.*

She put the back of her hand against her mouth.

It was not a laugh. Or it was, a little, a kind of shake in the ribs, soundless, that she had not felt in a long time. Her ears had gone completely flat. She sat with her fist at her lip and the book on her knee and her shoulders moving, in a cold room with the names of the dead cut into every post around her, and the dog lifted his head and looked at her.

"Don't," she said.

He put his chin down again.

*The birds are changing colour. It is a slow business, a feather at a time. One would not call it a decision.*

*One might. If one were the bird.*

*Today I learned that a ptarmigan will sit tight on the snow while a fox goes past. It is not that she is brave. It is that she is white.*

*I am white. I sat tight. —Atlas, week 11.*

And, lower, a fortnight on, different pencil, in a different pressure:

*I am trying to learn to do something else with it. —Day 36.*

She did not go on for some time.

She got up. She went to the door and opened it and stood in the cold, on the step, in her shirtsleeves, with the book held against her side. It was clear. The stars were out over the roofs, hard, close, millions of them, and under them the camp lay in its blue snow with one light in it, small and yellow, at the window of the hut across the yard.

She stood there for ten minutes. Then she came in and shut the door and sat down and began again at page 100.

*[Hers, from a year back, slanted hard to the right, the letters dropping:] She lies. She says it's the weather. It isn't.*

And beneath it:

*This one is from the night of the exam. You were tired. Your hand slants right when you are tired. I have been reading your tired pages the most.*

She read that.

She read it with great care, from the beginning of the line to the end. Then she closed the book on her thumb. She held it. She looked at the stove, at the iron barrel and its red seam, and the dog's flank rising, and her own bare knees, and she thought nothing, very clearly, for a long time.

She opened it.

*Page 112. This is where I stopped reading, the night of. I kept the place.*

The waiver lay in the gutter, folded in four. It had been flattened. She could see where a thumbnail had gone down the crease, again and again, until the paper had gone soft as cloth. She unfolded it. The pencil was faint. *The undersigned consent to the waiver of Rule 1 as it applies to one (1) lamp. Low.* Four names. The last, in ink, was in a hand so exactly upright that it looked ruled.

*W. Schnee.*

*Cold enough to hear the river. Cold enough to hear oneself.*

*Day 38. Yes. It talks. I did not expect it to say anything I would want to hear.*

*Thaw. The first drip from the eaves at noon. I stood under it with my face turned up like a fool.*

*Not yet.*

*A year. I have counted two hundred and eleven birds, give or take the lame. I came north to count them and found that I had been counting days. Whether that is a better thing to have counted I cannot say. I shall go down in the morning.*

*Day 44. I have been counting days. I had thought I was counting something else.*

She sat with the book open on her knee.

On the back flyleaf, the last page, there was the final note. It was in the first hand and was dated early. It was written in ink.

*If you are reading this, either I gave it to you, or it fell into your hands, which would mean something had happened to me. I should like it to be the former. —W.S., Day 3.*

* * *

She took out the pencil.

It was the stub that had been on the table in a row, squared to the edge. It was half the length it had been. She turned it in her fingers. She did not think about it. She put the point to the flyleaf, beneath the last line, and for a time did nothing at all.

Then she wrote, small and upright, a clear dry diagonal, in her own tired hand:

*The former.*

She looked at it. It was two words. She thought that it would do.

She turned back to page 14 and set the pencil by the old hook, beside the new word, and in the margin she wrote another.

*Reading.*

Then she closed the book. She closed it softly and put it under her pillow and lay down, in her clothes, with her boots on, and the dog moved up onto her chest, and turned round, and settled, and she lay with one hand on his flank and her eyes on the beams.

She slept without meaning to. She slept through the stove, and the wind, and the dog's long sighs, and she did not wake until the morning, when the knock came, and the tune, eight bars, coming up the yard.

* * *

"It's not much of a return," said Blake.

Weiss looked at it. She looked at her face, and the hand, and the cover, and she took it, and for a moment neither of them let go. Then Blake did.

"I'll read it later," said Weiss.

"Okay."

"I— thank you."

"Breakfast," said Blake. She went past her into the cold. In the crook of her elbow, under the grey coat, the dog had come out from the bunkhouse and was leaning against her knee.

*The former.*

And she made a sound that the dog came to the door to look at.

* * *

It was a Tuesday, after supper, on the seventy-fourth day.

She was at the basin. She had left it on. She had not known that she had. It had been a dull click and a hiss in the corner for two days, a sound she had stopped hearing, like a clock; and at eleven in the morning, across the hiss, a voice cut in. It was cracked and flat and extremely loud, and it said:

"Tove here."

Weiss crossed the room in two strides. She had the handset off its hook before she knew it. "Go ahead."

"Your cat's in my kitchen."

The room went out of true.

"She's eating." The voice hissed, and steadied. "She's rude. She's been in the corner by the stove for twenty hours. She's had two bowls of soup, and the heel of a loaf. She's said nine words. Four were *thank you.*"

"Is she—"

"She's eating."

"Is she—"

"I said." There was a pause. "She isn't hurt. She's cold. She's a person that's gone a long way and stopped. Don't come."

"I won't."

"Don't send that blonde one."

"I won't."

"I'll say when. Over." And the line clicked, and hissed, and was a clock again.

Weiss stood in the middle of the floor with the handset in her fist. It had a smell: cold bakelite and oil. After a time she became aware that she was holding it against her forehead, with her eyes shut, as one holds a hand there to check for a fever. She did not know how long.

The dog had come and sat down against her knee.

"She's eating," she said.

He looked up.

"Nine words." She put the handset on its hook. It took three tries. "Four were *thank you.*"

* * *

On the fourth day she made a list.

It was what she did. She had never done anything else in her life with a feeling that had no name; she took a clean sheet and ruled it, and wrote at the top, in her best hand, with a date.

*Things I Have Decided For Blake Without Asking.*

She looked at it. It was not the title she had intended. It had come out of the pen by itself. She did not cross it out.

*1. That she should rest. (Days 1–3.) [Schedule. Written. Handed over.]*

*2. That she would not want to talk about the door.*

*3. That she would prefer the stove lit.*

*4. That she slept badly. (She does. This is not the point.)*

*5. That the river was not safe.*

*6. That she wanted to be spared lamp day.*

*7. That she wished to be spared.*

*8. That I knew what she meant by "Okay."*

*9. That I should answer when Tove asked her a question.*

*10. That I should answer when Yang did.*

*11. That her silence meant yes.*

*12. That her silence meant no.*

*13. That she was tired, when she was tired. That she was tired, when she was not.*

*14. That I knew what she wanted.*

*15. That I was the person to say.*

She read it down. It was a good list. It was exact. She could find nothing in it to correct. She saw that it went on, below, in invisible ink, for a long way, and she saw how far. There was the whole of a house in it. There was a long table, and a man at the head of it, and a woman at the foot with a glass; there were children who learned to cut meat into pieces of the same size.

*16. That it was love.*

She sat looking at that one for some time.

She did not cross it out. It was probably true. It was the worst thing on the page.

She took a second sheet.

*Things I Have Not Asked.*

*What she would like for breakfast.*

*Whether she would like the window seat.*

*Whether she would like to talk about the door.*

*Whether she would like to talk about him.*

*What the dog is to her.*

*What she wants.*

She wrote the last in a smaller hand. She looked at it. Then, below it, after a long minute, she wrote the line she had known she would write from the start.

*Whether she wants to stay.*

She put down the pen.

* * *

It was the dog who got the first of them.

She had not planned it. He was lying across the foot of the cot, and she wished to move him, and put out her hand, and stopped. It was a ridiculous thing. It was a stupid, shaming, small thing; and she sat on the edge of the bed with her hand in the air over his head and her face getting hot.

"May I?"

The dog opened one eye.

"I should like to move you," said Weiss. "You're on my feet. Would you mind?"

He considered. He sighed through his nose, with immense reluctance, and removed himself an inch and a half.

"Thank you."

It was the first time that day she had felt anything but cold. She tried it on him again, at supper. *Would you like the fish skin?* He would. *Is it all right if I open the window?* He did not care. *May I sit here?* He moved, and she sat, and he lay down again with his back against her calf and his chin on her shoe.

She was aware that this was ridiculous. She was aware that she was a girl of eighteen, with an education, in an inspector's hut in a gale, asking a sled dog if he would mind. She did it anyway. She did it until the asking stopped feeling like a wall, with her forehead up against it, and began to feel like a door with a handle: a thing one could try, and have said no to, and survive.

"Is it all right," she said, "if I'm not very good at this?"

The dog's tail moved, once, on the boards.

* * *

She went over each evening to bank the stove.

It was a quarter of a minute across the yard. She went at eleven, in the dark, with the lamp. And at the door of the bunkhouse she stopped, in the snow, with her hand on the thong, and hummed.

Three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

She did the full eight bars. She did them clearly, facing the door, in the cold, to an empty building. Then she waited. She waited for a count of five, as one does for a person to answer, and there was nothing, and she untied the thong and went in.

It was dark. It smelled of old wool and cold. She lit the lamp on the rail, and went down the aisle past the posts with their names, and knelt at the barrel and laid the fire.

On the pillow of the stove-end bunk she left a note.

She put it there each night, folded once. She wrote them in ink, in her good hand, in the office, after the bunkhouse was dark; and each time she was careful with the wording, and each time she wrote it three times before she was satisfied.

*Stove lit. If you would rather it were not, say so, and I shan't. —W.*

*Stove lit again. Same offer.*

*I should like to leave an orange on this pillow. May I? (If not, I'll eat it.)*

*I have eaten the orange.*

*I did not light the stove tonight. I wasn't sure. It is cold. I will light it if you would like to find it warm. Leave word.*

She lay awake that night in the cot in the second room with the dog on her feet and listened to the wind on the roof, and at four she got up in the dark, and put on her boots and crossed the yard in her nightdress and the coachman's coat, and lit it.

She stood on the step of the bunkhouse afterward, in the cold, breathing hard.

*That was not asking,* she thought. *That was deciding with a note.*

She went back in. She stood a long while at the washstand with her hands in the basin. Then she took a clean sheet and wrote the last.

*I lit the stove. I said I wouldn't, and then I did. I'm sorry. I find that I would rather you came back to a warm room than a clean principle, and I'm aware that this is exactly the kind of thing I'm trying not to do. I am doing it anyway. You may tell me off.*

She looked at it. It was not good. It was too long, and it rambled.

She left it as it was.

* * *

She had expected to be angry.

She had it ready. It was in a drawer, a clean bright cold thing, the way she had had it ready once in a dormitory at five in the morning. She took it out and looked at it. It would not come. It lay in her hand like a thing from a shop.

What came instead was a long grey quiet.

It was like the quiet of the camp. She knew it now. She had thought, when she came, that it was the quiet of a place that had been left; it was not. It was the quiet of a place that was waiting. She sat in the chair with one arm, with the lamp out and the dog on her feet and the stove ticking, and found that in the quiet she could see all the way to the bottom of what she had done.

It was not that she had been cruel. She would have known what to do with cruelty. It was that she had been so careful. She had been so careful, for so long, to be kind in the only way she had been shown, and the only way she had been shown was a hand over a mouth.

She did not know who she was without it.

She sat in the dark and thought about that, quite calmly, with the whole of her attention, as she had once thought about a problem in a difficult score. It was entirely possible, she thought, that she was going to find out.

* * *

On the seventh night she wrote one more, short and steady, and folded it in four, and laid it on top of the others.

On the eighth night she did not leave a note.

She had nothing more to say. She had run through the supply. She laid the fire and lit it, and stood up from the barrel, and wiped her hands on her skirt, and it was only as she turned to go that she saw that the pillow of the stove-end bunk was not as she had left it.

It had a hollow in the middle, the shape of a head. The blanket had been drawn up and folded back. And the notes were gone: all seven, the whole small stack, which she had left squared on the tick. She looked on the floor. She looked under the bunk. The dog, who had come with her, stood at the end of it with his nose in the straw, shuddering all over, making a high thin sound.

Weiss stood and did not move.

There was a smell in the room. It was faint. It was the smell of a cold coat, and of woodsmoke, and of a long way off, and under it something like salt.

She looked at the bunk for a long time. She did not call out. She did not go to the door. Then she did the only thing she could think of that was neither a command nor a flinch: she put the lamp down on the rail, carefully, where the light would fall, and went out, and shut the door softly behind her.

She stood on the step in the snow.

The sky was perfectly clear. The stars stood hard and close over the roofs, and the whole camp lay under them in a blue stillness, every pole and rail and chimney drawn in fine black, as on a plate. At the edge of the yard, by the woodpile, thirty yards off, there was a shape that she had been taking, for the last minute, for a stack of logs.

It was not a stack of logs.

It was grey. It stood quite upright. It had two points at the top of its head, and it was not moving, and it was looking at her.

She did not go toward it. She had learnt that, at least.

She hummed.

It was for the form of the thing. She did the eight bars, clearly, across the thirty yards, in the dark, with her breath going up. Her voice shook on the climb. She held the top a little too long, as one does, and let it fall.

She waited.

"May I come over?" said Weiss.

The yard was quiet. A long way off on the hill something small went through the snow and stopped. The figure at the woodpile did not move at all, and she stood, and counted five, and then, because she was not finished, counted five again.

"Yes," said Blake.

It was hoarse. It was low. It crossed the snow and arrived, whole.

The house was two hours down the valley, and she walked it in the dark.

She did not run. She had thought she would. She had thought that when the shadow was standing and the door was open she would go out of the camp as a stone goes out of a sling; but she found, on the track, in the grey before dawn, that her legs did not want to. They went in a long, steady, mechanical stride, one and one and one, and she let them, and listened to the snow squeak under her boots, and tried not to hear what it said.

She had made the shadow at four. She had made it with care. She had stood in the dark of the bunkhouse with the empty kettle in her fist and put into the thing everything she knew about herself: the set of the shoulders, the length of the pause, the angle of the neck when she was listening and did not wish to be seen listening. It had been good work. She had walked across the yard and put it in the hut, in front of the stove, and set the kettle in its hand.

She had written the note in the book.

She went over that part again and again, on the track. She could not stop it. The pencil stub in the light of the stove. The page. She had been calm. She had thought of the words and found the words and set them down in a good dry hand, and had stood back and thought, with a feeling that she recognised in the dark of her own chest as *satisfaction: it is correct. Notice given.*

*Rule 12: notice given. —B.*

It was the cruellest sentence she had ever written. She had been proud of the grammar.

* * *

The house stood by itself at the head of a small side valley, a low sod-roofed thing half sunk in the snow, with a stovepipe and a row of drying fish on a pole and a team of dogs in the lee of the wall who got up and shook and said what they thought of her.

The door opened before she reached it.

Tove stood in it in a man's undershirt and a pair of enormous boots, with her ears flat to her head against the cold and a lamp in her hand. She looked at Blake for a good while. She looked at her feet, which were wet through, and at her face, and at the place on her left where, until that morning, there had been a dog.

"Hm," she said.

She stood aside.

It was a single room. There was a stove, a table, a bed in the corner under a quilt, a shelf with a row of brass pit lamps, thirty-one of them and a few over, each wiped and standing in its place. There was a radio in a box. There was a smell of dogs and smoke and cold fish and something cooking. She sat down where she was put, by the stove, and her boots were taken off by someone else, and a bowl was put in her hands.

She ate. It was soup. It had barley in it.

"Thank you," she said.

Tove said nothing. She went about her work, which was the mending of a harness, at the table, with an awl and a length of waxed thread. She did not ask a question. She did not look round. The dogs outside scuffled and settled. Blake sat in front of the stove in the warm and ate the soup, and then the loaf, and then another bowl, and said *thank you,* twice more, in a voice that belonged to a person on the other side of a pane of glass.

She slept on the floor. She slept for twenty hours.

* * *

The radio spoke on the second day.

She was in the corner, in the quilt, with her back to the wall. Tove was at the box with her hand on a knob, and she said "Tove here," in her flat dry voice, to the air, and there was a hiss; and she said a thing about a cat in her kitchen. It took twenty seconds.

And across the hiss, from a long way off, small and cracked and tinny, came a voice.

*"Is she—"*

Blake's ears came up.

She had known that she would hear it. She had not known that it would go through her like that. It was a thin quick voice, with all the starch gone out of it, and what was left was not a thing she had heard in it before: it was the voice of a person who has been standing a long time in a cold place and has just been told that something might be all right.

*"Is she—"*

"She's eating," said Tove.

*"I won't."*

The line clicked. The box went on hissing. Tove hung up the handset on its hook, and sat down at the table, and took up the awl, and did not turn round.

"Don't," said Blake.

"Didn't say anything."

"I know."

"Hm," said the old woman, and drove the awl through the leather.

* * *

On the fourth night she told her.

She told it in the dark, at the table, with the lamp turned low and the harness lying in her lap, in the same voice in which she said everything else. Blake had not asked. She was by the stove with her knees up, and Tove, who had not said a thing for an hour, began without any preface.

"My Halvard had a cough."

The stove ticked.

"It was the fortieth. He'd had it a week. It was a wet one, down in the chest; he'd lie awake with it and I'd hear him. I said stay home. He said he would." She turned the harness over. "It was a day's pay. We were behind. I'd a bill at the store. I said, *go on.* I said it twice."

She did not look up.

"He went. He was always a man who went. I sent him." The awl moved in the leather, in, out. "I haven't said that to anyone. Not to the girl. I thought I'd best say it to somebody who wasn't going to do anything about it."

"I'm sorry."

"That's not what I said."

"No."

"I'm not telling you so you'll feel better." Tove's voice did not change. "I'm telling you so you know it isn't special."

Blake looked at her.

"What you've got. What you're carrying, in the way you're carrying it, like a cat with a bird. It's not special. I've carried the same one for a year and a half. It weighs the same. I get up, and I feed the dogs." She bit off the thread. "There's a lot of us. That's all I've to say. It's not a comfort."

"No."

"It's a fact."

She laid down the harness. She sat for a minute with her hands on her knees, a short broad old woman in the lamplight, with her ears laid back, and the brass lamps behind her on the shelf, each with its small wick of shadow. Then she got up, creaking, and went to the bed.

"You want to go back," she said, over her shoulder.

"I don't know."

"You've a face like a dog at a door." She lay down, with a grunt, and pulled the quilt over her ears. "It's not locked."

* * *

She lay awake.

She had the floor by the stove. She had the quilt, which smelled of wool and age, and the red seam of light round the grille, and the sounds of the house: the old woman's long snore, the creak of the roof, the dogs in the wall. Outside the wind went down the valley and she heard it go.

She tried to think, and found she had been thinking for some time.

She had said *Adam.* It was not true. It was true in the way that a shadow is true; it was the right size, and the right shape, and it fell in the right place, and it was not him. She turned it over. What was true was that she had felt it. She had stood in the hut, in the lamp, with the paper in her hand, and every hair on her body had risen, because a person she loved was doing something with great gentleness for her own good, and the part of her that had been trained for six years had said: *there. That. It is starting.*

But it was not the same. She lay and looked at it. The difference was so plain that she was ashamed she had needed three days to find it.

Adam had never once been seen.

He had come from behind. He had come in the dark, and put his hand on her neck, and she had learned to know him by the silence; and in six years he had never stood in a doorway and been looked at. He had never written *rest* on a sheet of paper in a good hand and put it in front of her, in the light, to be read. He had never left it in plain view that she could say no.

Weiss had hummed.

Every night, across a yard, in a gale. She had walked toward a door in the dark and made a noise, so that it would be known; she had lit a stove and gone away singing, so that it would be known; and she had done it badly, and wrongly, and over the wrong things, and with a wall of careful kindness that had no gaps in it; but every time she had come, she had come from the front.

*I'm coming. I'm here. You may turn round.*

She had not heard it. She had been listening, for so long, for the lock.

She put her arm across her face.

She lay under the quilt on the floor of an old woman's house and let it come, which it did slowly, in a long cold silent heave, as if from a distance, and ran into her ears. The dog on her feet was not there. She missed him with a vividness that was almost comic. She lay and shook, without noise, for a long time, until the stove had gone down to a thread, and the old woman's snore had altered and become a different snore, and she was too tired to go on.

She thought: *Rule 12.*

She thought: *In person.*

* * *

On the eighth morning Tove said: "Go on."

She said it at the table, with her back to the room, to a pot. She did not turn round. There was a sack on the bench by the door, packed; it had not been there the night before. In it were a loaf, a twist of fish, a tin of the lamp oil, and two long bone-handled needles.

"For the sock," said Tove. "You do it badly."

"Thank you."

"That's four."

"Is it—"

"Door's not locked," said the old woman. She picked up a ladle and put it down. "It's not my door. But I've said."

The dogs came to the wall as she went. They stood in a row with their heads on one side. Tove did not come out. She was at the window, a broad shape behind the frost, and she lifted one hand, once, with the palm flat, and put it down.

* * *

She followed her own tracks.

They were nearly gone. A week of wind had laid a skin over them, and the print of her boots was only a soft series of dents, at intervals, going up the valley, which she could see better by looking a little to one side of them. She walked in them. She put each foot where she had put it before. It was a way of not having to choose.

She reached the camp at dusk.

It lay as it had always lain, in its blue snow, under the first stars: the long roofs, the stone, the dead tram, the little squared house at the head with its door to the wind. There was a light in it. She stopped on the track below the lip of the shelf, where she could see and not be seen, and stood for what might have been a quarter of an hour, with her hands in her pockets.

She did not go to the hut.

She would think about that, afterward, with something like despair. She went to the bunkhouse, as a cat goes to the place it knows, round the back, along the wall, in the dark. The thong was on its nail. She lifted it. The room was warm.

It had been lit.

She stood on the boards. There was the barrel with its red seam. There was the smell of wool, and of smoke. There was a lamp, turned low, on the rail of the end bunk; and on the pillow, folded once, square to the edge of the tick, there was a small stack of paper.

She went down the aisle.

She took up the stack and turned it over and began at the beginning.

*Stove lit. If you would rather it were not, say so, and I shan't. —W.*

She read it twice. She had never been able to help that. She read the second. *Stove lit again. Same offer.* The third. *I should like to leave an orange on this pillow. May I? (If not, I'll eat it.)*

Her hand had begun to shake. She sat down on the edge of the bunk.

*I have eaten the orange.*

It was too much. She put her fist against her mouth and made a sound into it which was not a laugh, though it had the shape of one; she bent over her knees, and the paper crackled against her lip. The fifth. *I did not light the stove tonight. I wasn't sure. It is cold. I will light it if you would like to find it warm. Leave word.* The sixth.

*I lit the stove. I said I wouldn't, and then I did. I'm sorry. I find that I would rather you came back to a warm room than a clean principle, and I'm aware that this is exactly the kind of thing I'm trying not to do. I am doing it anyway. You may tell me off.*

She read it three times.

The seventh was short. It had been written last, she thought, by the look of the ink, and in a different mood, steady. It was folded in four, and had lain on top of all the rest.

*I do not know whether you are coming back. I would like you to. This is not a request. It is a fact that I thought you ought to have. —W.*

She sat with it in her two hands.

The stove ticked. The lamp on the rail leaned in a draught. Outside, in the yard, in the black, the snow went on falling off the roof in soft slow slides. She did not know how long. At some point she took the seven papers and squared them in her palm, as she had seen it done, and folded them together, and put them inside her shirt, flat, against the skin.

She lay down on the pillow. It was cold. Then it was not.

She woke in the dark.

It was past ten. The lamp had burned low. She had the sense, as one has of a change in the pressure, that something had altered; and she lay, and listened, and heard across the yard a door open.

She was up. She was out of the bunkhouse by the back and along the wall before she had decided to be. She went on her toes, in the black, like a thing on a ledge. She did not go to the hut. She went to the woodpile, thirty yards off, and stood behind the stack, with her cheek to a frozen log, and watched a figure come across the snow with a lamp.

It was Weiss. She knew the stride.

It came to the bunkhouse door and stopped. It stood still, with the lamp held low, facing the wood; and then, softly, into the planks, it hummed.

Three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

She did it all. She did every bar. Blake stood at the woodpile with her ears flat against the frost and her heart going in her throat and watched her wait, with her head a little bent, for the count of five, for somebody to say, from the other side of a door that did not have anybody behind it, that she might come in.

The figure went in.

She was gone for some minutes. A glow moved behind the shutters. Blake stood, and she thought: *Go now. Go in, and say.* She thought: *She will find the pillow.* She thought it very clearly. She thought: *She will find the pillow and she will know, and I will be standing here like a post.*

The door opened. The figure came out.

It put the lamp on the rail inside, carefully, and shut the door. It stood on the step. It looked about, up at the sky, and across the camp, at the roofs, the poles, the white; and Blake saw the head turn, and stop, and the pale blur of a face lift toward the woodpile, and stay.

She had not moved. She had not made a sound.

The figure did not come toward her.

She had expected that it would. She had braced for it, for a rush, a hand, a voice calling her name across the snow in a tone of command. It did not come. The figure stood still on the step, thirty yards off, in the starlight, with its hands at its sides. And then she heard it, thin, clear, a little unsteady on the climb, going out over the snow toward her with great care: the tune, the whole of it, the eight bars.

It was not for the door. It was for her.

She stood with her hand on the log.

"May I come over?" said Weiss.

She counted five. She counted another five. She did it with the exactness of someone doing a sum. She was not asking twice because she had not heard. She was giving it time to be a real question.

There was a large silence in the world.

"Yes," said Blake.

* * *

She came across the snow.

She did it at a walk, with the lamp left behind, and stopped a yard off. A clean, scrupulous yard. She stood with her hands at her sides, in the coachman's coat, with her hair coming down and her face as white as paper, and Blake saw that she had been crying at some time in the last week, steadily, and had stopped, and that nobody had seen.

The dog came out of the dark in a rush and hit her knees.

She went down on one of them. He was shaking all over. He had his whole length against her and his nose in her neck and he was making a high continuous noise, and she put both arms round him and her face in the ruff and held on. She could feel Weiss above her, not moving.

"You gave notice," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"To a shadow."

"Yes."

"I stood in a room and talked to a coat." Her voice was perfectly steady. "For eleven minutes. I knew, about halfway, that it wasn't you. I finished. I wanted it said."

"I know."

"You don't."

"No." Blake lifted her head. "I don't."

"I am not finished being angry."

It went through her like a bell.

She knelt in the snow with the dog against her chest, looking up. It was the same voice. It was the voice of a girl at a door at five in the morning, in a coat black with frost, with her hair down, saying a thing to a bag on a bed. She had thought she would never hear it again, and she heard it, and it went right through to the floor of her.

"Okay," she said.

"I would like you to be here while I finish."

"I'm here."

"Good."

Weiss looked down at her. She was shaking, a very little, all over. She said: "It isn't all right."

"No."

"I'd like it to be said. It's *not.* You went. You stood in my kitchen, in my— and you *made* a— and then you wrote a *rule* in my book." The voice cracked and she let it. "That was cruel. That was the single most cruel thing I have ever seen done by a person with that much care. I have been—"

She stopped. She breathed.

"I'm not finished," she said, more quietly.

"Okay."

"You said Adam."

"Yes."

"It wasn't fair."

"It was half fair."

"It wasn't—"

"I said it because it was the worst thing I had." Blake did not look away. "I wanted it to hurt. I wanted you to stop. It worked, and I'm sorry, and it was half. It felt like the same thing. It's not the same thing." She swallowed. "He never came from the front."

Weiss said nothing.

"You do. Every night. I didn't hear it."

"It can be both," said Weiss, after a time. Her voice had gone odd. "Half fair."

"Yes."

"I shall have to think about the half."

"Okay."

"I'm thinking about it." She pressed her lips together. "I'll go on."

She stood in the snow with the starlight on her face, and went on. It did not take as long as Blake had feared. It was a list. It went down the way her lists did, point by point, flat, precise; there was the stove, and the sleeping, and the eleven minutes, and a good deal about the word *notice;* there was a pause, in the middle, in which something visible happened to her mouth. It was water. It was running off every roof, from the long eaves of the bunkhouse and the sheds, from the dead tram, from the gutters of the mess hall, all in different notes. It dripped in the lean-to. It ran under the snow with a sound like a stream under a floor. Down on the river the ice made a long groaning crack, a sound like a door opened in a large house, and a minute later another, farther off, and the dog got up and went to the window and stood with his nose to the glass.

The hut leaked.

They had patched the north side and not the south. By eight there were four buckets on the boards, and a basin, and a saucepan, and each received a drop at a different interval, so that the office ticked like a room full of clocks. *Plink. Plink-plink. Plink.* Weiss walked among them in her stocking feet with a cloth and a look of tight, ferocious unhappiness, and at ten she announced that it was no good, she could not sleep in it, and went into the second room and shut the door.

Blake lay on her pallet by the stove.

She lay on her back with the blanket to her chin and listened. She had become, over the weeks, a connoisseur of it. There was the saucepan, which was high, and sharp, and in a hurry. There was the biggest bucket, in the corner, a deep slow note, once every eight or nine seconds, as of a large animal drawing breath. She counted. She got to three hundred and eleven.

The door of the second room was ajar.

She could see the line of it, in the dark: a long black slit, an inch wide, with the faintest grey in it from the window beyond. She could hear what was in there. A cot creaking. A dog's tail on the boards. A person lying on her side, in the dark, with her eyes open, doing the same arithmetic.

She thought about it for some time.

She thought of it as she thought of a door she had not been through. She did the sum. She turned it round. She said to herself, with some severity, that there was no hurry; that it had been a year, and a winter, and a mountain, and that it would still be there at breakfast; that she was a person who had been taught, in a hard school, that to want a thing was to hand a man a knife. She lay and looked at the ceiling.

*Ask me tomorrow,* she had said.

Well. It was tomorrow.

She got up.

* * *

She stood at the door. She did not push it.

The saucepan went, behind her, *plink,* in the dark. She had one hand on the frame. She was barefoot, in her shirt, with her ears up, and her heart doing a thing she knew well and had never in her life been glad of; and she did what she had learned, in a camp in the north, to do in front of a door.

She hummed.

It came out thin. It was not as good as Weiss's. It was flat in the middle, and she held the top too long, and the last note cracked like a boy's. She did all eight bars. She did them to the black slit, and stood, and heard, on the other side, a silence of such completeness that it had a shape.

"You're humming," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"That's mine."

"It was never yours."

A pause.

"No," said Weiss. "I know." Her voice was quite steady, and hoarse, and a little too low. "It's rather good. You hold the top."

"It's the way it's written."

"So it is."

The dog's tail moved on the boards.

"May I come in?"

"Yes."

* * *

The room was small.

It had a cot and a washstand and a shelf, and a window with no curtain, and through the window the thaw-sky, which had cleared; it was spread with a thousand stars and a late thin moon. The light lay in a long grey bar across the blanket. Weiss was sitting up against the wall with her knees under the quilt and the dog across her feet, in a man's flannel shirt that came to her thighs, with her cropped hair standing out on one side like a feather. She looked, in the dark, extremely young.

Blake shut the door behind her. She left it an inch open.

She stood at the foot of the cot.

"I want to ask something," she said. "I'm going to get it wrong."

"I'm told that's permitted."

"By whom?"

"By me." A small breath, not quite a laugh. "I've found it's the only way I get anything done."

She came round the side of the cot. She stopped where the bar of moonlight began. She had not known her hands would be so cold. She put them together in front of her, fingers laced, as she had seen it done by a child at a recitation, and she looked at the floor, and then, because that was cowardice, at the face.

Weiss was looking at her.

She was looking with her whole attention, in the way she had; with her chin a little lifted, and her lips closed, and her eyes wide and bright and still. She had the look of a person standing at a gate. The scar was a pale line over the left. It made the eye look half-lidded, and not at all: it made it look like it was listening.

"May I kiss you?" said Blake.

It was so much plainer than she had thought. It came out in an ordinary voice. It lay in the dark between them, small, intact, with no ornament, like a stone on a table; and the saucepan dripped, behind her, *plink,* and a long way off on the river the ice cracked and the sound went away down the valley and was lost.

"Yes," said Weiss.

She had said it at once. It had come before the end of the question, fast, breathless, a little hoarse. She heard it as it left her, and put her hand to her mouth.

"I'm sorry. I meant to wait. I had a — it was supposed to be a considered—"

"Weiss."

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"Yes," said Weiss, and took her hand away.

* * *

She sat down on the edge of the cot.

She did it slowly. She was not quite sure of her legs. The mattress dipped, and the dog moved, grumbling, and was gone to the end. She was a foot away. She could smell the quilt: wool, and woodsmoke, and ink, and the peel of an orange. She could hear Weiss breathe. It was quick and light and a little uneven, and it had a catch at the top of each one, like a drawer that wasn't quite shut.

"Tell me what to do," said Weiss.

It was almost a whisper. She was holding herself so rigidly that the quilt shook.

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Stay there. Just—" Blake swallowed. "Stay where I can see you."

"Yes."

"If I stop—"

"Then you stop."

"I might not be able to say."

"Then I'll know." A pause. "I'm good at watching."

It was true. It was quite true. She had known that for a long time, a year, since a lamp in a library and a table with a plate, and she sat looking at the face a foot from her own and understood that she had been afraid of this since the beginning, since the first night, since a sheet of rules in ink; and that the fear was no longer the principal thing in the room.

She lifted her hand.

She did it slowly, in front of her, in the grey light. She held it up where it could be seen. The fingers were not steady. She brought it to the side of Weiss's face, and stopped, a half inch off the cheek, with the palm open, as one holds a hand over a flame to see how near it can come.

Weiss did not move.

Her eyes had closed. Her face tipped, a degree, into the hand. Then, slowly, with the air of a person lowering something heavy onto a table, she set her own palm over the back of Blake's, and drew it in, the last half inch, against her cheek.

It was warm. It was warmer than the quilt. The skin was dry and very smooth, and it fit.

She leaned forward.

She had thought it would be a decision. It was nothing like. It was the closing of a distance, a foot, then six inches, then three, as the last of the light goes out of a room; and her ears had gone entirely forward, and her breath was coming through her mouth, and she shut her eyes.

Weiss's mouth was cold.

It was dry, and cold, and for a second it did not move. Then it did. It opened, slightly, on a breath, and was warm; and a hand came up and rested against her jaw, not holding, only touching, with the thumb under the ear, so lightly that she felt the pulse in it. It lasted perhaps three seconds.

She drew back.

Her heart was going like a hammer. She sat with her eyes still shut, and her hand where it had been, against a cheek, and for a moment she did not know whose breath it was. It came in short pulls. Her ears had gone flat and then forward and then, absurdly, both ways at once.

"Blake."

"I'm here."

"Are you—"

"Yes. Wait." She breathed. She opened her eyes. Weiss's face was a hand's width off, in the moonlight, pale, and wet along one cheek, and looking at her with a terrible exactness. "I'm all right."

"You said not to say that."

"I know. I'm—" She stopped. "I'm okay."

Something shook in Weiss's face. It began at the mouth and spread, and she bit her lip, hard, and could not stop it; and a sound came out of her that was small and helpless and entirely without dignity. She put her hand over her mouth again.

"That was," said Weiss, through her fingers, in a high strained voice, "adequate."

Blake stared at her.

She had a moment of pure white blankness. And then it rose up in her from somewhere very deep, from the soles of her feet, from the whole length of the past year, and came out of her mouth in a noise she had never made before in her life: a short, cracked, helpless bark of a laugh. She bent over her knees. She laughed until her shoulders shook.

"High praise," she said, when she could.

"It was meant as such."

"I know."

"I'm sorry. I couldn't—"

"Don't be."

"I couldn't *not.*" Weiss was laughing too, silently, with her eyes shut, with the tears running down into the corners of her mouth. She wiped them, impatiently, with the flat of her wrist. "It's the shock."

"Yes."

"It was extremely good."

"Thank you."

"I should like to do it again, at some convenient time."

"Okay."

"Not at once."

"No."

"Possibly in a minute."

"I'll tell you," said Blake, "when I'm ready."

"I know you will."

They sat.

The room was full of stars. The dog, at the end of the cot, had put his head on Blake's foot with a sigh of immense contentment, and shut his eyes. In the next room the saucepan dripped, and the bucket answered it, a note lower. Weiss reached out her hand, palm up, in the grey light, and left it lying on the quilt between them, a foot from Blake's knee, open.

She looked at it.

Then she put her own into it, and the fingers closed.

* * *

She did not sleep, at first. She lay on her side, in the cot, in the narrow space left against the wall, with Weiss facing her in the dark, a foot away, under the quilt. Neither of them had suggested it. It had happened by degrees. There was a hand in hers, which had not let go. There was a leg, somewhere, not touching. The dog lay along their feet like a bolster.

"I can't sleep with someone behind me," she said.

"Yes. I know."

"I'd like to face you."

"Then you will."

"You'll have to — you'll be where I can see."

"I'm told I'm worth looking at," said Weiss, extremely dry, "in some lights."

She almost laughed again. She did not. She lay and looked, in the dark, at the small pale shape of a face, and the line of the scar, and the lashes, and the white of the throat; and she thought: *it has been a long way.* It was not a sentence she was used to. It sat in her chest, warm and heavy and entirely unfamiliar, like a cat that has chosen a lap.

"Staying?" said Weiss, very low.

It was past midnight.

She had never in her life been asked it at that hour.

"Today," said Blake. "And tomorrow. Ask me."

She slept.

* * *

She woke in the grey.

It was early. The window had gone the colour of pearl. There was a weight across her ribs, warm and light, and for the space of a heartbeat she did not know it; and then she did, and every muscle in her body locked.

It was an arm. It was Weiss's, flung over her waist in sleep, the hand open on the quilt, the fingers a little curled. It had not moved. Its owner lay close against her, with her face half in the pillow and her mouth slack, breathing slowly through the nose, and her cropped hair in her eyes; and her whole body was turned to Blake's as a plant turns.

It was not a hand on the neck. It was not behind her. It was in front, where she could see, and it was warm, and it was a girl asleep.

Her heart did not know that.

It went on, racing, in the dark, for the old reason. She lay rigid. She could feel the sweat starting along her spine. She breathed in, slow, and out, slow, as she had been taught on a strand, and she told herself the facts, one at a time, in order, in the voice of a clerk: *this is Weiss. This is a room. The door is open an inch. I can get up.*

She got up.

She did it by degrees. She lifted the arm and laid it down on the quilt, gently, and it curled a little into the warm place she had left. She stood in the grey on the cold boards, in her shirt. She was shaking. She picked up her boots, and stood with them in her hand.

She thought: *Go.*

She stood a long time.

She thought: *Rule twelve.*

She turned, and put the boots down, and crossed to the cot, and knelt beside it. She put her hand on the quilt, near the shoulder, and said in a low clear voice:

"Weiss."

The eyes opened. They were very blue. For a second they were empty; and then they took her in, the face, the stance, the boots by the door, and something went across them, quick as a bird, and was gone.

"Yes," said Weiss.

"I need to walk."

"Yes."

"I'm telling you before." She swallowed. "I'm not leaving. I just— it was a lot. I'd like to be out. I'll be back."

"When?"

"An hour."

"Right." Weiss pushed up on an elbow. Her hair stood out at the side. Her voice was rough with sleep, and quite level, and her hand had come out of the quilt and lay on the blanket between them, open, an inch from Blake's, not reaching. "Take your time."

"I'm coming back."

"I know."

"I'm saying it in person."

"I heard it." The corner of her mouth moved. "It was very well done. I should like it noted."

"Noted."

"Go on, then."

She went.

She walked down to the river in the cold. The light was coming up gold along the top of the hill and the whole valley was running with water, dripping, ticking, chuckling in a thousand small voices; and the ice at the bend had gone dark and rotten and had opened, in the middle, a long black seam, through which the river was visible, moving. She stood on the bank with her hands in her pockets and breathed.

She stood there for fifty minutes.

At the end of it she cut a hole in the shelf of ice that was left, and sat on a sack, and put a line down. It took her seven minutes. It was a fat one, a grayling, silver, with a sail on its back, and it came up out of the black water in a spray of light.

She carried it back by the gill.

The dog saw her first. He came out at a run and hit her knees. She stood in the yard with the fish in one hand and her other on his head, and the sun on her face, and looked at the hut. The door was open. Weiss stood in it. She had the quilt round her shoulders like a cloak, and her hair on end, and a cup in each hand, and she was not pretending to be doing anything else.

"Fifty-three minutes," said Weiss.

"You counted."

"Of course I counted. I'm a Schnee." She held out a cup. "It's the way you make it."

Blake took it.

It was hot. It was strong, black, a little burnt. She drank, and over the rim of the cup, in the clear early light, with a grayling hanging from her fist and a dog against her shin, she looked at the girl in the doorway and thought, with perfect calm: *Oh.* And then, as one lays down a thing that has been carried a long time: *There you are.*

The waiting was long.

It was a spring in the way that it is a spring in the north, which is to say it did nothing for days, and then rained for one night and was in its sixth week. The roofs dripped. The river went brown and loud. The ice at the bend broke up in great grey plates and went off down the valley with a noise like furniture. Each morning she woke to a new sound, and each night the stars were closer.

She learned to say it.

It was a thing like the tune. You went through it every time, from the bottom, and arrived at the top, and held it a little too long.

She stood a good deal at the window.

She watched the track. Blake, at the table behind her, mended a boot. She did it very badly. Neither of them said anything about the track.

She stood still. A door, in summer, at noon. The sun through it. A great flat sheet of white, a thing she had run at, as a child runs, with arms out, thinking that it was air.

*She held your head in her apron.*

Her hand had begun to shake. She put the letter down on the desk, flat, with her palm on it, to keep it from fluttering.

"Weiss."

"I'm all right," she said. "No. I'm — it's not that." Her voice was perfectly level. "It's that I've got a name."

She turned her head. Blake was beside her, a foot off. She had not moved to touch her. She stood with her hands at her sides and her ears forward, and her face, in the clear light, was open all the way down.

"Maren," said Weiss.

She said it aloud.

It was easy. It came out of her mouth like any word. It was a short soft name, with a round vowel in the middle, and it went into the air of the hut, among the stove and the cups and the sound of the water, and did not break.

"Maren Holt."

"Maren," said Blake.

The dog, at the door, lifted his head.

"Maren," said Weiss, a third time, to the kettle. And it came to her, in a long cold tremor that went the whole length of her, that it was a thing one could do. One could say the name. It could be said in the open, in a plain voice, in an ordinary room, to a person who was listening, and the person would say it back.

She sat down in the chair with one arm.

She took the marbled notebook from the shelf, the one with the thirty-one, and turned to the last page, the clean one. She uncapped the pen. She wrote in her best hand, upright, round-vowelled, unhurried, as one writes a name on a door:

*Maren Holt.*

She looked at it. She did not draw a line under it, or add a date. After a moment, under it, in a smaller hand, she wrote the only other word the page wanted.

*Ask.*

"Lamp Street," said Blake.

"Tove will know."

"Tove will know."

"Tomorrow," said Weiss.

"Tomorrow." Blake put her hand on the back of the chair. She did it slowly, in plain sight, with a small deliberate pressure; it lay along the wood an inch from Weiss's shoulder. "May I?"

"Yes."

The hand came down.

It was warm. It was heavy, a little, and rough at the knuckles, and entirely steady. It rested on her shoulder, through the wool, for a long time, and neither of them said anything at all.
