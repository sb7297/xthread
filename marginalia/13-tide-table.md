# 13. tide table

It was pinned to the kitchen wall by the stove, with four nails, and it ran the house.

A printed sheet from the harbour office, folded twice in a pocket for so long that the creases had gone soft and white. Her mother had gone over it in red pencil. *HIGH 5:51. LOW 12:08. HIGH 6:30.* Under each she had written what it meant: *boats out. Nets in. Don't swim by the rocks.* There was a column for the moon. There was a margin where, in a small cramped hand, she had added the dates of births, and one or two of deaths, and a recipe for lime pickle that had nothing to do with anything.

Kali read it in the mornings the way other people read the news. She stood with her tea and her tail curled round her ankle and her head a little on one side. She said, "Low at nine," to the room, and Ghira said "Mm" from behind a newspaper, and that was the day.

Blake had been in the house for a month before she noticed she'd stopped looking at it.

She knew it. She woke at six and knew where the water was, as she knew where the north was in a strange room, without having decided to know. She went down to the quay at the half-ebb and sat with Tobbe in the shade of the hull and the needle went in and out. She did not think about it. It was a thing the body did.

The sun was no longer the first thing. She had stopped noticing the looks. She noticed, now and then, that she had noticed, and was mildly surprised.

Sun had come in the second week. He'd stepped off the ferry with a string bag of mangoes and found her on the back step, and sat down on the other end of it, and said nothing at all. He was, she discovered, capable of this. It had surprised both of them. He stayed two afternoons. On the third morning there was a note on the kitchen table in his terrible writing that said *back soon, eat the mangoes,* and she did, one a day, standing at the sink.

* * *

"What's that you're humming?"

Her mother stood in the kitchen doorway with the kettle. It was late afternoon. Blake was on the step with a net across her knees, and the light came through the mesh in a hundred small gold squares onto the boards.

"Nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's got a middle."

"It's — I don't know what it is."

Kali hummed it back.

She did it exactly. She had always had a good ear, better than Blake's. She took the three notes and the rest and the phrase that climbed, and held the top of it a little longer than it deserved, and let it fall; and Blake's hands stopped on the net.

"It's a good tune," said her mother.

"Yes."

"Where did you get it?"

She thought of several answers. She considered the whole stock of them, laid out in a row on the step, like the nets.

"A girl hums it."

"Ah," said Kali.

That was all. She went and set the kettle down on the stove, and the sound of the gas going up came through the open door. After a time she said, to the stove: "You'll tell me her name or you won't."

"I won't."

"All right."

The net lay on her knees. The small gold squares moved. Down at the quay somebody was shouting about a rope.

"Weiss," said Blake.

It was quiet. It was quieter than she had meant it to be, and she heard it go into the kitchen, and not come out.

Her mother did not turn round. She stood with her back to the door and her hand on the kettle handle, and she said, after a decent interval, in a voice of immense calm:

"That's a cold name for a warm tune."

* * *

The boat came on a Tuesday.

She was at the quay. It was the half-flood, about ten, and the water was loud with sunlight, and the first she knew was the sound of the harbour going quiet. It went outward in rings. The shouting about the rope stopped. A child dropped a bucket. Tobbe's hand stopped on the cord.

It was a low grey boat with no name and a flag of no colour, and it stood off the point at the mouth of the bay and lay there, riding. A man stood in the bow. Even at that distance, against the water, he was white and red.

"Go home," said Tobbe.

"No."

"Girl."

"No."

She went at a walk. She did not run, because running was what they would be looking for. She went up through the alley behind the fish market with her pulse in her teeth and the old pressure in her ears, flat against the skull, and for the first time in a month they were doing what they had been made to do.

Her father was already in the yard. He had a coat on, and his hands were bare. He had taken down, from its hook above the door, an object she had not seen since she was a child.

"He is asking for you," said Ghira.

"I know."

"By name. In front of the harbour. He has sent a boy with a message." He did not say what it said. "I can have forty men on the north wall in a quarter of an hour. He knows it. He will not land where the wall is."

"Where?"

"The south strand. At the low."

She looked at the sheet in the kitchen, through the open door. *LOW 6:14.*

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"He's patient," said Blake.

"Yes."

Her mother had come out of the house. She stood on the step, with her hands folded in front of her.

"It is her door," said Kali.

Ghira said nothing. He looked at his wife for some time. Then at Blake, the length of the yard, with his heavy face still; the face of a man who has seen a long way down the road and has decided that he is not going to like it.

"She is eighteen," he said.

"She was seventeen when she left."

"I know."

"Then she has been grown for some time," said Kali, "and none of us noticed."

* * *

She did not remember the night.

She was told afterward that she had slept. She did not remember lying down. She remembered that her mother sat with her. She remembered the red pencil, the light on the sheet, a steady scratch as someone corrected a word. She woke before the dawn and was fully awake.

The south strand was a mile of grey sand under a long grey sky. At the half-ebb it was nearly a quarter mile broad. She walked out to the middle, alone, and stood. She had her blade. She had nothing else, and no ribbon, and the wind was in her ears.

She did not remember much of the next part either. It did not come back in order. It did not come back in pieces. It came back as a heaviness down one side of her, like a coat soaked through, and it lay there and did not move.

What she remembered was the end of it. She remembered the sand, and the water over her knees. She was sitting down. She did not know when she had sat. The tide had turned. It was coming in around her, quietly, over her boots and her thighs and the heel of her hands, and it was warm. Her hands lay open on the surface. They were steady. That was the thing she would not forget. They were steady, and they were not hers.

The blade was a little way off. She watched the water take the colour out of it.

Behind her somebody was walking down the beach. She heard it. She heard the pace, and knew it, and did not turn. The steps came to within ten feet and stopped. Her ears, which had gone flat, came slowly up.

Her mother came round to the front.

She did it carefully. She did it in a wide arc, through the water, where Blake could watch her come. She stopped at a little distance, with the sea to her waist and a tin cup in her hand, and her tail trailing on the surface, and looked at her daughter with her whole attention, and knelt.

"Drink."

"I thought it would feel like something."

"Drink."

She took the cup. The tea was hot. It had honey in it. It was a ridiculous thing, an impossible thing, to be sitting in the sea at seven in the morning with hot tea in a tin cup, and she held it in both hands, and the steam came up, and went into her face.

"It might," said her mother, after a while. "Later. Or it won't."

"Which?"

"I don't know. Drink anyway."

She drank it. The tide came in.

At twenty past seven it covered the place. It was exact. It rose over the sand in an even grey sheet, with a small noise like someone smoothing a bed, and when it had passed there was no mark on the strand at all, not a footprint, not a stain; and her mother, who had read the table for thirty years, nodded once, as at a clock that had kept good time.

* * *

She told them on the third night, at supper.

She'd gone over it all day. She had thought of the best way to do it. She had found there wasn't one; there was only the way that she did it, in the end, with a fork in her hand and her eyes on her plate, which was to say it.

"I'm going north."

Nobody spoke. Her father stopped chewing.

"Not tomorrow. Not for a while. I don't have the money, and I'd like to be sure about the roads." She put down the fork. "I'm telling you now. So it isn't a surprise. So you know."

It sounded stupid. It sounded like a child's thing.

"North," said Ghira. "To Atlas."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"A person."

There was a long pause. Kali picked up the bread and tore it, once, down the middle.

"The cold one," she said.

Blake looked at her.

"Take the good coat," said her mother, and put half the loaf on her plate. "The grey one. It's in the chest, under the blankets. It was mine. You will need it to be warm."
