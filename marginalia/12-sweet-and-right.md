# 12. sweet and right

> *Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori.*
>
> — Horace
>
> *...The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est*
> *Pro patria mori.*
>
> — Wilfred Owen

Winter came in the morning, in uniform, straight from the dock.

She did not knock. She never did. She stood in the door of the dressing room with her bag on her shoulder and her cap under her arm, and looked at Weiss in the vanity mirror, and Weiss looked at her, and neither of them said anything for some seconds. The dress was on its hanger on the wardrobe door. It was white. The ceremony was at two.

"Your hair," said Winter.

"I was going to do it."

"You were going to put it up like a secretary."

She set down the bag. She took off her gloves, one finger at a time, and laid them on the vanity beside a comb, and came round behind the stool. Weiss watched her in the glass. Her sister's face was exactly as it had been the last time, which was the spring before Beacon: pale, composed, a little tired round the eyes, with the same fine line between the brows that their father had. She picked up the comb.

She had done this when they were children. It had been Winter, not the nurse, in the end, on the mornings when there was an event, because Winter was quick, and the plait came out smooth, and Weiss had been told to stand still. She had stood still. She stood still now.

It took a minute and a half. The comb went through without catching. Winter's fingers moved at the back of her head in a rapid, certain, undemonstrative way, over and under, and tightened, and again.

Weiss did not know she was humming until the hands stopped.

They stopped halfway down the braid. They did not move, and the comb in Winter's other hand did not move, and in the mirror her face was the same. It was only the hands.

"Not in front of Father," said Winter.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The tune. He doesn't care for it."

"Why not?"

"I know that he doesn't care for it."

The hands began again. Weiss watched them go on, and said, "Do you know what it is?"

"No."

She said it evenly, to the back of Weiss's head. She finished the plait and pinned it, and the pins went in one after another, without hurry.

On the vanity, between the comb and the pins, lay a black wool glove with the fingertips cut off.

Winter picked it up. She turned it over. She was silent for a time, looking at it, with her thumb in the cuff.

"Whose?"

"A friend's."

"It's not regulation."

"No."

Winter set it down. She set it down precisely where it had been, with the thumb toward the mirror, and then, as though it were an afterthought, she took it up again and put it in the inner pocket of her uniform, and closed the button.

"I'll hold it for the duration," she said.

* * *

The Hall of Banners was long and grey and very cold. Someone had hung the flags of the four kingdoms in a row along the north wall, and under them, on easels, there were photographs. They had been blown up large and mounted on board. Weiss counted forty-one. Under each was a name, set in capitals, with two dates.

She knew nine of the faces.

Pyrrha's was in the middle. It was the large official portrait, the one from the tournament circuit, with the circlet and the red hair and the camera smile. It was three feet high. Someone had put a white flower at the foot of it, and the flower had already begun to go brown at the edge.

Behind the dais hung a banner, a hundred feet of dark cloth with gold letters.

DULCE ET DECORUM EST.

Weiss had been taught the old language at nine, by a man with a small dry cough, who had held that it was the only civilised tongue for a motto. She did not need to be told the rest. *It is sweet, and it is fitting.* People always forgot the second word. They remembered the sweet.

There were four hundred chairs, in rows, and perhaps three hundred were full. The families sat in front. There was a woman in the third row in a good black coat, with a photograph in her lap, not on an easel, a small cardboard one, in a frame. She was not looking at the dais. She was looking at it.

A band was playing something slow. In the aisle, by the door, a child stood at attention. She was about nine. She wore the full dress uniform of the Atlesian cadets, cut down, and she held a flag taller than herself in both hands, with the pole planted between her shoes, and an expression of perfect, terrible gravity. Nobody had told her she could move.

Weiss sat in the first row, on the aisle, beside her father.

He did not look at her. He sat very upright in a dark suit with his hands on his knees. He had been very kind in the car. He had asked her if she had eaten. He had said that he knew this was a hard day and she was bearing it well; and she had said "Thank you, Father," and he had patted her hand, twice, as he did, and looked out of the window.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Councilman Thrane.

He spoke for twenty-five minutes. He was good. He had a voice like a warm hand on the shoulder, and he used it well, dropping at the ends of phrases and then lifting. He spoke of the Festival. He spoke of the night, in a general way, as of a storm that had been survived. He spoke of the forty-one. "They gave all they had to give," he said. "They gave it without being asked. And there is a thing that a great many of us in this room have come to know, in the long years, about giving: that it is a sweet thing, in the end. And a fitting one. And that it is the whole of what a kingdom is for."

The room made a sound. It was a long exhalation, as of people being told what they had hoped to be told.

By the door the child with the flag had begun, very slightly, to tremble.

* * *

"And now," said Councilman Thrane, "the Company has asked that the Heir of the House of Schnee, who stood on that field herself, be permitted to lead us in the anthem."

Her father touched her elbow.

She went up the steps. They were wooden and carpeted and she took them with the care she took with everything, one at a time. The microphone was set to her height. She did not need it. She had been taught to fill a room with nothing, and this was a small room.

She looked at the back wall.

That was the technique. You did not sing to anyone. You sang to the back wall, a spot on it, at the level of the eye, and you let the sound go out to meet it; and if you looked at a face you would hear the face, and the note would curl.

The band gave her the pitch.

She breathed in, as she had been taught, low, and wide. She opened her mouth.

The first note came as it always did. It was clean. It was the bright, cool, centred sound that she had spent eleven years of her life learning to make, and it went out over the four hundred chairs without any effort and struck the far wall and came back. Her voice did what it was built to do. She listened to it as a conductor listens, from a little way off.

*Sing for the white, and the high, and the clear—*

She could see, at the edge of her sight, the child with the flag.

She could see the woman in the third row. She was looking down now. Her lips were moving and it was not the words.

*—for the land that was kept, and the hearth that was held—*

She was thinking, with great clarity, that Pyrrha had once picked up her pencil case. It had been in the corridor outside Port's room, in the second week. It had spilled. Pyrrha had gone down on one knee and collected all of it, the pens, the ruler, the little silver compass, and had handed it back, and said nothing about the fact that she was Pyrrha Nikos. It had taken eleven seconds. Weiss had said "Thank you." She had thought, at the time, that it was a very small thing.

*—and the sons and the daughters who fell where they stood—*

They had spelled her name correctly on the banner. It was the one thing they had got right.

The last verse rose. She took it up and out, to the E-flat, the high one, the note that Madame had told her for years she would never quite reach cleanly. She reached it cleanly. It hung under the roof of the Hall of Banners, cold and clear and perfect, and held, and she let it fall.

It did not break.

She had been hoping, in some small clerical corner of herself, that it would.

The applause began at the back. It rolled forward like a wave in a shallow bay. People were rising. She stood at the microphone with her hands at her sides and received it, and the child at the door looked up at the sound and the flag swayed and she caught it with both hands, white to the wrists.

* * *

The dressing room was empty. She shut the door.

She ran the tap in the basin until the water came hot. She washed her hands. She washed them again, with soap, up to the wrist, properly, the way one is taught before an operation. She held them under the stream. It was very hot. She let it be.

When the knock came she did not turn the tap off.

"Come in."

It was Whitley. He was thirteen, in a small dark suit with a white shirt buttoned to the neck, and he came in with his hands clasped behind him and stopped three paces off. He had her hair and her mouth. The eyes were their father's, and they were a great deal too old.

"That was beautiful," he said.

"Thank you."

"Father was pleased. He said you took the E-flat cleaner than Madame Orsolo has ever managed."

"Did he."

"I thought you would like to know."

He said it with a faint, careful air of delivering a parcel. She looked at him in the mirror. He had stood like that, she knew, at the door of the study, at the age of seven, nine, eleven, waiting to be told that he had said it right.

"Weiss?"

"Yes?"

"You didn't look at anyone."

"I was looking at the back wall."

"That's what I said," said Whitley. And he went out, closing the door as gently as a drawer.

She turned off the tap.

Her mother was in the passage. She had a glass in her hand, half full, and she was standing in the middle of the passage rather than against the wall, which she did when she had had a little. She smelled of lilies and red wine. She looked at Weiss for a time with her pale, kind, unfocused eyes.

"Darling. It was lovely."

"Thank you, Mother."

Willow put out her free hand, and touched the sleeve of the dress, and left it there a moment, light as a leaf. Her fingers were cold. Then she took it away, and went down the passage toward the sound of the reception, and did not look back, and the glass did not spill.

* * *

Winter found her at the end of the corridor, at the window.

It was past five. The Hall was emptying. The light was going yellow over the roofs of the upper city, and the airships were coming and going in the sky, small and silver and slow.

"I have the eight o'clock," said Winter. "I cannot stay."

"I know."

"I'm sorry."

"You don't need to be."

Winter said nothing. She unbuttoned the pocket of her tunic and took out a black wool glove and held it out on her flat palm. Weiss looked at it. She took it. It was warm from being near a body.

"You ate nothing at the reception."

"I had a little."

"You moved it to the other side of the plate." She said it in the dry way she said everything, and then, abruptly, took Weiss's hand in both of hers, the glove and the fingers and all, and held it. She held it hard, like someone checking a rail. It lasted perhaps five seconds. "Eat."

"Yes."

"I'll write."

"You always say that."

"I always do," said Winter.

She went. Her boots rang on the stone, evenly, to the end of the corridor, and turned, and were gone.

Weiss stood at the window. After a while she looked down at her hands.

The right still had its white silk glove, the formal one, with the pearl button at the wrist. The left was bare. She turned the black glove over once. Then she put it on. It was a little large, and it smelled of wool, and of a library in winter, and she pulled the cuff snug under the sleeve.

She wore them the rest of the evening, one white and one black.

Nobody said a word. It is a remarkable thing, what people will not say to a Schnee.
