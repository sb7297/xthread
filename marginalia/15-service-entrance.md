# 15. service entrance

The pantry light was on at eleven.

She saw it from the end of the passage, a bar of yellow under the door, and stood in the dark on the runner with her hand on the wall. She had not slept. She had lain on the made bed in her clothes for three hours, with the lamp off, rehearsing; and she had come to a decision, and then been unable to get her feet off the floor.

She knocked.

"Come."

Klein was at the long table with a tray of silver in front of him and a cloth in his hand. It was the good silver. There was no need to polish it. It lay in rows, each piece squared against the next, and he was working on a soup spoon with small, slow, circular strokes, and did not look up.

"Miss Weiss."

"Klein, I need to go to Hollin Gap."

The cloth stopped.

It was all that stopped. His hand went on holding the spoon, and his face, long and soft and grey-moustached, remained turned down toward it, as though the spoon had said something that required thought.

"Miss Weiss," he said, "don't."

"I'm not asking your permission."

"No." He set the spoon in its row. "You're asking for a coat."

She stared at him.

"It's a day by rail to the Mantle depot, and then another by sled. The line beyond was cut in the spring. There'll be a man at the Mantle depot who will drive for a price." He had folded the cloth into a square. He was speaking very mildly, to the tray. "I should say you want a good deal more than a coat. But I'd begin there."

"You'll be dismissed."

"He may try."

"Klein—"

"I'm very old, Miss Weiss. It takes a great deal to move something that old." He looked up for the first time. His eyes were small and wet and perfectly steady. "I have stood at that sideboard for twenty-six years. I've poured every glass in this house. I have been of use in the pantry, and the dining room, and the front hall, in more ways than I would care to enumerate, and I find, upon reflection, that I should like to be of use in another room for once."

She did not say anything. She found that she could not.

He opened the drawer under the table.

* * *

There was a coat. It was a long grey wool one, nine years old, heavy as a rug, with a worn collar and a button replaced with another of a different make. He held it up. It was too large. It would come to her ankles.

"You will look like a coachman."

"Thank you."

"It is warm. It was warmer in the old days, but it is still warm." He laid it over the back of a chair. From the second drawer he took a pair of gloves, brown, thick, lined with fleece, and set them on the coat.

"I have gloves," said Weiss.

She put out her hands to show him.

The right was white silk with a pearl button. The left was black wool, cut off at the knuckle, and rather large, and pulled up under her cuff.

Klein regarded them.

He did it for a considerable time. His moustache did something. It did it once and then it stopped, and his face, otherwise, remained as grave as a church.

"So you have," he said.

He put the brown gloves back in the drawer and closed it.

From the pocket of his waistcoat he brought a small envelope, thick, tied with string. He laid it on the coat. She knew without looking what it was, and put her hand over it.

"I can't."

"It is not a gift."

"Klein."

"It is a loan, Miss Weiss, against the tea. You may repay it," said the butler, "by never mentioning it."

She stood with her hand on the envelope. It was warm from being close to him. There was a clock on the wall of the pantry, a cheap white one with black hands, and in the silence it was possible to hear the second hand moving.

"The house," she said.

"At Camp Four? It was kept up for the inspectors. Two rooms and a stove. The key is on the board, with the others. I have not seen anyone ask for it since the spring." He paused. "No one will look for you there. It has your father's name on every wall."

"That's a poor recommendation."

"It's the best one I have," said Klein.

* * *

She wrote the note at the dining-room table in the dark.

She had her own pen. She could do it by touch. The room was cold and she did not light a lamp. The table went away from her on both sides in a long pale gleam, and at the far end, where the bare place was, the cloth lay smooth and white and unmarked, and the plate in the dark was a round of faint light.

*I am going. I will not say where. I will write when I can. —W.*

She had no one to give it to. There was a rule, and it was in ink, and she did not mean to be the second to break it. She folded the note in three. She laid it on the fifth plate, centred, squarely.

She stood looking at it.

It looked like a place card.

* * *

The kitchen was down a short flight and along a flagged passage that smelled of onions and coal. She had been in it perhaps six times in her life. She had been taken there as a child, for the pleasure of the cook, and had been lifted onto a stool and given a spoon; she remembered the spoon, and the warm wooden table, and a pair of large floury hands, and not the face above them.

There was a light at the far end. A lamp on the range.

A woman stood in front of it, with her back to the door, in a white apron and a cap, stirring a pot.

She was broad. She was perhaps fifty. Her sleeves were pushed above the elbow and her arms were thick and brown, and when she turned her head at the sound of the step the cap shifted, and Weiss saw her ears, which were round and dark and set high, under the cloth.

"Miss."

"Mrs. Abney."

The woman did not stop stirring. She looked at the grey coat, and at the envelope in Weiss's hand, and at Weiss's face. She did not look surprised. She looked as a person looks at a clock to see that it agrees with another.

"Klein's been down," she said.

"Yes."

"Sit."

She sat at the long table. It was scrubbed pale as bone. Mrs. Abney took a bundle from the dresser and set it in front of her, tied in a cloth: a loaf, a heel of cheese, a twist of salt fish, a flask. She put it down with some force.

"I'm not helping you, miss."

"No."

"I'm feeding somebody that's leaving. It's different." She wiped her hands on her apron, one and then the other. "If they ask me, I gave you nothing. If they ask Orla, she was asleep. If they ask Teodor, he'll say he's got a bad back."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do. But it's all right."

Weiss's hand, on the bundle, tightened.

She heard it. It was a phrase the cook had used ten thousand times, to the kitchen boy, to the dog. It was a thing said when a pot was put right. It went by like a bird against a window, and was gone, and the room was exactly as it had been.

She said: "What is your name?"

The cook looked at her.

"Abney."

"Your first name."

It was a long moment. The pot ticked on the range. Mrs. Abney's face, broad and closed and weathered, did a thing Weiss could not read; it was neither pleasure nor its opposite; it was a kind of slow settling, as of a door being tried.

"Dorrit," she said.

"Dorrit."

"It's nobody's business."

"Thank you," said Weiss. "For the bread."

"Mm." The cook turned back to the range. "Mind you eat it."

* * *

The back door was at the end of the passage, down three steps. It was narrow and painted green. She had never used it. She had not known it was green.

Teodor was at the gate.

He stood with his back to the wall in the dark, a thin tall man in a heavy jacket with the collar turned up, and as she came across the yard he took one hand from his pocket and lifted the bar. He did not look at her. His cap sat far back on his head, and above his brow two small antlers, no longer than her thumb, stood against the sky.

"Teodor."

"Miss."

"I'm sorry. I should have asked."

"Asked what, miss?"

"Your name." She stopped. "I have known you since I was six."

"It's all right, miss." He said it simply. He had his eyes on the gate. "It's a cold morning. Mind the ice by the second post."

The gate swung. The road lay beyond it, white, running down between the walls toward the lights of the lower city. A little wind came up it. She stood on the threshold with Klein's coat to her ankles and Mrs. Abney's bundle under her arm and, in her pocket, an envelope, and she heard it again, *all right,* in the thin air, in the voice of a man she had never thanked.

She had thought it would sound different now.

She went through.

It had been snowing. A thin dry fall, an inch or two, lying along the road and over the cobbles, perfectly untouched. The stars were out above it. She stood and looked at it. It was the first time in her life she had left a house by the door the work went out of.

She took a step.

Under her boot, high and small and flat, the snow squeaked.

She stopped. She stamped, once, to be sure.

It squeaked.

"There," said Weiss, to no one.

She walked on down the hill.
