# 8. kettle

*The kettle.*

It was not a kettle. It was a ceramic bowl on Weiss's side of the desk, and a pot, and a tin, and a small red crystal the size of a thumbnail, in a dish of its own.

"That's Dust," said Blake.

"The lowest grade. It is perfectly safe."

"It's a bomb in the shape of a crystal."

"It is a heating element." Weiss dropped the crystal into the bowl of water. It sank, and sat on the bottom, giving off nothing at all, and after a minute a thin line of bubbles began to rise off its edges. "Rule four says not near the radiator. It says nothing about on the desk."

"It says *Dust is stored in the locker.*"

"Stored. This is in use."

She was pouring the water over the leaves by then. The tea was black and smelled of smoke, and of something burnt and sweet underneath, like a field in autumn. She set the lid on the pot, and folded her hands, and began to hum.

Three notes, a rest, a phrase that climbed. The eight bars, through once, and once again.

At the end of the second she lifted the lid.

"It's four minutes," said Blake.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The tune. Twice through. You time it by that."

Weiss looked at the pot. "I have always done it that way."

"What is it?"

"It isn't — I don't know that it's anything. I've always hummed it." She poured two cups, with the strainer, and her hand was entirely steady. "It's just there."

Blake smelled the tea before she drank it, which she did with everything. Then she drank. It was strong and dark and a little bitter, and it was good, and she could not have said why it made her want to sit down.

"It's good."

"Of course it's good," said Weiss.

She did not offer sugar. She did not offer milk. She looked at Blake over the rim of the cup with exactly the expression of a person waiting to be told it was not good.

* * *

*The team.*

She took the ribbon off at the table, after supper, on a Tuesday. She had not planned to. She had been listening to Ruby tell a story about a hat, and she put her hand up to scratch, and her fingers found the knot.

"I'm a Faunus," she said.

It came out of her very flat. Ruby stopped in the middle of the hat. Yang, on the other side of the table, looked at her over a mug and said, "Yeah."

"You—"

"You sleep with your ears flat when you dream." Yang put the mug down. "I'm not blind. Week two."

"You didn't say."

"I figured you'd say when you wanted. It's yours to say."

Ruby was staring at the top of Blake's head with the expression of a person who had been shown a new colour. "Wait. Wait. Can I—"

"No."

"Okay." She sat on her hands. "Can I ask a different question?"

"Yes."

"Is it a cat?"

"Yes."

"I *knew* it," said Ruby, who had not.

At the desk by the window, Weiss's pen had stopped.

She had not turned around. Blake could see the back of her neck. It was still, and a little pink, and after a while she said, to the window, "I didn't know. Until I found out in the worst way a person can."

"Yeah," said Yang. "I heard."

"From whom?"

"From the way you've been holding doors." Yang considered. "It's terrifying."

Weiss said nothing at all. But the back of her neck went from pink to a colour you could have used to mark a map, and her pen began again, and Blake, looking at the table, found that she was doing something she was not accustomed to with her face.

* * *

*The book.*

"You looked at the cover."

"It's about birds."

"It's about a woman who spends a year alone in a hut in the north and counts them. That's the plot. It's the whole plot."

"That's what I said."

Blake gave it to her anyway. It was a small thick paperback with a green cloth spine. *A Year with the Ptarmigan,* H. Ingram. She had bought it second-hand in a shop in Vale that had no sign, and the waiver was still in it, folded in four, at page 112.

Weiss took it back on the fourth night. She held it out with both hands.

"Your waiver fell out."

"Leave it in."

"It will wrinkle."

"It's been wrinkled."

Weiss put the paper back at the place it had come from. Her thumbnail ran down the fold, once, flattening it.

The next time Blake opened the book, there was a mark on page 14. Beside the sentence she had underlined twice, *I am told that a solitary life is a life without witnesses; I find it is truer to say that it is a life with very few, and those very attentive,* there was a word in the margin in a pencil that was lighter and smaller and held at a steeper angle than hers.

*Disagree.*

She considered it for some time. Then, under it, she wrote: *Which part.*

Two days later, when she looked, it said: *Attentive.*

*She's counting birds,* wrote Blake.

*Birds are not witnesses.*

*Then who's she counting for.*

There was nothing, after that, for six days. On the seventh, in the margin, was a short pencil line, with a hook at the end, which might have been the start of a letter and might have been nothing at all.

She left it there. She didn't write under it.

* * *

*The shower.*

Weiss's shower was at twenty to seven, the second-best hour. Blake sat in the corridor opposite the door, on the floor, with her back to the wall and a book in her hands. The water ran. Behind the water, after a time, there began to be a voice.

It was not humming. It was singing. It was a full, clean, open sound, without words, only a sustained *ah* that went up in a line and held and came down, and Blake had heard people sing before, but she had not heard anyone sing as if the building had been built around the note. It filled the corridor. It went up the stairs and came back. Her ears came up, both of them, under the ribbon, and she pushed down on the knot with one hand and went on holding the book.

She heard the three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

"Whatcha doing?"

Yang had come up the stairs with a towel round her neck.

"Reading."

"Book's upside down."

"It's a stylistic choice."

"Mm," said Yang. She slid down the wall and sat next to her. Inside, the phrase went up again and Weiss took it out past where it was written, a long clear unexpected reach of sound that hung in the stairwell like a piece of glass, and held it, and let it go. Yang's eyebrows went up.

"Whoa."

"Yes."

"She's actually *good.*"

"Yes."

Yang was silent. It was an extraordinary thing to watch, in that it clearly cost her. She looked at her hands. She looked at the ceiling. She looked at Blake's book, which was still upside down.

"Okay," she said. "I'm going to go stand over there."

"Why?"

"Because I'm not going to say anything, and it's taking a lot out of me."

She went and stood at the far end of the corridor with her back to them both, humming something unrelated, with great and obvious effort.

* * *

*The snow.*

It began at one in the morning. Blake knew because she had been awake, and she heard it before she saw it: a faint dry whisper on the glass, like sand sifting. She went to the window, and found that the window was already occupied.

Weiss stood there in her nightdress with her hands flat against the pane.

"It's snowing."

"Yes."

"It isn't the same."

"Isn't it?"

"At home it's a different kind." Weiss did not turn her head. "Quieter. It's dry. It squeaks."

"Squeaks."

"Under boots. When it's cold enough. Like — a sort of—" She made a noise, high and small, in her nose.

"Show me."

It was against the rules. It was against, in fact, most of the rules, and the sheet by the door said so in ink; but Weiss was already getting her boots, and then her coat over the nightdress, and she took the stairs on her toes with Blake behind her, past the door of the night warden's office, past the portrait of the first Headmaster, out through the side door into the court.

There was an inch down. It was soft and wet and heavy, and it covered the lawn like a thing spilled. The lamps made their circles. Weiss stepped out into the middle of it and stamped.

Nothing happened.

She stamped again. Nothing. She took three steps, and the third time she brought her heel down it made a flat wet noise like a hand being slapped.

"It's wrong," she said.

"It's snow."

"It is not *snow.* This is — it's rain that has lost its nerve."

Blake didn't say anything. She couldn't, because Weiss had turned round with her hair coming down on one side and snow on her eyelashes and a look of such offended dignity that nothing she could have said would have been equal to it. And then, in front of her, all at once, Weiss laughed.

It was not the laugh she used at dinner. That one was low and musical and went a short way and stopped. This one came out of her nose. It was a snort, short, surprised, and ugly, and she clapped a hand over her mouth, and her eyes above the hand went wide.

Blake looked at her.

"Don't," said Weiss, through her fingers.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

She had been going to. She had been going to say nothing at all, for as long as it took, and she did.

For a week afterward she tried to make her do it again. She was not good at it. She was not a person who made jokes, and the ones she tried were dry as a bone, and Weiss received them with a face of stone. But it occupied her. It was a great comfort to have something to work on.

* * *

*The weekend.*

Weiss went home on Friday for a reason that no one asked about. She went in her good coat, the white one with the grey fur, with her case in her hand, and she took the afternoon airship, and the room was quiet.

She came back on Sunday at nine.

She said "Good evening" to the room. She put her case at the foot of the bed. She sat down and began to unpack, and Blake, from the window, watched her fold a blouse in three, and fold it in three again, and lay it in the drawer, and take it out, and fold it once more.

Nobody said anything. Ruby looked at Yang, and Yang looked at the ceiling. After a time Ruby began, gently, to talk about her afternoon, and Weiss said "Yes" and "Oh" and "I see," in the voice of a person standing at the very edge of a platform.

Blake went out. She was back in a quarter of an hour.

She had a clip lamp. It was small and black and she'd got it from the common room, from the desk of a second-year who had not been using it, and she had paid him, which she had not mentioned. She fixed it to the shelf above Weiss's bunk. She angled it down. It took her under a minute.

Weiss watched her do it from the bed.

"I didn't request that."

"It's low."

There was a silence in which neither of them moved.

"Yes," said Weiss. "I recall."

She lay down. She turned onto her side and pulled up the blanket. After a time she reached up, without looking, and clicked the lamp on. The shelf went yellow. It was a small light.

She read for some time, with the book propped on the pillow. Then the page stopped turning. Then the book slid, slowly, sideways, and came to rest against her face.

Blake got up. She crossed the room, on the quiet boards. She lifted the book from her face, and marked the page with a finger, and closed it, and set it on the shelf under the lamp.

She did not turn the lamp off. She left it on all night, like the other, and slept, for the first time in a long time, in the glow of the two.
