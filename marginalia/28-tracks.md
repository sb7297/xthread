# 28. tracks

The house was two hours down the valley, and she walked it in the dark.

She did not run. She had thought she would. She had thought that when the shadow was standing and the door was open she would go out of the camp as a stone goes out of a sling; but she found, on the track, in the grey before dawn, that her legs did not want to. They went in a long, steady, mechanical stride, one and one and one, and she let them, and listened to the snow squeak under her boots, and tried not to hear what it said.

She had made the shadow at four. She had made it with care. She had stood in the dark of the bunkhouse with the empty kettle in her fist and put into the thing everything she knew about herself: the set of the shoulders, the length of the pause, the angle of the neck when she was listening and did not wish to be seen listening. It had been good work. She had walked across the yard and put it in the hut, in front of the stove, and set the kettle in its hand.

She had written the note in the book.

She went over that part again and again, on the track. She could not stop it. The pencil stub in the light of the stove. The page. She had been calm. She had thought of the words and found the words and set them down in a good dry hand, and had stood back and thought, with a feeling that she recognised in the dark of her own chest as *satisfaction: it is correct. Notice given.*

*Rule 12: notice given. —B.*

It was the cruellest sentence she had ever written. She had been proud of the grammar.

* * *

The house stood by itself at the head of a small side valley, a low sod-roofed thing half sunk in the snow, with a stovepipe and a row of drying fish on a pole and a team of dogs in the lee of the wall who got up and shook and said what they thought of her.

The door opened before she reached it.

Tove stood in it in a man's undershirt and a pair of enormous boots, with her ears flat to her head against the cold and a lamp in her hand. She looked at Blake for a good while. She looked at her feet, which were wet through, and at her face, and at the place on her left where, until that morning, there had been a dog.

"Hm," she said.

She stood aside.

It was a single room. There was a stove, a table, a bed in the corner under a quilt, a shelf with a row of brass pit lamps, thirty-one of them and a few over, each wiped and standing in its place. There was a radio in a box. There was a smell of dogs and smoke and cold fish and something cooking. She sat down where she was put, by the stove, and her boots were taken off by someone else, and a bowl was put in her hands.

She ate. It was soup. It had barley in it.

"Thank you," she said.

Tove said nothing. She went about her work, which was the mending of a harness, at the table, with an awl and a length of waxed thread. She did not ask a question. She did not look round. The dogs outside scuffled and settled. Blake sat in front of the stove in the warm and ate the soup, and then the loaf, and then another bowl, and said *thank you,* twice more, in a voice that belonged to a person on the other side of a pane of glass.

She slept on the floor. She slept for twenty hours.

* * *

The radio spoke on the second day.

She was in the corner, in the quilt, with her back to the wall. Tove was at the box with her hand on a knob, and she said "Tove here," in her flat dry voice, to the air, and there was a hiss; and she said a thing about a cat in her kitchen. It took twenty seconds.

And across the hiss, from a long way off, small and cracked and tinny, came a voice.

*"Is she—"*

Blake's ears came up.

She had known that she would hear it. She had not known that it would go through her like that. It was a thin quick voice, with all the starch gone out of it, and what was left was not a thing she had heard in it before: it was the voice of a person who has been standing a long time in a cold place and has just been told that something might be all right.

*"Is she—"*

"She's eating," said Tove.

*"I won't."*

The line clicked. The box went on hissing. Tove hung up the handset on its hook, and sat down at the table, and took up the awl, and did not turn round.

"Don't," said Blake.

"Didn't say anything."

"I know."

"Hm," said the old woman, and drove the awl through the leather.

* * *

On the fourth night she told her.

She told it in the dark, at the table, with the lamp turned low and the harness lying in her lap, in the same voice in which she said everything else. Blake had not asked. She was by the stove with her knees up, and Tove, who had not said a thing for an hour, began without any preface.

"My Halvard had a cough."

The stove ticked.

"It was the fortieth. He'd had it a week. It was a wet one, down in the chest; he'd lie awake with it and I'd hear him. I said stay home. He said he would." She turned the harness over. "It was a day's pay. We were behind. I'd a bill at the store. I said, *go on.* I said it twice."

She did not look up.

"He went. He was always a man who went. I sent him." The awl moved in the leather, in, out. "I haven't said that to anyone. Not to the girl. I thought I'd best say it to somebody who wasn't going to do anything about it."

"I'm sorry."

"That's not what I said."

"No."

"I'm not telling you so you'll feel better." Tove's voice did not change. "I'm telling you so you know it isn't special."

Blake looked at her.

"What you've got. What you're carrying, in the way you're carrying it, like a cat with a bird. It's not special. I've carried the same one for a year and a half. It weighs the same. I get up, and I feed the dogs." She bit off the thread. "There's a lot of us. That's all I've to say. It's not a comfort."

"No."

"It's a fact."

She laid down the harness. She sat for a minute with her hands on her knees, a short broad old woman in the lamplight, with her ears laid back, and the brass lamps behind her on the shelf, each with its small wick of shadow. Then she got up, creaking, and went to the bed.

"You want to go back," she said, over her shoulder.

"I don't know."

"You've a face like a dog at a door." She lay down, with a grunt, and pulled the quilt over her ears. "It's not locked."

* * *

She lay awake.

She had the floor by the stove. She had the quilt, which smelled of wool and age, and the red seam of light round the grille, and the sounds of the house: the old woman's long snore, the creak of the roof, the dogs in the wall. Outside the wind went down the valley and she heard it go.

She tried to think, and found she had been thinking for some time.

She had said *Adam.* It was not true. It was true in the way that a shadow is true; it was the right size, and the right shape, and it fell in the right place, and it was not him. She turned it over. What was true was that she had felt it. She had stood in the hut, in the lamp, with the paper in her hand, and every hair on her body had risen, because a person she loved was doing something with great gentleness for her own good, and the part of her that had been trained for six years had said: *there. That. It is starting.*

But it was not the same. She lay and looked at it. The difference was so plain that she was ashamed she had needed three days to find it.

Adam had never once been seen.

He had come from behind. He had come in the dark, and put his hand on her neck, and she had learned to know him by the silence; and in six years he had never stood in a doorway and been looked at. He had never written *rest* on a sheet of paper in a good hand and put it in front of her, in the light, to be read. He had never left it in plain view that she could say no.

Weiss had hummed.

Every night, across a yard, in a gale. She had walked toward a door in the dark and made a noise, so that it would be known; she had lit a stove and gone away singing, so that it would be known; and she had done it badly, and wrongly, and over the wrong things, and with a wall of careful kindness that had no gaps in it; but every time she had come, she had come from the front.

*I'm coming. I'm here. You may turn round.*

She had not heard it. She had been listening, for so long, for the lock.

She put her arm across her face.

She lay under the quilt on the floor of an old woman's house and let it come, which it did slowly, in a long cold silent heave, as if from a distance, and ran into her ears. The dog on her feet was not there. She missed him with a vividness that was almost comic. She lay and shook, without noise, for a long time, until the stove had gone down to a thread, and the old woman's snore had altered and become a different snore, and she was too tired to go on.

She thought: *Rule 12.*

She thought: *In person.*

* * *

On the eighth morning Tove said: "Go on."

She said it at the table, with her back to the room, to a pot. She did not turn round. There was a sack on the bench by the door, packed; it had not been there the night before. In it were a loaf, a twist of fish, a tin of the lamp oil, and two long bone-handled needles.

"For the sock," said Tove. "You do it badly."

"Thank you."

"That's four."

"Is it—"

"Door's not locked," said the old woman. She picked up a ladle and put it down. "It's not my door. But I've said."

The dogs came to the wall as she went. They stood in a row with their heads on one side. Tove did not come out. She was at the window, a broad shape behind the frost, and she lifted one hand, once, with the palm flat, and put it down.

* * *

She followed her own tracks.

They were nearly gone. A week of wind had laid a skin over them, and the print of her boots was only a soft series of dents, at intervals, going up the valley, which she could see better by looking a little to one side of them. She walked in them. She put each foot where she had put it before. It was a way of not having to choose.

She reached the camp at dusk.

It lay as it had always lain, in its blue snow, under the first stars: the long roofs, the stone, the dead tram, the little squared house at the head with its door to the wind. There was a light in it. She stopped on the track below the lip of the shelf, where she could see and not be seen, and stood for what might have been a quarter of an hour, with her hands in her pockets.

She did not go to the hut.

She would think about that, afterward, with something like despair. She went to the bunkhouse, as a cat goes to the place it knows, round the back, along the wall, in the dark. The thong was on its nail. She lifted it. The room was warm.

It had been lit.

She stood on the boards. There was the barrel with its red seam. There was the smell of wool, and of smoke. There was a lamp, turned low, on the rail of the end bunk; and on the pillow, folded once, square to the edge of the tick, there was a small stack of paper.

She went down the aisle.

She took up the stack and turned it over and began at the beginning.

*Stove lit. If you would rather it were not, say so, and I shan't. —W.*

She read it twice. She had never been able to help that. She read the second. *Stove lit again. Same offer.* The third. *I should like to leave an orange on this pillow. May I? (If not, I'll eat it.)*

Her hand had begun to shake. She sat down on the edge of the bunk.

*I have eaten the orange.*

It was too much. She put her fist against her mouth and made a sound into it which was not a laugh, though it had the shape of one; she bent over her knees, and the paper crackled against her lip. The fifth. *I did not light the stove tonight. I wasn't sure. It is cold. I will light it if you would like to find it warm. Leave word.* The sixth.

*I lit the stove. I said I wouldn't, and then I did. I'm sorry. I find that I would rather you came back to a warm room than a clean principle, and I'm aware that this is exactly the kind of thing I'm trying not to do. I am doing it anyway. You may tell me off.*

She read it three times.

The seventh was short. It had been written last, she thought, by the look of the ink, and in a different mood, steady. It was folded in four, and had lain on top of all the rest.

*I do not know whether you are coming back. I would like you to. This is not a request. It is a fact that I thought you ought to have. —W.*

She sat with it in her two hands.

The stove ticked. The lamp on the rail leaned in a draught. Outside, in the yard, in the black, the snow went on falling off the roof in soft slow slides. She did not know how long. At some point she took the seven papers and squared them in her palm, as she had seen it done, and folded them together, and put them inside her shirt, flat, against the skin.

She lay down on the pillow. It was cold. Then it was not.

* * *

She woke in the dark.

It was past ten. The lamp had burned low. She had the sense, as one has of a change in the pressure, that something had altered; and she lay, and listened, and heard across the yard a door open.

She was up. She was out of the bunkhouse by the back and along the wall before she had decided to be. She went on her toes, in the black, like a thing on a ledge. She did not go to the hut. She went to the woodpile, thirty yards off, and stood behind the stack, with her cheek to a frozen log, and watched a figure come across the snow with a lamp.

It was Weiss. She knew the stride.

It came to the bunkhouse door and stopped. It stood still, with the lamp held low, facing the wood; and then, softly, into the planks, it hummed.

Three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

She did it all. She did every bar. Blake stood at the woodpile with her ears flat against the frost and her heart going in her throat and watched her wait, with her head a little bent, for the count of five, for somebody to say, from the other side of a door that did not have anybody behind it, that she might come in.

The figure went in.

She was gone for some minutes. A glow moved behind the shutters. Blake stood, and she thought: *Go now. Go in, and say.* She thought: *She will find the pillow.* She thought it very clearly. She thought: *She will find the pillow and she will know, and I will be standing here like a post.*

The door opened. The figure came out.

It put the lamp on the rail inside, carefully, and shut the door. It stood on the step. It looked about, up at the sky, and across the camp, at the roofs, the poles, the white; and Blake saw the head turn, and stop, and the pale blur of a face lift toward the woodpile, and stay.

She had not moved. She had not made a sound.

The figure did not come toward her.

She had expected that it would. She had braced for it, for a rush, a hand, a voice calling her name across the snow in a tone of command. It did not come. The figure stood still on the step, thirty yards off, in the starlight, with its hands at its sides. And then she heard it, thin, clear, a little unsteady on the climb, going out over the snow toward her with great care: the tune, the whole of it, the eight bars.

It was not for the door. It was for her.

She stood with her hand on the log.

"May I come over?" said Weiss.

She counted five. She counted another five. She did it with the exactness of someone doing a sum. She was not asking twice because she had not heard. She was giving it time to be a real question.

There was a large silence in the world.

"Yes," said Blake.

* * *

She came across the snow.

She did it at a walk, with the lamp left behind, and stopped a yard off. A clean, scrupulous yard. She stood with her hands at her sides, in the coachman's coat, with her hair coming down and her face as white as paper, and Blake saw that she had been crying at some time in the last week, steadily, and had stopped, and that nobody had seen.

The dog came out of the dark in a rush and hit her knees.

She went down on one of them. He was shaking all over. He had his whole length against her and his nose in her neck and he was making a high continuous noise, and she put both arms round him and her face in the ruff and held on. She could feel Weiss above her, not moving.

"You gave notice," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"To a shadow."

"Yes."

"I stood in a room and talked to a coat." Her voice was perfectly steady. "For eleven minutes. I knew, about halfway, that it wasn't you. I finished. I wanted it said."

"I know."

"You don't."

"No." Blake lifted her head. "I don't."

"I am not finished being angry."

It went through her like a bell.

She knelt in the snow with the dog against her chest, looking up. It was the same voice. It was the voice of a girl at a door at five in the morning, in a coat black with frost, with her hair down, saying a thing to a bag on a bed. She had thought she would never hear it again, and she heard it, and it went right through to the floor of her.

"Okay," she said.

"I would like you to be here while I finish."

"I'm here."

"Good."

Weiss looked down at her. She was shaking, a very little, all over. She said: "It isn't all right."

"No."

"I'd like it to be said. It's *not.* You went. You stood in my kitchen, in my— and you *made* a— and then you wrote a *rule* in my book." The voice cracked and she let it. "That was cruel. That was the single most cruel thing I have ever seen done by a person with that much care. I have been—"

She stopped. She breathed.

"I'm not finished," she said, more quietly.

"Okay."

"You said Adam."

"Yes."

"It wasn't fair."

"It was half fair."

"It wasn't—"

"I said it because it was the worst thing I had." Blake did not look away. "I wanted it to hurt. I wanted you to stop. It worked, and I'm sorry, and it was half. It felt like the same thing. It's not the same thing." She swallowed. "He never came from the front."

Weiss said nothing.

"You do. Every night. I didn't hear it."

"It can be both," said Weiss, after a time. Her voice had gone odd. "Half fair."

"Yes."

"I shall have to think about the half."

"Okay."

"I'm thinking about it." She pressed her lips together. "I'll go on."

She stood in the snow with the starlight on her face, and went on. It did not take as long as Blake had feared. It was a list. It went down the way her lists did, point by point, flat, precise; there was the stove, and the sleeping, and the eleven minutes, and a good deal about the word *notice;* there was a pause, in the middle, in which something visible happened to her mouth. There was the dog. At the end of it she stopped, and stood, with her hands closed at her sides, and she looked at the stars over the roof of the bunkhouse as one looks at a long sum that has come out.

"That's all," she said. "I think that's all."

"Okay."

"You may say what you like."

Blake got up. She did it slowly, with the dog against her leg. She brushed the snow from her knees. The cold had got into her hands and the old ache was in her ears, and she stood a yard off from a person she had walked three thousand miles to find, in the middle of the night, in a yard, and for a long minute she could not think of one thing to say.

"Say it again," she said.

"What?"

"What you said. To the—" She made a small gesture, at the empty air, at a stove. "I wasn't there."

"It was a coat."

"Say it."

Weiss looked at her.

It was a long look. She had never seen that face on her before, not in a library or on a roof or a dance floor. It was the face of someone at the end of a diving board.

"You were right," said Weiss.

She said it in the open. She said it in the starlight, in the plain voice, with her hands at her sides and her chin up. She did not look at the sky.

"I decided for you. I wrote *rest* on a sheet and thought it was care. I was taught it was. I'd like to ask you instead. About the stove and the river, and everything, I expect. Before." She stopped, and started again, and her voice dropped. "I'll be bad at it. I should like to be bad at it near you."

The wind went over the roofs.

Blake's ears were forward. She could feel them. She could feel every hair on them stand, to the root, toward the face in front of her, as at a sound; and she did not do anything about it. She let them.

"I'd like to hear it in person next time," she said. "That I'm — I'd like the notice in person. If you go."

"I'd like it too."

"I'll give it. I will."

"Even if it's *I'm going.*"

"Even then."

"I would rather you didn't."

"I know."

"I'm told I can ask."

"You can ask."

Weiss drew breath. She stood very upright. She said, as one hands over a thing across a counter, with both hands:

"Don't leave."

It was the plainest thing she had ever said. It lay in the air between them, small and cold and clear.

Blake looked at her for a long time.

"Ask me tomorrow," she said.

Weiss blinked.

"And the day after." She heard her own voice, thin, strange, a little unsteady. "I might say no. Some day. I might mean it. But I'd like to be asked. Every day. Not— not decided. Asked."

"Every day."

"Yes."

"That's—" Something happened at the corner of Weiss's mouth. "That's extremely inconvenient."

"Yes."

"I'll do it."

"Okay."

"I'll— write it on the schedule."

"Don't write it on the schedule."

"No," said Weiss. "No. I won't." And she laughed.

It was not the snort. It was not any of the ones Blake had heard. It was something rusty and small, that had been shut up a long time and was not used to the air; it came out of her in a breath, in a plume of white, and she put her glove over her mouth, and her eyes above it were wet and bright and appalled.

"Would you like some tea?" she said, through her fingers.

"Yes."

"Made the way I make it, or—"

"The way you make it."

"I'm asking."

"I know," said Blake. "The way you make it."

They went across the yard. The dog went ahead, and stopped at the door of the hut, and looked back. Behind them, in the bunkhouse window, the lamp on the rail burned on, steady and small; and neither of them thought to put it out.
