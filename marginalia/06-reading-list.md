# 6. reading list

Blake came to the north court at six. She came at two minutes past, which was how Weiss knew she had stood outside the gate for two minutes first.

"Good morning."

"Morning."

That was all. They fought for an hour. It was the best they had fought. Weiss noticed that, and did not know what to do with it, and went on noticing it through the whole of her cooldown and the walk back and the shower, which was in her allotted slot, and which she took at a third of her usual speed.

Four mornings of that. On the fifth she tried again.

"Did you sleep?"

"Fine."

"It's still quite warm in the—"

"Yes."

On the sixth morning she did not say anything, and Blake did not say anything, and the silence was no better, but it was not worse, and she thought that might be how it was going to go.

* * *

The library was empty at that hour on the east side. She had not been in it for a week. She had avoided it, if she was honest, because the chair opposite her usual table was a chair, and she knew who had sat in it.

She took the list to the catalogue desk and asked the librarian for a thing she had never asked for before.

He looked at her for a time. He had an orange half peeled in one hand. Then he wrote three call numbers on a slip and added a fourth from memory, and slid it across, and returned to the orange.

She read for six days.

*The Faunus Question,* in three volumes, by a man named Tull, who was against them and said so civilly. *Hearth and Hardship,* which was about households, and had drawings. A short history of the Faunus rights movement with a green cover, written by a professor who clearly meant well. An account of the war, the old one, eighty years back, which she had been taught in school as a story about a king. It was a different story when told from the other side of the table.

She was a good reader. She took notes. She was halfway through the second volume of Tull on the fourth night before she noticed that she had stopped taking them.

The footnotes had caught her. At first they were only footnotes: a ledger, a treaty, a series of dates. Then there was one that said *S.D.C., wage records, first quarter,* and one that said *S.D.C., Mine No. 2, contract labour,* and one that said *N. Schnee, letter to the Board, on the matter of housing,* and she stopped, and read it twice, and then she turned back and read the paragraph it hung from.

*...the Company's founder, who in this respect was no worse than his peers and in others rather better, nonetheless saw no contradiction between a charitable disposition and a deduction for rent...*

Nicholas Schnee was her grandfather. He had died before she was born. There was a portrait of him in the east hall at home. He wore a grey coat and looked as if he were about to say something reasonable.

She closed the book. She looked at the cover, as if she might have picked up the wrong one, and then she put it down square to the edge of the table and sat with her hands on either side of it.

After a time she began again, and read to the end.

* * *

On the seventh day she took four of the books to the dormitory and left them on Blake's pillow, squared into a stack, with a note on the top. She had written the note three times. The version she left said:

*Background. —W.S.*

It looked like the label on a file.

She went out before Blake came back. She sat through two lectures without hearing either. At supper she watched Blake not look at her.

In the morning there was a stack on her own desk.

It was her stack. She knew it by Tull, who had been on top. But there were things in it now: small slips of white paper, pushed into the books at various depths, flagged with a pencil in a hand she knew. Upright, small, educated. Quick.

*Written by a mine owner.* That was the green one, and it was stuck at the front.

*Written by a mine owner's nephew.* Tull.

*Good.* That was stuck in *Hearth and Hardship,* at a drawing of a kitchen.

*Wrong. See p. 212. They didn't leave. They were moved.*

*Be careful of this man.*

And on the top of the stack, folded once, a single sheet.

*You didn't have to.*

Weiss stood with the page in her hand. Nothing happened on her face, which was the usual arrangement. She straightened the stack against the desk, squared the corners, and then took the sheet and read it again. It was five words. She had thought it would take longer.

She put it in the inside pocket of her bag, flat, between two sheets of notepaper.

* * *

For the next several days she practised.

She did it as she did everything. She set a time. In the evenings, after supper, when the others were out, she sat at her desk with her homework and Blake sat in the window with the ribbon off. This was a thing that had begun on its own, on the second night after the books, without any announcement: the ribbon coming off, quietly, as one removes a shoe, and the ears coming up, and the page turning. Neither of them said anything.

Weiss did her sums.

And every so often a sound would come from somewhere, the radiator knocking, someone on the stair, and one of the ears would swing toward it. And something in her would go to jump.

She held still. That was all there was to it. She held still and let the thing in her go to jump and arrive at nothing, and the ear came back to centre, and she wrote down the next figure. She practised it like scales: slowly, and without admiration. The first night, she held still eleven times. The second night, nine. The fourth night she was well into a long division, and looked up to find that the sky had gone dark and the lamp was on, and that she had not once thought about it for what must have been an hour.

She went back to the division. She did not tell anyone. Nothing had happened that anyone could have been told.

* * *

"You can look," said Blake.

It was the sixth night. Ruby and Yang were at a party on the fourth floor that had been described, by Yang, as an "event." Weiss had a pencil in her mouth.

"I have been looking."

"At the wall."

"At the wall behind you."

"That's a kind of looking."

Weiss took the pencil out. "Yes."

"It's rude."

"I know."

Blake had not turned her head. She was reading, or was holding a book in front of her face, in the lamp. Her ears were up and slightly forward, and Weiss, who had been looking at the wall behind them for a fortnight, put the pencil down and looked at them.

They were not like anything. They were ears. The left was pitched a little further forward than the right. The fur at the tip was shorter, and she could see the grain of it, the small whorl at the base, where it met the hair of the head. It moved a fraction. It had been listening to the pencil.

She looked for what she imagined was a long time. Then she looked at the rest of Blake, who was looking at her with a face that gave away nothing at all.

"May I ask something?"

"You may ask."

"Does it hurt, the ribbon?"

"At the hinge." Blake touched, with two fingers, the place where the ear met the skull. "After a few hours."

"Then why—"

"People look."

"They would look at the ears."

"They would look at the ears. And then they would look at me differently."

Weiss said, "I did."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry I stepped back."

Blake closed the book. She did it slowly. It took, Weiss thought, the longest time she had ever seen anyone take to close a book.

"I'd have stepped back."

"No, you wouldn't."

"I might. In your place."

"That's generous."

"It's not generous." The ear on the left turned a degree, away, and returned. "I've been looking at people's faces for six years when they find out. Yours was one of the less bad ones."

"That is not reassuring."

"It's how you measure a thing."

There was a small silence. Weiss, who had not been sure she could, said: "You said, *it's how you tape a wrist.*"

"I did."

"It is a good principle."

Something happened at the corner of Blake's mouth. It lasted a second.

"I said *quite all right,*" said Weiss.

"You did."

"It was unforgivable."

"It was fairly unforgivable."

"I'm sorry. I would like to — is that all right? To say it. Now. Is that—"

"Don't say that one."

"Which?"

"*All right.* You said it then."

Weiss looked at her hands. "What shall I say?"

"*Okay.*"

"That isn't formal."

"Good," said Blake.

Weiss was silent for some time. In the corridor someone laughed and the laugh went away down the stairs. The ear on the left swung toward it and back.

And nothing in her moved.

"Okay," she said.

Blake looked at her a moment longer. Then she opened the book again, and Weiss picked up her pencil, and neither of them said anything at all.
