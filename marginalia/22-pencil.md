# 22. pencil

Weiss gave it to her on the ninth evening, in the office, with both hands.

"I took this from the ruin," she said. "It's yours. I should have given it to you at the door."

It was green cloth, warped at the spine. Blake took it. It weighed what it had always weighed. She had bought it in a shop in Vale with no sign, for three lien, because a woman on the cover was standing in a field of snow with her back to the camera.

"You were busy."

"I was— yes." Weiss's hands had returned to her sides. "I wrote in it."

"I know. I started it."

"Afterward. I mean. A good deal." She looked at the stove. "I'd prefer you read it when I'm not in the room."

Blake considered her. It was a request. It was made in the voice in which Weiss made requests, which was the voice of someone reading a statement into the record; but it was a request, and it was made to her, and she had not often been asked.

"Okay," she said.

She took it across the yard in the dark.

* * *

The night stove was hers. It said so on the schedule, in her own pencil: *Stove (night).* The bunkhouse was warm when she opened the door.

It had been warm every night. For twelve nights she had come in at eleven, from the last round of the traps, and found the barrel red and the kindling laid for morning and no sign of how. She had not said anything. It was a kindness. She had begun to notice, at about the sixth night, that it was also not hers to refuse.

She put that away. She sat on the bunk at the stove end with the lamp on the rail above her and the dog on her feet, and opened the book.

*Flyleaf.* In her own hand, upright, quick, a thing she had written at the counter of the shop, the day she bought it, with no particular feeling: *B. Belladonna. If found, please do not return.*

Under it, in a lighter hand, held steeper:

*Found. Will return in person. Date to be arranged. —W.S.*

She read it twice. She turned the page.

She knew the book. She knew where every one of her own notes was, and what the weather had been when she made it: a margin at page 9, a line at page 31, the long run at page 103 that she had written on the night after an examination, lying on her stomach with the lamp on her back. They were all there, small, neat, upright, a little dry. She had been, she thought, a very plain person. She had thought she was being private.

The other hand began at page 14.

*Disagree.*

*Which part.*

*Attentive.*

She went down it with her thumb, the old argument, the one she had left on the hook. *Birds are not witnesses. Then who's she counting for.* And below it the short pencil line with the curl at the end, which had sat there for a year and a half, unfinished, in the margin of a page.

There was a word beside it now.

*Whoever reads it.*

She sat very still.

She looked at the old stroke, and she looked at the new. She had thought, for all that time, that the hook was nothing. It was the stroke of a pencil that had not finished. She saw now where it would have gone. It was the first line of a *W.* Weiss had stopped, in a dormitory, in a lamp's light, on a night Blake could now place, and put the pencil down.

She turned her hand over on the page.

Under it, a little way down, set apart, there was another line. It was in the same pencil and it was by itself. It was extremely small.

*I signed.*

She read it. She read it again.

She did not know what it meant. She had no idea. It lay in the margin like a coin on a table, and she put her fingertip very lightly on the first word and held it there, and did not write anything under it, and did not take the finger away for a long while.

* * *

It took her four hours.

She read it as she would have read a letter. She went slowly. She went back. The stove ticked and the wind got up and went down and the dog, on her feet, gave a long sigh and shifted. She read the Ingram. She had known it by heart; she read it again.

*More snow. I am told by those who have read my letters that I complain of the weather. I do not complain. I record.*

*Atlas weather also not to be complained of. Snow in the east garden since the 3rd. The dry kind. It squeaks.*

*Counted twelve on the scree, and one that was lame. I do not count the lame one with the twelve. I count him by himself, because he is the one I will look for tomorrow.*

Under it, in her own hand, from the year before, *She counts the lame one by himself.* And under that:

*Yes. I noticed this the first time. I thought you had noticed too.*

She breathed.

*A white-out. Stayed in. Kept the stove banked low to make the wood last. It is a mistake to burn it as though the winter were short.*

*True. Day 17.*

*Wrote to my sister. Did not post it. The post comes once a month and I find I prefer the letters I have not sent; they are so much better than the ones that arrive.*

*I have four. They are very good. They are all very good. —Atlas.*

*Day 31: five.*

*Day 33: six.*

*Day 47: seven. They are getting worse. I think that means I am getting closer.*

She put the back of her hand against her mouth.

It was not a laugh. Or it was, a little, a kind of shake in the ribs, soundless, that she had not felt in a long time. Her ears had gone completely flat. She sat with her fist at her lip and the book on her knee and her shoulders moving, in a cold room with the names of the dead cut into every post around her, and the dog lifted his head and looked at her.

"Don't," she said.

He put his chin down again.

*The birds are changing colour. It is a slow business, a feather at a time. One would not call it a decision.*

*One might. If one were the bird.*

*Today I learned that a ptarmigan will sit tight on the snow while a fox goes past. It is not that she is brave. It is that she is white.*

*I am white. I sat tight. —Atlas, week 11.*

And, lower, a fortnight on, different pencil, in a different pressure:

*I am trying to learn to do something else with it. —Day 36.*

She did not go on for some time.

She got up. She went to the door and opened it and stood in the cold, on the step, in her shirtsleeves, with the book held against her side. It was very clear. The stars were out over the roofs, hard, close, millions of them, and under them the camp lay in its blue snow with one light in it, small and yellow, at the window of the hut across the yard.

She stood there for ten minutes. Then she came in and shut the door and sat down and began again at page 100.

*[Hers, from a year back, slanted hard to the right, the letters dropping:] She lies. She says it's the weather. It isn't.*

And beneath it:

*This one is from the night of the exam. You were tired. Your hand slants right when you are tired. I have been reading your tired pages the most.*

She read that.

She read it with great care, from the beginning of the line to the end. Then she closed the book on her thumb. She held it. She looked at the stove, at the iron barrel and its red seam, and the dog's flank rising, and her own bare knees, and she thought nothing, very clearly, for a long time.

She opened it.

*Page 112. This is where I stopped reading, the night of. I kept the place.*

The waiver lay in the gutter, folded in four. It had been flattened. She could see where a thumbnail had gone down the crease, again and again, until the paper had gone soft as cloth. She unfolded it. The pencil was faint. *The undersigned consent to the waiver of Rule 1 as it applies to one (1) lamp. Low.* Four names. The last, in ink, was in a hand so exactly upright that it looked ruled.

*W. Schnee.*

*Cold enough to hear the river. Cold enough to hear oneself.*

*Day 38. Yes. It talks. I did not expect it to say anything I would want to hear.*

*Thaw. The first drip from the eaves at noon. I stood under it with my face turned up like a fool.*

*Not yet.*

*A year. I have counted two hundred and eleven birds, give or take the lame. I came north to count them and found that I had been counting days. Whether that is a better thing to have counted I cannot say. I shall go down in the morning.*

*Day 44. I have been counting days. I had thought I was counting something else.*

She sat with the book open on her knee.

On the back flyleaf, the last page, there was the final note. It was in the first hand and was dated early. It was written in ink.

*If you are reading this, either I gave it to you, or it fell into your hands, which would mean something had happened to me. I should like it to be the former. —W.S., Day 3.*

* * *

She took out the pencil.

It was the stub that had been on the table in a row, squared to the edge. It was half the length it had been. She turned it in her fingers. She did not think about it. She put the point to the flyleaf, beneath the last line, and for a time did nothing at all.

Then she wrote, small and upright, a clear dry diagonal, in her own tired hand:

*The former.*

She looked at it. It was two words. She thought that it would do.

She turned back to page 14 and set the pencil by the old hook, beside the new word, and in the margin she wrote another.

*Reading.*

Then she closed the book. She closed it softly and put it under her pillow and lay down, in her clothes, with her boots on, and the dog moved up onto her chest, and turned round, and settled, and she lay with one hand on his flank and her eyes on the beams.

She slept without meaning to. She slept through the stove, and the wind, and the dog's long sighs, and she did not wake until the morning, when the knock came, and the tune, eight bars, coming up the yard.

* * *

She was at the door with the book.

"It's not much of a return," said Blake.

Weiss looked at it. She looked at her face, and the hand, and the cover, and she took it, and for a moment neither of them let go. Then Blake did.

"I'll read it later," said Weiss.

"Okay."

"I— thank you."

"Breakfast," said Blake. She went past her into the cold. In the crook of her elbow, under the grey coat, the dog had come out from the bunkhouse and was leaning against her knee.

Weiss did not open the book. She stood in the yard, in the clean light, with it held to her chest in both arms, as a child holds a thing it has been given to carry. She stood there some while.

When she did finally turn the pages, in the evening, alone, the first thing she found was the flyleaf.

*The former.*

And she made a sound that the dog came to the door to look at.
