# 20. arrangements

Weiss's heartbeat was not regular.

That was the first thing Blake heard, standing in the middle of the floor with the snow coming off her in sheets. It went fast and then not, with a catch in it, like a drawer that sticks. She had listened to it for forty minutes once, across a dark room, and it had been kept as a clock is kept. This was not that. It was quick and unfinished. It was going on without permission.

She did not say so. She stood where she had been put.

"I'll take your coat," said Weiss.

She had come across the room as one crosses ice. She stopped a yard off. In the lamp, with the wind shut out, she was thinner than she had been, with a face that had gone all bone and eye, and her hair was down. Blake had seen it down once, at dawn, frozen. It hung to the middle of her back, loose, and not quite clean, and it was the first thing in the world to be exactly as she had remembered it.

"May I?"

"Yes."

Weiss undid the buttons. Blake's own hands would not do it. They had stopped being hands somewhere on the gap and were two things at the ends of her arms. She stood and let it be done, one button, two, a pause at the third where the cloth had frozen to the thread; and the left hand that worked at it was in a glove.

It was black. It was wool, with the tips cut off at the first knuckle. It was a little large, and pulled up under the cuff.

Blake looked at it.

She looked at it for some seconds. The cook had told her. She had been told in a warm kitchen, over soup, and she had thought she understood, and it was nothing at all like seeing it: the stiff fingers, bare at the ends, going about their business at her collar, in the lamplight, on a hand that was not hers.

The hand stopped.

"It was cold," said Weiss.

"It's snowing."

"Yes."

"I know," said Blake.

The coat came off her shoulders. It weighed a good deal, soaked. Weiss hung it on the peg by the stove and spread it with the flat of her hand, and kept her back to the room for a little longer than was necessary to spread a coat.

* * *

She was told to sit.

She sat on the chair with one arm, and her boots were taken off by someone else, and her feet put to the stove. There was a blanket. There was tea, in a tin mug, black, smoking, with the smell of burnt sweetness, and she held it in both hands and the pain began.

It came in slowly. It began in the fingers and went up the bones, and she did not make a sound. She watched the dog, who lay across Weiss's feet and breathed.

"Pockets," said Weiss. "Papers dry flat."

She did it without waiting for an answer. She stood at the desk with her back to Blake and emptied the pockets of the grey coat one at a time, and set each thing on a clean sheet of newsprint, in a row.

Blake watched her do it. It was how she did everything. She laid it out the way she had laid out her shoes, and her rules, and her books: squared to the edge, spaced, each thing apart from the next.

A ferry stub. A rail pass, soft with damp. A smooth black stone from the south strand, the size of a hen's egg, which had no purpose and which she had not been able to leave. Fourteen lien. The tin of knife-oil. A pencil stub. A cloth, folded, with a hem of green thread, and in the corner a small embroidered *S.*

Weiss held the cloth for a second longer than the rest.

She did not look round. Her thumb passed once over the stitching. "Dorrit," she said.

"Is that her name?"

"Yes."

"She gave me soup."

"Yes," said Weiss. "She would have."

She laid it down. She lifted the last thing from the pocket, a curl of leather, dark, dry, brittle, and turned it over, and put it, without seeming to think, to her nose.

It was the peel of an orange.

Blake had eaten the orange in a freight shed in Mantle, on the nineteenth day, out of a net bag, from a man who sold them by the one. She had kept the peel. She could not have said why.

Weiss set it down. She did it quickly, with the tips of her fingers. The back of her neck had gone a colour.

There was one thing more, a cheap notebook, with a marbled card cover that had been wet and dried. She put it at the end of the row, square, closed. She did not open it. She did not look at it. She laid her palm, for an instant, flat on the top, as one does with a child, to settle it, and took the palm away.

It was a small thing. Blake noticed that she had noticed.

* * *

"You're welcome to stay."

It was Weiss, at the desk, with her hands folded, forty minutes on. The stove ticked. The room had begun to be warm in earnest and Blake had stopped shaking, which she had not known she was doing.

"Of course. For as long as you—"

She stopped.

It went out into the room and lay there. Blake heard it. She heard the cool, composed, hostess quality of it, the little cadence at the end, the gracious open hand. She had heard it before. She had heard it in the dark, at two in the morning, over a puddle on a floor.

*That's quite all right.*

Weiss had heard it too. Blake could tell by the heart. It missed a beat, and went on, and Weiss's mouth opened and closed on the next thing, which was evidently an apology, and was evidently going to be worse.

"Thank you," said Blake.

She said it level. It came out as it always did.

The silence was very long. In it she could hear the wind, the stove, the dog's breath, the small sound a person makes when she has swallowed something she wanted to say.

"I'll take the bunkhouse," said Blake.

"It isn't heated."

"I'd like it."

"There are twenty-four beds."

"That's plenty."

"Not the third from the door." Weiss said it very quietly, to the desk. "That one's — taken."

"By who?"

"Someone's husband."

Blake looked at her.

It was nothing like what she had been expecting. She had been expecting a number of things, a shut door, a speech. She saw a girl in a coachman's coat, with her hair coming down, sitting with her hands in her lap and her jaw set, and she saw, at last, that the cheekbones were not only thin. They were sharp as a thing that had been whittled.

"Okay," she said.

Weiss nodded. She said, "I'll give you a lamp."

* * *

The bunkhouse was a long dark roof with the wind on it.

She went across the yard with the lamp and the dog, who had decided without consultation to come, and a blanket over her arm. It was ten yards. It took a long time. The storm had thinned to a hard fine dry snow, driven flat, and it hissed on the lamp glass and on the dog's back, and above her the sky had cleared in places and showed, through the rents, the sharp blue-black of the cold.

The door was tied with a thong. She untied it.

Twelve beds a side, in tiers. The air stood in the room like water in a well. She went down the aisle with the lamp held up, and as it moved the dark moved back along the walls and showed her what was on them.

Names.

She stopped.

They were on the posts and the rails and the end-boards, as high as her chin and as low as her knee. Cut. Scratched. Burnt. She put out her hand and drew a thumb along the nearest, an *A* and a *V,* with a drift of dust in the grooves. Further down: *D.Q. WAS HERE AND WAS RIGHT.* She held the lamp to it. She read it slowly, in a voice that did not leave her throat.

She had a notebook in her bag. It was ruled in columns.

She stood in the cold with the lamp at the level of her face and looked at the wall, and understood that she had crossed a sea and a continent and climbed a mountain to arrive at a room that was the same as the one she had been carrying. She had not known the lists could be so long.

She chose the bunk at the stove end.

She did not light the stove. There was wood in the bin, split, and a box of matches, and she stood in front of it for some time with her hand on the lid. Then she took her hand away. She spread the blanket on the tick, and lay down on her back in her clothes, with the lamp turned low and her boots on, and the dog came up and settled across her feet.

The ceiling was close. The cold came down through the blanket. She lay and looked at the beams, and she listened to the wind, and under it, across the yard, through two walls, to a heart that had not slowed.

She thought: *I will not sleep.*

She slept almost at once.

* * *

She woke to heat.

It was full dark. The lamp was out. She lay without moving, with her eyes open on the beams, and knew in the first second that the room had changed. The air was soft. There was a ticking at the end of the building, an iron barrel creaking as it took the warmth, and a glow around its door and along the floor in a thin red line.

Someone had lit the stove.

She had heard nothing. That was impossible. She had slept through a door and a stride and a match, who had never slept through a footstep in her life, and she lay and went over it, and came to the end, and it was as plain as a wall. She had been too tired. That was all. She had not been so tired for years.

And then, outside, she heard it.

It was a very thin sound. It came from the yard, a long way off, going away: a little thread of tune, hummed low, hardly more than breath. Three notes. A rest. A phrase that climbed. It went across the snow toward the other building and grew fainter and was gone, and she lay with her ears up, and listened to it all the way.

She did not move for a long time.

She was thinking of the way, in the last stack of a library, she had once held a door shut against weather. She was thinking that she had hummed that tune for months, in a country where she knew no one, with her hands busy, and had said she did not know where it was from.

She thought that it was strange for a person to hum, in the dark, in a gale, crossing a yard, when no one was awake to hear.

She did not finish the thought.

The stove ticked. The room was warm. After some time she turned on her side, with her face to the barrel, and the dog shifted, and she slept.

* * *

In the morning there was a knock.

It came at the door. It came after a sound, a little tune, hummed outside in the cold, not far off, and coming nearer, three notes, a rest, a phrase. It went on for exactly eight bars. Then the knock.

"Breakfast," said Weiss, through the wood. "There's porridge. It's not good."

"I'll come."

"I've made a list."

She opened the door.

Weiss stood on the step in the coachman's coat with a sheet of paper in her hand. The cold stood round her in the doorway, bright, shaking with light. She had pinned her hair up. She had not slept, that was plain, but she had been careful about it, and the care sat on her like a coat.

The paper was a schedule.

It was ruled in columns. *Wood. Water. Stove. Radio. Meals.* Across the top, in her best hand, were two names, and under each a series of small clean entries, with times.

Blake took it. She read it down.

Under *Blake,* for the first three days, in every row, in the same neat black ink, there was one word. It was repeated like a stitch.

*Rest.*

She looked at it for some while.

"I thought—" said Weiss.

"It's good."

"It's only for the—"

"It's good," said Blake. "Thank you."

It was not a thing she would have done herself. She noticed that. She stood on the step in the cold with the paper in her hand and she noticed it, as one notices a stone in a shoe, and she put it away. It was a kindness. She was so tired that she could have lain down on the snow.

She folded the sheet, and put it in her pocket, and went to eat.
