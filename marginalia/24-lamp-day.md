# 24. lamp day

She told her the night before.

She had put it off for as long as a person can put a thing off in a room with two chairs. It was past ten. The stove ticked. Blake was across the desk with the cheap notebook shut in front of her and a pencil in her fingers that she was not using, and the dog was between them on the floor with his nose on his paws.

"I need to tell you something before tomorrow."

Blake looked up.

"It isn't long." Weiss had her hands flat on the desk, on either side of the log. She had arranged what to say and then, at the last, discarded the arrangement, which she did not often do. "My father is the Schnee Dust Company. I don't mean that as a figure of speech. In the summer, after Beacon, he gave me a binder to read. It had a table in it. On page seven, there was a line about Number Four. It said *settled.*"

Blake did not move.

"A man came from Public Relations with a folder. He said they were letters of condolence, and that they would mean more from the family. I asked to see one. He showed me a master, and it was very decent. I signed thirty-one of them. I signed where he pointed. I didn't read the second page." She heard her own voice go on, level, even, clear. It was the voice in which she would have recited. "It said that the claims had been reviewed and found to be without standing. It said that nothing would be paid. It was dated nine days after the roof came down. I have read it since. I've read it a number of times."

The stove ticked.

"I found out at dinner. My father said *of course.* He said it was easier to receive a refusal from a girl who was sorry than from a lawyer." She stopped, and made herself go on. "He was right. I should like you to know that he was right. It was easier. I signed in a good hand."

She sat with her palms flat. She did not look at the pencil, or the dog, or the stove.

"That's all," she said. "That's what I did."

"I know," said Blake.

Weiss's head came up.

"It's on page fourteen."

She had forgotten. She had actually forgotten. It rose in her like a dropped stone: the small pencil line under the hooked stroke, two words, in her own hand, that she had put there in the dark and given away on the ninth evening with both hands. She heard her breath go out of her.

"You read it. You — you didn't say."

"It was yours to say."

"You could have asked."

"I didn't want to." Blake turned the pencil in her fingers. "I thought, if I asked, you'd tell me because I'd asked."

Weiss looked at her.

She had thought that she knew every way a person could be kind. She had learned a good many. She had thought she had the list.

"Tomorrow they come," she said.

"Tove said."

"They'll say it to my face."

"Yes."

"I'll let them." She swallowed. "I thought— you might prefer to stay in the bunkhouse. It's your own people, you see, and it needn't be— you needn't be in the middle of it. I'd quite understand if you—"

"I'm coming."

"I only meant—"

"I know what you meant." Blake did not raise her voice. Her ears were forward; her eyes, in the lamp, were level, and gold, and entirely steady. "I'm coming."

There was a short silence.

"Okay," said Weiss.

* * *

Tove came at noon.

She came on skis, with the sled, and on the sled, packed in straw, in two long wooden crates, were the lamps.

They were the old ones, the pit lamps, brass, dented, with a hook at the top and a thick glass chimney blackened at the rim. She lifted them out one at a time and stood them in rows on the snow, thirty-one of them and a few over, and she went along the rows with a rag and a can of oil. Weiss watched her fill them. She did it with the exactness of a woman who has done it before, and who does not intend to be hurried, and who has not at any point asked for help.

"Tonight a Schnee'll be there," she said, to a lamp.

"Yes."

"You'll stand at the back. If any of them has something to say, he'll say it to your face. Then he's done." She screwed a cap down. "It's the rule. He gets to say it once."

"I understand."

"You don't, but it's all right."

Weiss's hand moved at her side. Tove saw it, and her mouth turned down at one corner.

"I mean it's the usual," she said. "It's what I say. It's not a thing I say at you."

"I know."

"Hm." She held a lamp out. "You'll light one."

"May I?"

"It's not an honour," said Tove Dahl. "It's a lamp."

* * *

At four the light began to go. At five they came.

She had been out on the step of the hut since half past four. She had put on the coachman's coat and her one good scarf, and had stood there with the dog at her knee, looking down the track. Blake was at her left. She had said nothing about being there. She had come across the yard with her hands in the pockets of the grey coat and taken up a position, a little behind, as she did, and stood.

The sky was a dark clear green over the hill. Down the valley, where the track came out of the trees, there was a point of yellow.

Then another.

They came up through the dusk in a line. First two, close together; then three; and then so many that she lost the count, a string of small lights swaying a little as they moved, going up and down with the walk of the bearers. They came slowly. There was no talking. There was no sound at all but the dry squeak of snow under a great many feet, and the faint tick of skis, and, once, a child coughing, and being hushed.

She counted eleven.

Eleven people, and a dozen lamps, for some had carried two. They came into the yard and stopped. They were Faunus, mostly, and old. There was a man with a stick, thin, grey-bearded, bent at the neck, with the ears of a stoat; two women in their fifties, a pair of sisters by the look of them, in matching shawls; a boy of sixteen with a sullen face and small curling horns. Three children, closely wrapped, with one lamp between them, held by the smallest, who had to use both hands. An old woman with a walking frame and a human husband. They stood in the blue snow and the lamplight and looked at the camp, the long roofs, the dead tram, the stone.

They did not look at the hut.

Tove stood at the head of them. She had changed her coat. She was wearing a long dark one, buttoned to the throat, and a black scarf over her head with her ears coming through it, white, tipped with black, quite upright.

She walked past the hut without turning.

They went by in single file. Weiss stood on the step with a lamp in her hand, which Tove had given her, and watched them pass. A woman looked at her. A boy looked at her. The old man with the stick turned his head slowly as he went by and gave her a long, level, appraising look, as one gives a tradesman.

Blake said, low: "Now."

They went down the step and fell in at the end.

* * *

They set the lamps at the foot of the wall.

Each did it in turn. They crouched, or knelt, or bent stiffly, and set it on the snow in front of the new stone, touching the one before, so that the row grew along the base from the left, a low line of yellow light, with the stone above it going gold. Weiss watched from the back. When her turn came she crossed the snow with the lamp in both hands and set it at the end of the line, and straightened.

The wall was warm-coloured now. It looked almost kind. The brass plate shone. *THE COMPANY REGRETS.*

Tove took a paper out of her coat.

She did not unfold it. She held it against her chest in both hands, and looked up at the wall, and said in the voice in which she said everything:

"Anselm Voe."

"Come up," said the voices.

It was low. It was ragged. It was eleven people in the cold, saying it at different speeds; the children's voices came a half beat behind. It went across the yard and was gone.

"Dara Quil."

"Come up."

She stood at the back with her lamp at her feet, and the dog against her boot, and heard it go. She did not know where to look. She looked at the stone. It went on: *Pettigrew Oakes. Emil Voss. Corin Hale.* The syllables fell one at a time into the dark, and were answered, each, with the same two words. There was a rhythm to it. It was the rhythm of something done a long time; it went down through the soles of her feet. *Ilse Brandt. Joss Tenney. Linus Pell.*

By the tenth she had her lips moving.

She said it with them. She did not decide to. A name came, and the answer came, and her mouth made the shape of it before she knew. *Come up.* She heard it small and cracked in her throat, a whisper, drowned. *Come up.* She said it again. It was the first time in her life she had said it to anyone.

On her left, in the dark, so quietly she could barely hear, Blake said it too.

*Tomas Harrow.*

There was a gap.

It was the length of a breath. It was not long. It was the sort of gap in which a person decides.

"He told them Thursday," said someone, in the front. A man. The thin one with the stick. "Thursday, Thursday. He told them it for a month."

"He went in with them," said Tove.

"I know where he went."

"He went in on the fortieth to look at the props himself, and he took the roof the same as the rest." She did not turn her head. She held the paper to her chest. "Tomas Harrow."

The silence lasted three seconds.

"Come up," said the thin man, roughly, in a voice like a saw.

"Come up," said the others.

It went on. It took nineteen minutes by the watch she did not look at. The last name was the thirty-first; it was said by the old woman, and not in the dry voice but a very different one, lower, with the edge gone out of it, as of a person speaking across a table in the dark.

"Halvard Dahl."

"Come up," said the yard.

Tove folded the paper. She put it in her coat. She stood for a time with her back to them, still, and her ears were down, flat to her head, and the light of thirty-one lamps shook on the white fur.

* * *

"And her."

It was the man with the stick. He had turned round.

He stood at the front of the little crowd, in the lamplight, with the wind in his grey beard, leaning on the stick with both hands. He had not raised his voice. It was a voice that had been used for shouting down a shaft, and it carried.

"What's she doing here?"

"She lives here," said Tove.

"She's a Schnee."

"Yes."

"She signed it." The stick came up and pointed, across the yard. "Thirty-one letters, in her own hand. My sister had one. *Without standing.* In a good hand. She framed it, my sister. She put it on the wall to look at." The stick shook. "And you let her stand with us."

"I've let her stand," said Tove. "She's said it. To me, plain. I asked her for the words and she gave them."

"Then let her say them here."

Nobody moved.

Weiss felt it. She felt all eleven faces turn, slowly, like a field of something in a wind, in the lamplit dusk; she felt a child's eyes. She felt the dog lean against her.

Blake's hand came and rested, for one moment, flat against the small of her back, a light pressure, a thumb's width. It was gone almost at once.

She walked forward.

She went through the people. They made a passage. She went to the front, past the lamps, to where the light was fullest, and stopped, three yards from the man, and stood with her hands at her sides.

She had no voice.

She had always had it. She had been able to put a note into the roof of a hall. She stood in front of an old man with a stick and opened her mouth, and what came out was a thing so small and rough that she hardly knew it for her own.

"I signed it."

"Louder."

"I signed it." It came a little clearer. "I did not read the second page. I was not asked to, and I didn't ask. That is not an excuse. My father decided not to pay, and I put my name to it." She breathed. "The roof was reported on the thirty-first and the thirty-sixth and the thirty-eighth and the thirty-ninth and the fortieth. I have the log. I have read it. I'll go on reading it."

The wind blew. The lamps leaned.

"Words," said the man.

"Yes."

"You've no more?"

"No. I haven't any better."

He looked at her for a long, long time. He had small, red-rimmed eyes, deep in his head, with a gleam in them, like water at the bottom of a well; they went over her once, from boots to hair, and rested on her face. She did not look away. It was the hardest thing she had ever done, and it did not take any effort at all.

"No," he said. "I don't suppose you have."

He turned his head.

"And you." His eyes had found the figure at her shoulder. "Belladonna's girl."

"Yes."

"You stand with her?"

"I stand where I am," said Blake.

The man grunted. It was not a sound of approval. It was not a sound of anything. It was the sound a person makes who has been given a plain answer and finds, to his annoyance, that he has no use for the rest of his speech.

"Hm," he said.

In the second row, one of the two women in matching shawls had turned her back. She did it slowly, deliberately, and stood facing the dark, with her shoulders squared; and nobody said anything to her, and nobody said anything about her, and Weiss thought: *That, too.*

"Done?" said Tove.

The man leaned on his stick.

"Done," he said.

* * *

It was Tove who began it.

She did not say that she was going to. She lifted her head, and set her feet, and a note came out of her like a cart drawn out of a ditch. It was low and cracked. It was a hoarse flat old-woman's voice, entirely without art, and it went out over the lamps and stopped.

*Lamp's gone low and the shift is done—*

A second voice came in under it, the thin man's. Then the sisters. Then the boy with the horns, unwillingly, with a scowl, in a baritone that cracked in the middle. Then the children, in a thread of pure piping sound. They sang it ragged, out of time, a little flat. They sang it as a thing sung every year, for no one, by people who did not care how it sounded.

*—the cage comes up to the morning sun.*

Weiss stood in the front.

She knew every note. She had known them since before she could read. She had lain in a dark room in a bed with high white rails, and a voice had come out of the dark, warm and low and slightly out of tune, and a finger had gone into a notch in an ear. She had been seven. She had been carried.

*Tell the dark you're not back yet—*

She opened her mouth.

It came out of her without any instruction. It came from below the ribs, from the place she had been taught, and she did not look at the back wall. She did not pick a spot at the level of the eye. She sang to the lamps, which were low, and warm, and in front of her, and a row of them along the bottom of a wall; and her voice, her bright cool centred voice, went out over the heads of the miners' families in the cold, and lay on the snow among their own.

*Come up, come up—*

On the next note it cracked.

It was not a little crack. It went right through. The pitch dropped a third and split and went into breath, and for one terrible instant she heard it all as if from outside: the ugliness of it, the raw rasp, a voice that has been used to hold up a roof.

Nobody turned round.

She had been waiting for that. She had waited for it all her life. It did not come.

*—the kettle's set.*

She finished the line. It came out thin. She sang it through to the end, in her broken voice, with her face wet and a good deal of it running down her neck into the scarf, and she did not wipe it, because it would have been an admission, and then she thought: *It is one.*

At her left, so low that she could barely hear it, someone was humming.

She had not known that Blake would. She did not know the words. She was humming it as she had hummed it for a year, three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed, with her hands in the pockets of the grey coat and her eyes on the lamps. She was a little flat in the middle. She held the top a trifle too long.

In the second row the old woman with the walking frame had looked round.

She was old, and small, and her ears were nearly bald with age. She looked at Weiss steadily out of two filmed eyes. Then she nodded. She did it once, with her chin, slowly, as at a child who has finally put its foot down on the right stair.

* * *

They went down to the mess hall afterward for the stew.

It was Tove's. She had brought it in a pot on the sled and it had been warming on the range since three, and the whole long room smelled of onion and barley and smoke. Nobody asked Weiss to come. She stood at the door of the yard until they had all gone in, and then she did not go in.

Tove came out.

She stood on the step with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the row of lamps along the wall, which were going on burning, thirty-one of them, in the clear cold dark, each with its small steady flame.

"Cracked," she said.

"Yes."

"Good."

That was all.

She went in. The door closed on a rush of warmth and voices and the clink of spoons.

Weiss stood in the yard. She was shaking. It had begun without her notice, and she could not stop it; the long muscles of her back, her knees. Her face was stiff with salt. She put her hands into her sleeves and stood there.

After a time she felt, at her left, a presence.

It did not touch her. It stood a few inches off. She felt the cold coming off the grey coat and under it a warmth, faint, steady, like that off a stove that has been banked. A hand moved in the dark between them, and did not reach, and hung, at the level of her own, a hair's breadth off, as a hand hangs over a flame.

Neither of them spoke.

Behind them, in the light, along the foot of the wall, the lamps burned on. They would burn till morning. In the morning there would be thirty-one rings in the snow, and a smell of oil, and nothing else. But that was the morning. It was a long way off.

She did not move her hand.
