# 23. from behind

"You sleeping?" said Tove.

It was Thursday, and she had just put down the fish. She said it to Blake, across the table, in the flat offhand voice she used for everything, and Blake opened her mouth.

"She sleeps badly," said Weiss. "I've been taking the night stove."

Blake closed her mouth.

Tove looked from one of them to the other. Her ears moved. She did not say anything. She took the cloth off her sack and shook it, and the flour went up in a small white cloud and hung in the light, and she looked at it for some time.

"Mm," she said.

That was all. She took her silver and went. And Blake sat at the table with her hands round a cold cup, and thought: *It's true.* It was true. She did sleep badly. The stove had been lit every night, before she woke. It was a kindness. It was also an answer, given on her behalf, in front of a third person, in a tone that closed the question, and she noticed a small hard grain in the middle of her chest, like a seed in a fruit, and she put it away.

She was good at putting things away. It was most of what she knew.

* * *

It was a Tuesday, after supper, on the seventy-fourth day.

She was at the basin. It was an enamel one, chipped, on the shelf under the window, with a kettle beside it, and she had her sleeves up and the tap of hot water running into it from the kettle's spout in a thin steaming thread. The plates were stacked. The dog was under the desk. Weiss was at the stove, behind her, with the heavy pot, and had been for some time, doing something with a ladle.

Outside, the light was going. The window had gone from white to violet, and her own face lay in it faintly, over the snow, with the two ears.

She was thinking about nothing at all. That was the extraordinary thing. For a quarter of an hour she had been thinking of nothing; the warm water, the plate, the sound of the ladle; it had been the nearest thing to rest she had had since the spring. She turned a plate in her hands and rinsed it.

There was no hum.

She did not notice. She would think about that afterward, a great deal. She did not notice, because it was not there to be noticed; the room was quiet in the ordinary way, the ladle, the stove, the pot set down with a clank; and then, with no sound of approach, no tread on the boards, a hand came down on her shoulder from behind.

It was a light hand. It was warm, through the cloth.

The plate was in her hand. And then it was not.

* * *

There was a gap.

She would try, later, to find the edges of it, and not be able to. It was a cut in the film. She was at the sink, with the water running, and then she was on the other side of the room with her back to the stove and her left forearm across a collarbone and her right hand on the hilt at her hip with the blade half out of its sheath, and something was falling, in pieces, on the boards. It made a noise like laughter.

She was holding a person against the wall.

The person was Weiss.

She was white. Her head was back against the planks and her chin was up, and her left hand had come up of its own accord and was spread, palm out, in the air beside her face, as one raises a hand to a horse. Her right wrist was in Blake's fist. She had not made a sound.

Her eyes were wide.

They were not on the blade. They were not on Blake's other hand, or on the arm at her throat. They were on Blake's face. They were steady. She had never in her life seen anyone look so entirely unafraid, and so afraid, and it took her a moment to see that it was not for herself.

"Blake," said Weiss.

She said it softly. She said it in a voice you would use to a child at the edge of a roof.

She let go.

She did it all at once. The arm came away from the collar, and the fist from the wrist, and the hand from the hilt, so that the blade went home in the sheath with a small sound, and she stepped back. And back. Her heel found the leg of the table. There was a plate in forty pieces on the floor between them, and a smear of water going dark in the boards.

The wrist was already red.

It had the print of her fingers on it, white at the centres, a ring. It would be blue by morning.

"It's—" said Weiss.

"Don't."

She did not remember crossing the room. She did not remember the door. There was the cold, and the yard, and the snow, and the sky coming down in a vast violet bowl over the camp with the first star in it, and she was walking, and then she had her back against the bunkhouse wall and was sitting on a chopping block with her hands on her knees, and she was shaking, and could not stop.

* * *

Her hands were steady.

She looked at them. They lay on her knees in the blue light, open, the fingers slightly curved, and they did not shake at all. It was the rest of her. It was the long muscles of her thighs and the cords of her back. She looked at her hands and thought, quite clearly: *They are not mine.*

It was a thing she had thought before. She had thought it in the water, on the south strand, in the shallows. She sat on a block of wood in the snow with her ears flat to her skull and watched the dusk come on, and she thought it as one remembers a phone number: *They are not mine, they are not mine, they are not mine.*

She did not know how long she sat.

A door opened across the yard.

She heard it. She heard the boots, and the pause, and the slow crunch as they came out; and she heard them stop, a long way off, at the edge of the light from the window. They did not come on.

"May I sit here?"

It was Weiss's voice. It came across the snow, level, clear, not raised. She was somewhere near the woodpile.

"No."

"I'll sit there, then."

There was a rustle, and a small creak. A pause. Nothing else.

Blake looked at the snow. It was a hard blue. A little wind came down the yard and shifted the surface and went on.

She did not look round. She could hear her without looking: a figure on the woodpile, twenty feet away, upright, with her hands in her lap. She could hear the heart, quick and clipped. She could hear a breath. She could hear, faintly, a click, which was a pair of teeth shutting.

Ten minutes. Twenty.

"You'll freeze," said Blake.

"Yes. I have the coat."

"You haven't got gloves."

"I have gloves."

"They don't match."

There was a pause. It lasted just long enough.

"No," said Weiss.

It was a ridiculous thing to say. It was ridiculous to say it at all, and she heard it, and something in her chest gave a little, without leave, like a rope paid out a foot. She put the heel of her hand against her eye.

"You can come closer."

The boots came across the snow. They stopped a yard off, on her left, and she heard a skirt rustle and a knee creak as someone lowered herself onto the cold ground. A yard. It was a clean, scrupulous yard, measured as one measures a bolt of cloth. She could have put out her hand, and not reached.

They sat.

* * *

"I forgot," said Weiss.

"I know."

"I put my hand—"

"I know where you put it."

"I won't."

"It's not that." The words came out of her stiff, as if they were being taken from a drawer that had not been opened. "Don't say you won't. Say that you'll tell me."

"Tell you what?"

"That you're there. Before."

Weiss was quiet.

"You already do it," said Blake.

"I—"

"You hum."

It lay between them on the snow. She did not look at her. She watched a thread of wind move across the yard, and the dog, who had come out and was standing a little way off with his head on one side, looking from one of them to the other.

"You noticed," said Weiss.

"I didn't know until it wasn't there."

"I hadn't meant—" Weiss stopped. "I didn't want to make a thing of it. If I said it, I thought it would be a thing. And you'd have to — have it. Be someone who had it."

"It was a thing already."

"Yes."

There was a long pause. Blake looked at her knees.

"He used to come up behind me," she said. "He'd put a hand here." She touched the back of her own neck with two fingers, flat, below the hairline. She did not look to see how it was received. "He thought it was gentle."

Silence.

It went on, and she waited for it to be filled. She waited for the sound of a word, the rustle of a gesture, someone's hand coming toward her in the dark. She had had it from others. She had had it from her mother, from Yang, from a boy with a tail and a string bag of fruit. It was meant well. It was always meant well. It came toward her like weather.

Nothing came.

"Thank you for telling me," said Weiss.

"Don't thank me."

"Then I won't."

"Don't—" She stopped. She was cold. Her face felt stiff. "Just hum."

"I will."

"On purpose."

"I was doing it on purpose." A pause. "I'll do it more on purpose."

A sound came out of Blake that was not a laugh. It was something nearer a cough, and her shoulders did something, and she bent over her knees and was quiet.

When she sat up the sky had gone nearly black.

* * *

"May I ask you something?"

"Yes."

"Is there someone I should be watching for?"

She had known it was coming. It had been coming since the door.

"No. He's dead."

"I know. I didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant." She breathed. "It doesn't stop."

"No."

"It's a different thing from the person. I thought it would be the same thing. I thought when he was gone it would just— I'd go to sleep. I'd stop doing the door." She heard her own voice, thin and dry and light. "I check the door."

"Yes. I've seen."

"You don't say."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you'd know I'd seen," said Weiss.

It was such a plain answer that she turned her head.

She was sitting a yard off with her knees up, in the grey coat, with her chin on her fists and the dusk behind her. She was looking at Blake. She had a face that she was holding still, and the effort showed, as the grain shows through a coat of paint.

Her right wrist lay on her knee. The cuff of the glove had slipped. There was a ring round the bone, now, dark as a plum.

"I hurt you."

"It's a wrist."

"Weiss."

"It's a wrist. I have two."

"I'll—" She got up. Her legs held. "I'll get the tape."

* * *

It was in the first-aid box on the shelf, in the office, where it had always been. She took it down. She sat at the desk. Weiss sat opposite, with her wrist out on the blotter, palm up, in the lamplight, and the dog under the table put his chin on her boot.

She did not say *May I.* She had not meant not to. She held out her own hand, palm up, and waited. Weiss laid the wrist in it.

It was warm. It was thin. The skin on the inside was almost translucent, with the blue lines of the veins in it, and the bone stood up on the outer side like the knob of a door. She turned it once, to see how it moved.

She began to wind the tape. Twice round, and across, and round.

"You hold your breath," said Weiss, "while you do that."

Blake's hands stopped.

She looked up. Weiss's face was perfectly grave. There was a smudge of ash on the cheekbone, under the scar. Her eyes were red-rimmed and extremely bright, and her mouth was held still at the corners, with a care that was nearly heroic.

Blake let out her breath.

"Does this hurt?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"That is not," said Weiss, "how you reassure someone."

"It's how you tape a wrist."

She smoothed the end flat with her thumb.

There was a pause. It went on, and in it she watched the corner of Weiss's mouth give way, a little, then more, until it had lost its argument entirely. A sound came out. It was not the snort. It was a smaller, shakier thing, a long unsteady breath through the nose, with a break in the middle of it. She put her head down on her arm, on the desk, and her shoulders shook.

Blake sat still.

She did not touch her. She kept the taped wrist in her two hands, lightly, as one holds a bird, and listened to it go on. It went on a good while. She did not say anything at all.

Then it stopped.

"Thank you," said Weiss, into her sleeve.

"Don't."

"For the tape."

"Oh," said Blake.

* * *

At eleven, in the bunkhouse, in the dark, she lay on her back with her hands folded and listened.

The door of the hut opened across the yard. She heard the step on the stair, and the snow, and the stride coming nearer. And then, from some thirty feet out, in a clear carrying voice that could have been heard in the next valley, and probably was, came eight bars: three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed. They were pitched at an exact and deliberate volume. They were sung straight at the door.

The dog got up off her feet, and left the room.
