# 31. thaw

The first drip came at noon.

She was on the roof of the hut with a hammer in her hand and a mouthful of nails, and she heard it before she knew what she was hearing: a small clear tick, from the north eave, like a finger on glass. She stopped. The sun had been on the iron for three hours. The whole slope of it shone wetly, and a skin of water lay over the old snow along the ridge, thin as breath, and from the edge, at the end of a long slow bead, a drop hung and grew heavy and let go.

*Tick.*

Below her, in the yard, Weiss had her face turned up.

She had been holding the ladder. She stood at the foot of it with her hands at her sides, in the coachman's coat, with her cropped hair blown up at one side, and she was looking at the eave. Her mouth was a little open. The second drop fell. It struck her on the cheekbone, just under the scar, and ran down.

She did not move. She stood with her eyes shut and her face tipped to the sky and let the third one come.

Blake took the nails out of her mouth.

She had read the page. She knew what was written beside it, in the margin of the book, in a steep light hand. *Thaw. The first drip from the eaves at noon. I stood under it with my face turned up like a fool.* And under that, two words: *Not yet.*

She put the hammer down on the ridge, carefully, so as not to drop it on anyone.

She thought, quite clearly, in the sun: *Now.*

She did not say it. She sat astride the roof with her hands on her knees and her ears warm, and watched a girl in a borrowed coat stand in the first thaw of the year with her eyes shut, being rained on.

* * *

By dark the whole camp was talking.

It was water. It was running off every roof, from the long eaves of the bunkhouse and the sheds, from the dead tram, from the gutters of the mess hall, all in different notes. It dripped in the lean-to. It ran under the snow with a sound like a stream under a floor. Down on the river the ice made a long groaning crack, a sound like a door opened in a very large house, and a minute later another, farther off, and the dog got up and went to the window and stood with his nose to the glass.

The hut leaked.

They had patched the north side and not the south. By eight there were four buckets on the boards, and a basin, and a saucepan, and each received a drop at a different interval, so that the office ticked like a room full of clocks. *Plink. Plink-plink. Plink.* Weiss walked among them in her stocking feet with a cloth and a look of tight, ferocious unhappiness, and at ten she announced that it was no good, she could not sleep in it, and went into the second room and shut the door.

Blake lay on her pallet by the stove.

She lay on her back with the blanket to her chin and listened. She had become, over the weeks, a connoisseur of it. There was the saucepan, which was high, and sharp, and in a hurry. There was the biggest bucket, in the corner, a deep slow note, once every eight or nine seconds, as of a very large animal drawing breath. She counted. She got to three hundred and eleven.

The door of the second room was ajar.

She could see the line of it, in the dark: a long black slit, an inch wide, with the faintest grey in it from the window beyond. She could hear what was in there. A cot creaking. A dog's tail on the boards. A person lying on her side, in the dark, with her eyes open, doing the same arithmetic.

She thought about it for some time.

She thought of it as she thought of a door she had not been through. She did the sum. She turned it round. She said to herself, with some severity, that there was no hurry; that it had been a year, and a winter, and a mountain, and that it would still be there at breakfast; that she was a person who had been taught, in a hard school, that to want a thing was to hand a man a knife. She lay and looked at the ceiling.

*Ask me tomorrow,* she had said.

Well. It was tomorrow.

She got up.

* * *

She stood at the door. She did not push it.

The saucepan went, behind her, *plink,* in the dark. She had one hand on the frame. She was barefoot, in her shirt, with her ears up, and her heart doing a thing she knew well and had never in her life been glad of; and she did what she had learned, in a camp in the north, to do in front of a door.

She hummed.

It came out thin. It was not as good as Weiss's. It was flat in the middle, and she held the top too long, and the last note cracked like a boy's. She did all eight bars. She did them to the black slit, and stood, and heard, on the other side, a silence of such completeness that it had a shape.

"You're humming," said Weiss.

"Yes."

"That's mine."

"It was never yours."

A pause.

"No," said Weiss. "I know." Her voice was quite steady, and hoarse, and a little too low. "It's rather good. You hold the top."

"It's the way it's written."

"So it is."

The dog's tail moved on the boards.

"May I come in?"

"Yes."

* * *

The room was very small.

It had a cot and a washstand and a shelf, and a window with no curtain, and through the window the thaw-sky, which had cleared; it was spread with a thousand stars and a late thin moon. The light lay in a long grey bar across the blanket. Weiss was sitting up against the wall with her knees under the quilt and the dog across her feet, in a man's flannel shirt that came to her thighs, with her cropped hair standing out on one side like a feather. She looked, in the dark, extremely young.

Blake shut the door behind her. She left it an inch open.

She stood at the foot of the cot.

"I want to ask something," she said. "I'm going to get it wrong."

"I'm told that's permitted."

"By whom?"

"By me." A small breath, not quite a laugh. "I've found it's the only way I get anything done."

She came round the side of the cot. She stopped where the bar of moonlight began. She had not known her hands would be so cold. She put them together in front of her, fingers laced, as she had seen it done by a child at a recitation, and she looked at the floor, and then, because that was cowardice, at the face.

Weiss was looking at her.

She was looking with her whole attention, in the way she had; with her chin a little lifted, and her lips closed, and her eyes very wide and bright and quite still. She had the look of a person standing at a gate. The scar was a pale line over the left. It made the eye look half-lidded, and not at all: it made it look like it was listening.

"May I kiss you?" said Blake.

It was so much plainer than she had thought. It came out in an ordinary voice. It lay in the dark between them, small, intact, with no ornament, like a stone on a table; and the saucepan dripped, behind her, *plink,* and a long way off on the river the ice cracked and the sound went away down the valley and was lost.

"Yes," said Weiss.

She had said it at once. It had come before the end of the question, fast, breathless, a little hoarse. She heard it as it left her, and put her hand to her mouth.

"I'm sorry. I meant to wait. I had a — it was supposed to be a considered—"

"Weiss."

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"Yes," said Weiss, and took her hand away.

* * *

She sat down on the edge of the cot.

She did it slowly. She was not quite sure of her legs. The mattress dipped, and the dog moved, grumbling, and was gone to the end. She was a foot away. She could smell the quilt: wool, and woodsmoke, and ink, and the peel of an orange. She could hear Weiss breathe. It was quick and light and a little uneven, and it had a catch at the top of each one, like a drawer that wasn't quite shut.

"Tell me what to do," said Weiss.

It was almost a whisper. She was holding herself so rigidly that the quilt shook.

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Stay there. Just—" Blake swallowed. "Stay where I can see you."

"Yes."

"If I stop—"

"Then you stop."

"I might not be able to say."

"Then I'll know." A pause. "I'm good at watching."

It was true. It was quite true. She had known that for a long time, a year, since a lamp in a library and a table with a plate, and she sat looking at the face a foot from her own and understood that she had been afraid of this since the beginning, since the first night, since a sheet of rules in ink; and that the fear was no longer the principal thing in the room.

She lifted her hand.

She did it slowly, in front of her, in the grey light. She held it up where it could be seen. The fingers were not steady. She brought it to the side of Weiss's face, and stopped, a half inch off the cheek, with the palm open, as one holds a hand over a flame to see how near it can come.

Weiss did not move.

Her eyes had closed. Her face tipped, a degree, into the hand. Then, quite slowly, with the air of a person lowering something very heavy onto a table, she set her own palm over the back of Blake's, and drew it in, the last half inch, against her cheek.

It was warm. It was warmer than the quilt. The skin was dry and very smooth, and it fit.

She leaned forward.

She had thought it would be a decision. It was nothing like. It was the closing of a distance, a foot, then six inches, then three, as the last of the light goes out of a room; and her ears had gone entirely forward, and her breath was coming through her mouth, and she shut her eyes.

Weiss's mouth was cold.

It was dry, and cold, and for a second it did not move. Then it did. It opened, very slightly, on a breath, and was warm; and a hand came up and rested against her jaw, not holding, only touching, with the thumb under the ear, so lightly that she felt the pulse in it. It lasted perhaps three seconds.

She drew back.

Her heart was going like a hammer. She sat with her eyes still shut, and her hand where it had been, against a cheek, and for a moment she did not know whose breath it was. It came in short pulls. Her ears had gone flat and then forward and then, absurdly, both ways at once.

"Blake."

"I'm here."

"Are you—"

"Yes. Wait." She breathed. She opened her eyes. Weiss's face was a hand's width off, in the moonlight, pale, and wet along one cheek, and looking at her with a terrible exactness. "I'm all right."

"You said not to say that."

"I know. I'm—" She stopped. "I'm okay."

Something shook in Weiss's face. It began at the mouth and spread, and she bit her lip, hard, and could not stop it; and a sound came out of her that was small and helpless and entirely without dignity. She put her hand over her mouth again.

"That was," said Weiss, through her fingers, in a high strained voice, "adequate."

Blake stared at her.

She had a moment of pure white blankness. And then it rose up in her from somewhere very deep, from the soles of her feet, from the whole length of the past year, and came out of her mouth in a noise she had never made before in her life: a short, cracked, helpless bark of a laugh. She bent over her knees. She laughed until her shoulders shook.

"High praise," she said, when she could.

"It was meant as such."

"I know."

"I'm sorry. I couldn't—"

"Don't be."

"I couldn't *not.*" Weiss was laughing too, silently, with her eyes shut, with the tears running down into the corners of her mouth. She wiped them, impatiently, with the flat of her wrist. "It's the shock."

"Yes."

"It was extremely good."

"Thank you."

"I should like to do it again, at some convenient time."

"Okay."

"Not at once."

"No."

"Possibly in a minute."

"I'll tell you," said Blake, "when I'm ready."

"I know you will."

They sat.

The room was full of stars. The dog, at the end of the cot, had put his head on Blake's foot with a sigh of immense contentment, and shut his eyes. In the next room the saucepan dripped, and the bucket answered it, a note lower. Weiss reached out her hand, palm up, in the grey light, and left it lying on the quilt between them, a foot from Blake's knee, open.

She looked at it.

Then she put her own into it, and the fingers closed.

* * *

She did not sleep, at first. She lay on her side, in the cot, in the narrow space left against the wall, with Weiss facing her in the dark, a foot away, under the quilt. Neither of them had suggested it. It had happened by degrees. There was a hand in hers, which had not let go. There was a leg, somewhere, not touching. The dog lay along their feet like a bolster.

"I can't sleep with someone behind me," she said.

"Yes. I know."

"I'd like to face you."

"Then you will."

"You'll have to — you'll be where I can see."

"I'm told I'm worth looking at," said Weiss, extremely dry, "in some lights."

She almost laughed again. She did not. She lay and looked, in the dark, at the small pale shape of a face, and the line of the scar, and the lashes, and the white of the throat; and she thought: *it has been a long way.* It was not a sentence she was used to. It sat in her chest, warm and heavy and entirely unfamiliar, like a cat that has chosen a lap.

"Staying?" said Weiss, very low.

It was past midnight.

She had never in her life been asked it at that hour.

"Today," said Blake. "And tomorrow. Ask me."

She slept.

* * *

She woke in the grey.

It was early. The window had gone the colour of pearl. There was a weight across her ribs, warm and light, and for the space of a heartbeat she did not know it; and then she did, and every muscle in her body locked.

It was an arm. It was Weiss's, flung over her waist in sleep, the hand open on the quilt, the fingers a little curled. It had not moved. Its owner lay close against her, with her face half in the pillow and her mouth slack, breathing slowly through the nose, and her cropped hair in her eyes; and her whole body was turned to Blake's as a plant turns.

It was not a hand on the neck. It was not behind her. It was in front, where she could see, and it was warm, and it was a girl asleep.

Her heart did not know that.

It went on, racing, in the dark, for the old reason. She lay rigid. She could feel the sweat starting along her spine. She breathed in, slow, and out, slow, as she had been taught on a strand, and she told herself the facts, one at a time, in order, in the voice of a clerk: *this is Weiss. This is a room. The door is open an inch. I can get up.*

She got up.

She did it by degrees. She lifted the arm and laid it down on the quilt, gently, and it curled a little into the warm place she had left. She stood in the grey on the cold boards, in her shirt. She was shaking. She picked up her boots, and stood with them in her hand.

She thought: *Go.*

She stood a long time.

She thought: *Rule twelve.*

She turned, and put the boots down, and crossed to the cot, and knelt beside it. She put her hand on the quilt, near the shoulder, and said in a low clear voice:

"Weiss."

The eyes opened. They were very blue. For a second they were quite empty; and then they took her in, the face, the stance, the boots by the door, and something went across them, quick as a bird, and was gone.

"Yes," said Weiss.

"I need to walk."

"Yes."

"I'm telling you before." She swallowed. "I'm not leaving. I just— it was a lot. I'd like to be out. I'll be back."

"When?"

"An hour."

"Right." Weiss pushed up on an elbow. Her hair stood out at the side. Her voice was rough with sleep, and quite level, and her hand had come out of the quilt and lay on the blanket between them, open, an inch from Blake's, not reaching. "Take your time."

"I'm coming back."

"I know."

"I'm saying it in person."

"I heard it." The corner of her mouth moved. "It was very well done. I should like it noted."

"Noted."

"Go on, then."

She went.

She walked down to the river in the cold. The light was coming up gold along the top of the hill and the whole valley was running with water, dripping, ticking, chuckling in a thousand small voices; and the ice at the bend had gone dark and rotten and had opened, in the middle, a long black seam, through which the river was visible, moving. She stood on the bank with her hands in her pockets and breathed.

She stood there for fifty minutes.

At the end of it she cut a hole in the shelf of ice that was left, and sat on a sack, and put a line down. It took her seven minutes. It was a fat one, a grayling, silver, with a sail on its back, and it came up out of the black water in a spray of light.

She carried it back by the gill.

The dog saw her first. He came out at a run and hit her knees. She stood in the yard with the fish in one hand and her other on his head, and the sun on her face, and looked at the hut. The door was open. Weiss stood in it. She had the quilt round her shoulders like a cloak, and her hair on end, and a cup in each hand, and she was not pretending to be doing anything else.

"Fifty-three minutes," said Weiss.

"You counted."

"Of course I counted. I'm a Schnee." She held out a cup. "It's the way you make it."

Blake took it.

It was hot. It was strong, black, a little burnt. She drank, and over the rim of the cup, in the clear early light, with a grayling hanging from her fist and a dog against her shin, she looked at the girl in the doorway and thought, with perfect calm: *Oh.* And then, as one lays down a thing that has been carried a long time: *There you are.*
