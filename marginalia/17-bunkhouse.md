# 17. bunkhouse

On the twelfth day a dog barked.

She was at the stove, with the damper in her hand, trying to learn what it was for. The sound came across the yard, one flat bark and a pause and another, and she put the damper down and went to the window and wiped a hole in the frost.

A figure was coming up the track on skis.

It was small and broad. It moved with a short, steady, shuffling stride, bent forward over the poles, in a heap of grey wool and sheepskin that made it hard to tell where the coat stopped. Behind it a little sled came sliding on a rope. Beside the sled, trotting, with one ear up and one ear down, went a long lean grey dog with a serious face.

Weiss put on the coat. She took Myrtenaster from the peg, and then put it back. She opened the door.

It was a woman. She had stopped at the edge of the yard to knock the snow from her skis, one and then the other, with great deliberation, and she looked up only when she had finished. Her face was brown and creased and wide-cheeked. Her eyes were pale. From under the sheepskin cap, on either side, rose two long white ears, white as the snow, tipped with black, one of them nicked at the edge by a small old notch.

"You'll be the Schnee."

"Yes."

"Thought so." The woman unstrapped the skis. "Smoke on the second day. Nobody else burns wood like they're angry at it."

"I've been told I'm slow with the stove."

"You're not slow. You're loud." She looked Weiss over, once, from the hair to the boots, and her gaze stopped on the hands. "Your gloves don't match."

"No."

"Hm."

She bent and lifted a bundle from the sled. It was tied in oilcloth. She put it into Weiss's arms without ceremony, and it was heavy, and cold, and smelled of smoke and salt. "Fish. Flour. Lamp oil, a can. Four lien. I'll take it in silver if you've got it."

Weiss found the coins. They changed hands.

The dog had come to sit beside the woman's boot. It looked at Weiss. It looked at the door behind her, from which a little warmth was coming, and then at Weiss again, with an air of reasonable inquiry.

"That's Pike," said the woman. "He isn't staying."

"I didn't ask."

"I'm saying." She tugged her cap straight. "I'm Dahl. Tove Dahl. I got your letter."

* * *

She had known it was coming. She had known for twelve days, and for two hundred miles, and she had rehearsed it in the dark on the cot, and it was nothing like.

"Neat hand," said Tove Dahl.

"Mrs. Dahl—"

"Tove. We'll get on." She was looking not at Weiss but past her, up the slope, at the grey square of stone. "I wrote you. I thought they'd keep it back."

"They did. For a time."

"Did you read it?"

"Yes."

"Better than the other, then."

It was said with no change of tone. Weiss felt it arrive, and stood in the doorway, with the oilcloth in her arms.

"Yes."

"Hm." The woman nodded slowly. "Well. You've been up there."

"Yes."

"Did you say anything?"

"No."

"Don't," said Tove. "It's a wall. It can't hear." She shifted her feet in the snow. "I asked for the words. Have you got them?"

She had had them ready. She had had a good many. They lay in order behind her teeth; she could feel them there, polished, in line, and not one of them could be said.

"I didn't know what the letter said," she said. "I signed it without reading. I was told it was a letter of sympathy, and I was glad to sign. I didn't ask to see the second page. That is not an excuse."

"No."

"I know that it isn't."

"Good."

"They decided not to pay. The Company. My father decided. And I signed it."

"You."

"I signed it."

Tove looked at her. It was the first time she had properly done so, and it went on a long while: the pale eyes in the brown creased face, steady and unhurried and entirely without heat. The dog yawned. In the camp behind them, somewhere, a loose sheet of iron ticked in the cold.

"That's the words," said the old woman. "That'll do."

She turned and began to put her skis on the sled.

"That's all?"

"What did you want?" She did not look round. "A scene?"

"I—" Weiss stopped.

"I've no use for you being sorry. I'll tell you now. Don't be sorry at me. It's no good to me and it's tiring for you." Tove straightened. She put her hands in the small of her back and arched, with a grunt, like a woman of any age. "You burn like an engine. Somebody should chop it. You can chop wood."

"I'm sorry?"

"There. That's what I mean."

* * *

The bunkhouse was the long roof.

She had not been inside. She had passed it forty times on the way to the river and the lean-to, and she had learned its outline as one learns the outline of a thing one will not look at: the low eaves, the door at the gable end with its latch tied shut, the row of small windows with their shutters hanging.

Tove lifted the latch with a thumb.

It was dark. It smelled of cold wood and wool and old tobacco. She stood in the door and let her eyes learn it. Two rows of bunks, twelve a side, in tiers, with a wide aisle down the middle, and at the far end a squat stove gone red with rust. Some of the blankets were still on the beds. Someone had left a boot under the fourth. On a nail by the door hung a coat with the shoulder gone, and a lamp of the old kind, with its glass blackened.

Tove went in. Weiss went after her.

"Look," said Tove.

It was on the posts.

She had thought, at first, that the wood was marked by age. It was marked by hands. Every upright, every rail, every end-board of every bunk, to the height a man could reach lying down, was cut with names. They were cut with knives and nails and the points of spoons. They were carved deep, or scratched shallow, or burnt in with a hot wire. There were dates. There were small drawings: a boat, a tree, a woman's face with large eyes. There were tallies. There were a great many rude jokes.

She went along the aisle with her hand not quite touching.

*A. VOE.* On a lower rail, near the door. Careful, upright capitals.

*D.Q. WAS HERE AND WAS RIGHT.*

*P. OAKES. SICK OF BEANS.*

And on an end-board, at the height of the eyes, in a hand she knew from the log, small and exact, underlined: *−21.4.*

"Anselm," said Tove.

She had come up behind her. She stood at Weiss's elbow with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the rail. Her ears, long and white, stood still.

"Snored. Like a boat on a rope. You could hear it from the cookhouse. He'd a wife in Mantle and sent her every lien. That one is Dara." She nodded at the next bunk. "Cheated at cards. Was no good at it. Lost four shifts' wages in a winter to a man she'd taught." Her eyes moved. "Pettigrew couldn't sing. He did, though. Every night. E.V. there, the surveyor, he wrote the temperature to the decimal. We said he'd write the day he died to the second. He'd have liked that."

She went on.

She did not do it for effect. She said each one in the same dry flat voice, in a tone almost of complaint, as one might go through a list of tools, and each had something. *Left-handed. Owed me a pound. Wept at weddings. Could whistle with two fingers and a thumb. Terrible with children.* They came up out of the dark of the bunkhouse one at a time, the way objects come out of a drawer. They had boots. They had mothers. It was a long room.

Weiss stood and listened with her hand against a post.

She had thirty-one names in a book. She had written them with care. She had written them in a good hand, with a good pen, on good paper, over three nights, in the voice she had begun to hear. She had thought it was the thing to do.

She had thought it was knowing them.

"I have them written down," she said.

"Do you?"

"All thirty-one."

"Let's see."

She had the notebook in her coat. She held it out. Tove took it in two brown hands and opened it close to her face and read, in the half dark, with her lips a little moving. She turned the pages slowly. She went down the list.

"Hm. Neat hand."

"Yes."

"Spelled Quill wrong."

"It's from the roster."

"Roster's wrong. It's Quil. One L." Tove held the book up to the light of the nearest window, and tapped the page with a nail. "She hated the second. Said it was a waste of ink."

Weiss took the pen from her pocket. She looked at the name for a moment. *Dara Quill,* in a good clear hand, in the sixth place down.

She struck out the second *l.* She did it carefully, with a single line, as one strikes out an error in a ledger; and then she wrote the correction in the margin, small and clean. *Quil.*

She stood with the pen over it.

It was a small thing. It was a letter of the alphabet. She was aware of her own hand, steady on the page, and of a sensation behind the breastbone that she could not name and did not try to, as of something heavy being turned slightly over.

"There," said Tove Dahl. She was looking not at the book but at her face. She did not look away. "That's better."

* * *

At the third bunk from the door, on the lower tier, Tove stopped.

It was no different from the others. A grey blanket, folded; a hollow in the straw tick; a name cut into the end-board in wavering capitals, and under it, much smaller, a line that Weiss could not read from there. The old woman stood and looked at it. She did not touch it. After a time she said, in the same voice, "That one's mine."

Weiss said nothing.

"Halvard. Twenty-two years." She said it plainly. "Sang flat. Never looked at the weather. I've been up here every month since, to sit on it. I don't know why. It's a bed."

The dog had gone ahead of them.

It had been ranging the length of the room with its nose to the boards, and now it had come back and gone, without hesitation, to the third bunk from the door. It put its forepaws on the edge. It looked back at them both over its shoulder. Then it climbed in, turned round three times, and lay down in the hollow of the straw with its chin on the grey blanket, and shut its eyes.

"Pike," said Tove.

The dog did not move.

"Pike."

He opened one eye. He shut it.

"Hm," said the old woman.

She regarded him for some time. Then she turned and looked at Weiss, who was standing in the aisle of the bunkhouse with a notebook in one gloved hand and a pen in the other, and there was something in her face for a moment that was not unlike amusement, and not unlike something else.

"He's decided."

"I can't keep a dog."

"You can't send him back either. He'll sort it out." She was already walking to the door. "Thursday, with the fish. You'll want to draw your damper down. You're wasting wood."

"Mrs. Dahl."

"Tove."

"Thank you."

The old woman paused with her hand on the latch. She did not turn. Her ears, against the grey light of the door, long and pale, with their black tips, turned a very little toward the sound.

"For what?" she said.

"For — saying them."

"Somebody should," said Tove, and went out, and the door swung, and she was a small figure crossing the white yard, on skis, with an empty sled.

* * *

She stayed on in the bunkhouse until it was dark.

She sat on the end of the nearest bunk with the notebook on her knee, and a stub of pencil, and wrote by the window. She went down the list. She did it slowly, with her tongue between her teeth. Beside each of the thirty-one names she put, in a small hand, in the margin, what she had been told.

*Snored.*

*Cheated at cards.*

*Couldn't sing. Did.*

She did not have it for them all. She had it for eleven. The others she left, with a clean space beside, and told herself she would ask.

At some point there was a weight on her feet.

She looked down. The dog had come from the third bunk and lain across her boots, heavily, as a coat is laid over a chair. It was warm. It breathed with a small deep whistle, and its flank went up and down against her ankle.

"I'm not keeping you," said Weiss.

The tail moved once on the boards.

She finished the page.
