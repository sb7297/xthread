# 25. deathless and knowing what is right

> *a̱mṛtā̍ ṛta̱jñāḥ*
>
> — Rig Veda 7.35

She heard the engine four miles off.

It was the afternoon of the hundred-and-eighth day. She was on the river with the line wound round her fist, and it came up the valley as a thin tearing sound under the wind, a high mechanical whine like a saw being drawn through tin. She did not know it. She knew it. She stood with the line forgotten and her ears turned all the way round, and the dog, who had been lying by the hole with his chin on the ice, got up and began to bark.

By the time she reached the yard it was in the camp.

It was a snow machine, a battered grey one with a cracked windscreen, and it came up the last of the track in a spray of white and swung round the dead tram and stopped. The engine died. In the silence the snow went on falling off it, in a long soft slide.

The rider sat there. She wore a sheepskin coat that was too big for her and goggles pushed up on a knitted cap, and a long rope of yellow hair came down from under it and over one shoulder. She pulled off a glove with her teeth. She looked at the camp, at the long roofs and the stone, for some while, with the face of someone reading a map she had been told was wrong.

Then she turned her head.

It was thirty feet. Blake stood at the end of the bunkhouse with a frozen line in her hand and her ears flat and forward at the same time, which she had not known was possible.

"You absolute—" said Yang.

She was off the seat. She was crossing the snow. She did not run; she walked, quick and heavy, like a woman going to meet a train she suspected of being late, and her face was working. She came up to Blake and stopped. For an instant they stood and looked at each other, and Blake saw her eyes, which were red, and the hard line of the jaw, and how she was breathing.

"I'm so mad at you."

"I know."

"I'm so—" Her fist came up and hit Blake in the shoulder. It was not a hard blow. It was the kind one gives a door. "—*mad.*"

"I know."

And then Yang's arms were round her.

She had not been ready. She stood rigid in it. One arm was warm, a living weight across her back; the other was cold, so cold that she could feel it through the grey coat and the shirt under it, a long hard bar of metal laid down the spine, fitted, precise, unbending. She smelled engine oil and woodsmoke and, under it, very faintly, something like honey.

Her hands came up. She did not decide it. They went round the sheepskin, and held.

"Don't do it again," said Yang, into her collar.

"I'll try."

"Don't *try.*"

There was a long pause. Over Yang's shoulder the sky was an even white. She could see the stump of the dead tram. She could see, on the step of the hut, a figure that had come out and stood quite still with a hand on the frame.

"Okay," said Blake.

Yang drew back. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her good hand, once, hard, as if clearing a windscreen, and looked her up and down, and a slow grin came across her face, crooked and wet.

"You've got your ears out."

"Yes."

"They're great. They've always been great." She sniffed. "I mean it. I should've said."

Blake looked at the left sleeve. It was empty to the elbow, and then it was not. The cuff had ridden back a little over a joint of brushed steel. She looked at it and could not look at anything else, and Yang saw her do it, and her face changed.

"It's a good arm."

"Yang—"

"It's a good *arm,* Blake." She said it quite gently. "I'm not saying it's fine that you left. It wasn't fine. I'm saying the arm's fine."

* * *

Weiss was in the door of the hut.

She had on the coachman's coat and one of the good scarves, and her hair was pinned up, and her face was composed in an expression that Blake knew from long ago, a careful blank courtesy, such as one might wear on meeting a delegation.

"Yang."

"Hey, Ice Queen."

They looked at each other. Neither of them had moved.

Then Yang crossed the yard in four strides and put her arms round her, and Weiss stood in it with her own arms out a little from her sides, like a person being measured for a coat. After a moment she lowered them. She patted Yang once on the back.

"Your arm is cold," she said.

"Yeah, sorry. I can't feel when I'm squeezing."

"You're fine."

"Am I crushing you?"

"A little."

"Cool." Yang let go. She held her at arm's length by the shoulders and examined her with her head on one side, frowning. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"You look better than I thought, actually."

"Thank you."

"You both look terrible," said Yang, looking from one of them to the other. Her eyes had gone very sharp and went on being sharp for about a second. Then she let go, and clapped her hands together, and said, "Okay! I brought stuff."

* * *

She had brought a great deal. She had a pack the size of a child. There was a sack of coffee, which Weiss received in both hands like a relic, and a tin of biscuits, and a number of letters done up in string, and a wad of Mistral newspapers eleven days old. And at the bottom, in a net bag, packed in a sock, a thing that she drew out with a flourish and set in the middle of the desk.

Oranges.

There were six. They were large and rough-skinned and absurdly orange, the colour of nothing else in a world of white, and the smell came up off them as the sock was peeled back and filled the hut in a single breath: sharp, wet, sweet, green at the edges. Blake felt it go into her like a hand.

"They're ridiculous," said Yang. "I nearly got arrested at the Mantle checkpoint. The guy thought they were Dust. I had to *eat one* to prove it."

Nobody said anything.

Weiss was looking at the oranges with an expression Blake could not read. She was standing quite still by the stove with the coffee in her arms.

Yang gave one to each of them, and kept one. She sat on the corner of the desk and began tearing the rind off hers in great ragged strips, with her thumb, and flicking them into the stove. Blake held hers. She turned it over. After a moment she dug her thumbnail in at the top and began, carefully, round and round, to peel.

She did it in a single spiral.

The rind came away in one long curl, never breaking, and hung from her hand, and she laid it on the desk where it lay in its own coil like a spring. She did not look up. She knew where Weiss's eyes were.

"He did it like that," said Weiss, very low.

"I watched him."

"Who?" said Yang.

"A man," said Blake.

"Okay," said Yang, with enormous tact, and ate a segment.

* * *

They ate at the table. It was fish, and potato, and a loaf Weiss had learned to make that was nearly bread. Yang ate three helpings, talking with her mouth full, and told them about Mistral: the checkpoints, the markets, the street where a man had tried to sell her a live goose. She told the story of a fight in a tavern. She did all the voices. At the part with the goose, Weiss's mouth did something, and Blake saw it and looked down.

It was easier than she had thought. It was nearly easy. There was a third person at the table, and the third person was loud, and the air between the other two had been moved aside to make room.

"So how are you?" said Yang.

She said it to Blake. She said it lightly, over the potato, with her fork in the air, but her eyes had come up and were steady. "Honest. You okay? You look wiped."

Blake opened her mouth.

"She's tired," said Weiss. "She's been sleeping badly. I've been taking the night stove."

There was a very small pause.

Yang's eyes moved to Weiss. They moved back. She put her fork down on the plate, quietly.

"Right," she said. "Cool. Good to know."

Blake said nothing. Her ears had gone back, and she let them.

* * *

The letter came out afterward.

It was a thick envelope, addressed in coloured pen, with the *W* of *Weiss* drawn in four different inks and a small rose in the corner. Yang laid it on the table with the face of one performing a surgical procedure. "She wrote it in, like, six sittings. She made me promise to wait till I'd eaten." She lowered herself into the chair. "She says read it out. She says she doesn't care who."

Weiss unfolded it. It ran to four pages in a round fast hand that sloped uphill. She read it silently, the first page, and then the second. Her lips tightened. Then she held it out across the table to Blake without a word, and Blake took it, and read aloud, in a flat even voice, as she did everything.

*Dear Weiss and Blake,*

*(Yang says you're together. I said I didn't know. She said "together like in the same place," and I said oh.)*

*We're in Mistral. A lot has happened. I'm going to try to explain and I'm going to do it badly, so please be patient with me.*

*Ozpin isn't dead. Or he is, but he also isn't. He's been alive for a really long time, like a thousand years, by coming back in other people when he dies. Right now he's in a boy called Oscar, who is fourteen and from a farm and really nice. He didn't tell us. He didn't tell anyone. He says he had reasons. Qrow says he had reasons. I think maybe he did. I'm so mad. I don't know how to be that mad and also still—he's our friend. I think.*

*There's a woman called Salem and she's behind all of it, and it's a long story, and I'll tell you when I see you.*

*Weiss, you said you'd write. I know you couldn't. I'm just saying I noticed.*

*Blake, Yang says you came back, and went to find Weiss, and I'm so happy I'm not even mad. (I'm a little mad.) (I'm not.)*

*Please come when you can. We need you. I need you.*

*Love, Ruby.*

*P.S. Jaune says hi. Nora says hi, aggressively. Ren nodded.*

She put it down.

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, in the white dark, the wind had come up a little, and was feeling along the eaves.

"Well," said Yang. "That's the gist."

* * *

"He must have had reasons," said Weiss.

She said it slowly. She had been looking at the letter for a long time, with her hands folded on the desk, and she said it in the voice she used for the board of a problem when she was working her way round it. "It isn't impossible. There are some things that can't be known without cost. If it were widely known—a thousand years. A woman who's behind all of it. People would—"

"He didn't ask," said Blake.

"I don't say it was right."

"You say he had reasons."

"Everyone has reasons." Weiss's chin came up a little. "Having a reason isn't the same as being right. I'm not defending him. I'm only saying that it's a position one can understand. If he'd told us, if he'd said it plainly to a room of children—there would have been panic. There would have been chaos. Perhaps it was a judgement."

"It was a judgement."

"Yes."

"His."

"Someone must make it."

"Why?"

"Because otherwise—" Weiss stopped, and began again, more evenly. "Because if one doesn't, then everyone chooses for themselves, with what they know, and most people know very little, and so most of them choose badly. That is simply how it goes. It's how it goes in a company. In a Council. In—"

"A house."

Weiss's eyes came up.

"Is that what you think?" said Blake. "That people choose badly?"

"Some do."

"And it's for somebody to say which."

"I didn't say it was pleasant."

"No," said Blake. "It's restful."

It was out before she had known it was there. She heard it. It went into the room like a shot and stuck. Weiss's face did not move. A line of white came round her mouth, and went.

Yang said: "Okay, so we're all mad at the old guy—"

"Where did you hear that?"

"What?"

"*Restful.*" Weiss's voice was perfectly level. "Where did you hear it?"

"It's in the book."

It was on page fifty-eight, in a steep hand, from the last week in Atlas. *Father says that I shall find it very restful.* She had read it eleven times. She had not meant to use it. She sat with the letter in her fingers and her ears pinned flat and her heart going like a hammer in her chest, and it was strange; she heard herself from a long way off, as from the far end of a corridor. A part of her stood at one side with its arms folded and said, quite clearly, *stop.*

She did not stop.

"He decided what we could bear," she said. "That's what you're defending. Somebody sat in a room and decided what the rest of us could stand to be told. And it was *kind.* It was for our sake. I'd like to know how long you've been practising."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The stove." Her own voice was thin and cold and quite steady. "I had the night stove. It's on the schedule, in my hand. It's been lit before I woke for fifty-seven nights."

"You were tired."

"I know I was tired."

"You sleep badly—"

"I *know* I sleep badly!" She heard it crack, and kept going. "I'd like to have been asked if I wanted the stove. I'd like to have been asked about the *river,* where you go first in case the ice is thin, when it isn't, you know it isn't. I'd like to have been asked about Tove. About lamp day. 'You needn't come.' 'It's your own people.' 'You'd rather stay.' You answer when they ask me. You told Yang I was tired. I was *sitting here.* I had my mouth open."

"I only—"

"You write 'rest.' You write it on a sheet and you give it to me with both hands. You've done it since the door." She was on her feet. She did not know when she had stood. "I thought it was kindness. I did. I thought it was— I let it. I let it for two months because I was so— And it's the same. It's *exactly* the same. Someone decides what I can have and what I'd like and when I've had enough. And then they're the one who's good."

"I was trying to help."

"He was trying to help."

"Who?" said Weiss.

Blake looked at her.

She stood there across the lamp, in the hut, with her breath coming short, with her hands open at her sides. The whole room had gone away. There was a face in front of her, white and thin and extremely still, with a scar over the eye, and behind it, quite distinctly, a long way down a corridor, another one, a mask. She could not tell which of them she was looking at. She knew that she should be able to.

"*I'm only trying to keep you safe,*" she said. "Every day for six years."

The silence was total.

It was the silence of a room from which something has been taken out. Yang, at the end of the table, had not moved at all. Her hand lay flat on the wood, the right one, and the metal one lay beside it. Even the stove seemed to have stopped.

Weiss said, "Adam."

"Yes."

"You think—" She stopped.

Blake saw it happen.

She saw it, as one sees a window shut. It went across the face from the brow downward, smoothly, a lid coming down, so that for a second there was a pale unlit surface, composed, courteous, entirely without expression. It was a face she had seen once before in her life, at two in the morning, over a puddle on a floor.

"I see," said Weiss.

It was so quiet.

She picked up her cup. It was empty. She carried it to the basin, and washed it, and dried it with the cloth, and set it on the shelf with the handle turned toward the wall, exactly, as she did. She folded the cloth. She laid it over the rail.

"Yang," she said. "The floor by the stove is the warmest. There are blankets in the chest." Her voice was polished, impersonal and gracious. "Please make yourself at home. You are very welcome to stay."

"Weiss—"

"Good night."

She went into the second room, and the door closed behind her. It closed with a small clean click.

* * *

Nobody moved.

Then Yang let out a long breath through her teeth, and sat back, and ran the flesh hand slowly down her face.

"Okay," she said. "That was a lot."

"I know."

"You okay?"

"No."

"Yeah." Yang looked at the door. She looked at the letter, and the six oranges, and the long coil of peel on the desk. "She's not wrong about Ozpin though. I mean. Somebody's gotta—"

"Don't."

"I'm just saying—"

"Don't."

"Okay," said Yang.

She stood up. She took an orange from the net bag and turned it in her hand, a little uncertainly, and put it back. She put on the sheepskin. At the door she turned, with the latch under her hand, and for a moment the old Yang was in her face, the one who stood in front, who stepped in, who never in her life let anything go.

"I'm gonna go check on the dog," she said, and went out.

* * *

She lit the stove herself.

She did it at ten, in the bunkhouse, alone. It was the first time in fifty-seven nights. She knelt in front of the barrel with the box of matches and built it up as she had been taught, paper and splinters and logs, and it caught, and drew, and the red came up behind the grille.

It was hers. It was warm. She sat in front of it on her heels with her hands out and her ears down and watched it burn.

It did not help.

At eleven she lay down on the bunk in the dark with her boots on. The dog lay across her feet. He had come in at ten, and had stood in the door a long time looking back across the yard at the light in the hut, and then had come and lain down with the air of a creature that has made a very difficult choice.

She lay on her back.

She waited.

She did not know that she was waiting until the bell went in her head for the quarter. The bunkhouse was warm. The wind went along the roof. Across the yard a lamp was still lit, a pale oblong, at the window of the hut; and the door of the hut stayed shut.

The yard was silent.

Nobody crossed it. Nobody hummed. She lay and listened, with her ears up and turned, out into the cold, as far as they would go, until there was nothing left to listen to, and the bell went the half, and then twelve, and the lamp in the window went out.
