# 30. vanity

> *Again, if two lie together, then they have heat: but how can one be warm alone?*
>
> — Ecclesiastes 4:11

The mirror was a shaving mirror, round, on a stand, with a crack across the lower left corner that gave the corner of the room a second corner. It had been on the washstand in the second room since the day she came. She had not looked in it. She had washed her face with her eyes shut, like a woman in a dark hotel, and used the window.

On the hundred-and-thirty-fourth morning she looked.

She had her hair up. She had been wearing it up for a month with a pencil through it, a thing she would have thought unthinkable, and it had held; but the ends had split, and the weight of it pulled at her neck by evening, and it had not been cut since a woman called Odile had trimmed it, in a house a long way south, in a life with a different smell. She took out the pencil. It fell, to the middle of her back, in a dry white sheet.

It was not a good face.

She examined it with the dispassion she gave to a score. It was thinner. The nose looked longer. There was a brown flush across the bridge and the cheekbones where the sun came off the snow, and the lips had cracked, and the skin under the eyes was the colour of old paper. The scar was white against the flush. It ran from the brow into the lid, an inch and a half, slightly puckered, a clean ridge.

She had stopped seeing it at about eleven. That was the way with things one lives with. She saw it now, because she was looking; and what struck her was not that it was ugly, which she had always been told, but that it was ordinary. It was a mark on a face. It was a place where something had happened.

"You've got a pencil in your hair."

She turned.

Blake was in the door with a bucket. She had come in from the river, and her ears were rosy at the tips, and there was a rime of ice on the front of the grey coat where the water had slopped.

"It was a hair-stick."

"It's a pencil."

"It performs the function."

"It's yours," said Blake. "It's the one from the desk. I've been looking for it for a week." She set the bucket down. "Staying?"

"Today," said Weiss. "You?"

"Today."

It was a habit already. It had a rhythm, now, like a handshake. They said it at the door, one or the other, in the morning, and sometimes at night, and once, absurdly, over the river, shouted across forty yards of ice; and it was a thing that had to be done honestly or not at all, and she had found that it got neither easier nor harder, but only more like itself.

She looked at the mirror again.

"I'd like it cut," she said.

* * *

"I'm not a barber."

"You have the best hand in the camp."

"I have a good hand for fish."

"The principle is the same." Weiss had found the scissors in the first-aid box, a small pair with a blunt tip, and a comb in the case. She laid them on the washstand in a row. "I'd like to ask, rather than assume. Would you?"

Blake looked at the scissors.

She was standing in the middle of the floor with her hands at her sides and her ears slightly back, and what was in her face was not reluctance. It was a very careful inspection, of the kind one gives a floor before stepping on it.

"Behind you."

"Yes."

"With a blade."

"With a pair of blunt scissors. I'll face the glass. You'll be in it the whole time. I shall be able to see you." Weiss paused. "And you'll hear me."

"You'll hum."

"If you like."

"I'd like."

She sat on the stool. It was a plain pine one, with a split seat. She put her back straight and her hands in her lap, and looked at the cracked glass, and in it, over her own shoulder, a small distance behind, Blake's face came up. It looked grave. It looked, in the mirror's slight tilt, like a face in an old photograph, taken at a window.

She began to hum.

The comb went through first. It took a minute to find the ends. Then the sound of the scissors began: a short dry crisp bite, and a pause, and another. A lock came away at the nape and fell past her shoulder. She watched it go.

* * *

"How did you get it?"

It came without warning. It was asked in the plainest voice, to the back of her head, as if it had been in the air for some time.

"The scar."

"They say a Grimm."

"Who says?"

"My father." The scissors bit. "Winter says a fall. She was in Mantle, at the time, with her instructor. She came back to find me in bed with a bandage."

"How old?"

"Five."

"Do you remember?"

Weiss considered. In the glass, the crack ran like a vein across the lower corner, and Blake's hands moved in it, grave and exact, lifting, measuring, closing.

"A light," she said. "I think. A very white light, very close. I've never been sure that it's mine. I may have been told it." She paused. "It's rather odd. I've lived with the account for thirteen years and I've never once asked the right person."

"Who's that?"

"Whoever was there."

The scissors stopped.

"Who was there?"

"I've wondered." She said it quietly. She watched her own mouth in the glass. "I think it may have been the person who looked after us."

Blake didn't answer. The scissors started again.

"Ask Winter," she said.

"I mean to."

"When?"

"There's a letter. It's written. It's been written for three weeks, on the shelf, in the box. I haven't sent it." Weiss turned a hand palm up on her knee. "I don't have an address I'm willing to put on it."

"Why?"

"If I put the address, he can find me."

"Your father."

"Yes."

"Does it matter?"

It was so level a question that for a moment she did not hear that it was one. She sat with her hands open. The scissors waited. In the cracked glass Blake's eyes had come up, steady, gold, under the dark brows, and rested on hers.

"I think it does and it doesn't," said Weiss. "I think I should like it not to."

"Okay."

"That's all you're going to say?"

"I'm cutting your hair."

It was so extremely dry that she nearly laughed, and had to hold her head very still, with her chin up and her lips pressed together; and in the glass Blake's mouth did the thing at the corner, once, and went back.

"Hum," she said.

She hummed.

The scissors went in time. She had not known that they would. It was a slow soft bite on the first beat of every bar, and then the rest, and then the climb; the white locks fell on the boards at her feet with a faint whisper, one, two, one, and the dog lay with his chin on his paws and watched them come down.

* * *

It stopped a little above her shoulders.

It was blunt, and very straight, and slightly uneven on the left. She turned her head. It swung. It was extraordinarily light; the back of her neck was bare to the stove, and cold, and she put her hand to it, and found a stranger there.

"It's crooked," said Blake.

"It's wonderful."

"The left is short."

"It's *wonderful.*" She stood. She looked in the glass. She did not know the girl who looked out of it, a thin sharp-faced girl, clear-eyed, cropped to the jaw like a page, with a scar and a burn across her nose and an expression of absurd astonishment. She turned her head from side to side. "I look like a boy who's been to the dentist."

"You look—" Blake stopped.

"Yes?"

"Fine."

"That is the single most unconvincing word I've ever heard you say."

"I know." The ears were forward. "It's all I've got."

Weiss laughed. It came out clean. She took the scissors out of Blake's hand. Their fingers met on the blades for an instant, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Now you."

"No."

"You trim it with the knife."

"It's fine."

"It shows."

"*Weiss.*"

"I'm asking," said Weiss. "I'm asking whether I may. I should like to. You may say no. I'll mind, and I shall go on." She held her hands out, a little, palms up. "I thought I might do it from the front."

Blake's face did something complicated.

She stood in the light with the scissors in her fist. Her ears went back and came up. She looked at the stool, and at Weiss, and at the stool, as if calculating a distance.

"From the front," she said.

"If you'd like."

"I'd like to see your hands."

"Yes."

"Okay," said Blake, and sat down.

* * *

It was a different thing.

She had known it would be. She had not known how. She stood in front of the stool with the comb in one hand and the scissors in the other, in the thin cold light from the window, and Blake sat below her, with her face turned up and her back to the glass, so that every move Weiss made was in front of her eyes. She kept very still. She kept her hands in her lap. She watched Weiss's hands as one watches a bird.

The hair was fine. It was heavier than she had expected, and cool, and it had a faint sheen, an oil-colour, like ink before it dries. She lifted a lock at the side. She combed it down. It fell between her fingers in a silent rope.

The scissors made their small sound.

She took the length from the fringe first. It fell on Blake's lids and she shut them, and a hair lay across the lashes of one eye, and Weiss said, "May I?" and Blake said, "Yes," and she put her thumb to it and brushed it away. It was warm. The skin of the eyelid was very thin, and it moved under her thumb, once.

She went round the side.

"May I move this?"

She had her hand near the left ear. She did not touch it. She held her fingers in the air at a half-inch's distance from the rim, quite steady, a little cold.

Blake's eyes opened.

"Yes."

She put out her thumb. She set it, very lightly, against the edge, and bent the ear down and away from the scissors as one folds a page.

It was warm.

It was warmer than she had thought, and softer; it was velvet over a thin, resilient thing, a cartilage like the cartilage of her own, and as she pressed it she felt, with the pad of her thumb, a tiny quick pulse. It ran under the fur. It was going rather fast. It twitched, once, sideways, under her thumb, as the skin of a horse twitches, and settled; and she thought, very distinctly, in the place where she kept her thoughts about notes: *a hundred and ten.*

"Is it—"

"It's fine."

"I can stop."

"Don't stop."

She did not stop. She cut around the ear, a snip, a snip, with the tips of the blunt blades well clear, and her thumb on the rim; and then she moved to the other side, and said, "May I?" and was told "Yes," a little hoarsely, and did that one too. She worked slowly. She worked as she had once worked at the end of a long division, with her whole mind.

She became aware of the face.

It was a foot from her own. She had not been looking at it. She had been looking at the hair, and the scissors, and the ears; and when she raised her eyes it was right there, below her, turned up, eyes shut, with the lashes lying on the cheek in two black crescents and the mouth a little open. The cheekbones were flushed. There was a small scar at the corner of the jaw that she had not seen before.

Blake was holding her breath.

"You're holding your breath."

"Am I."

"Yes."

Blake let it out. It went over Weiss's wrist, warm, in a long slow sigh.

"Better," said Weiss.

She did not know what her own voice was doing. She snipped the last lock, over the left temple, and drew back, and stood with the scissors in her hand.

Blake opened her eyes.

They looked at each other. It was a foot. It was less. It lasted, perhaps, a good deal longer than was strictly required for the completion of a haircut; and neither of them moved, or said anything, or did any of the things that were available to be done. Weiss could hear, very distinctly, the stove, and a drip, and the dog's flank going up and down.

"Done," she said.

"Okay."

"You may look."

Blake stood. She did it with the same slow care with which she did all things, and turned to the mirror, and examined herself in the cracked glass with an air of cold attention. The hair fell neat and short at her jaw. The ears stood clear on either side, with their dark upright points. She turned her head to the left, and to the right.

"It's crooked," she said.

"It's not."

"It's a little crooked."

"It's *you,* and it's what it is."

Blake looked at her in the glass.

Something went across her face, and was gone. Her ears turned, both together, toward Weiss's reflection.

"It's good," she said.

* * *

The hair lay on the floor.

It lay between the stool and the washstand in a wide drift, in the light from the window, and in the draught from under the door it moved. There was a great deal of it. There was the white, long, fine, shorn in locks; there was the black, shorter, clean, in small crescents. They lay on the pine boards in a patch the size of a rug. They had begun, where the air moved them, to mix.

"I'll sweep," said Weiss.

"Leave it."

"It's a mess."

"Leave it for a bit."

She stood with the broom half lifted. Blake was looking at the floor. Her face was composed and a little tired and there was nothing on it that she could read, except that it was turned to the drift, steadily, as one looks at a thing one means to remember.

"All right," said Weiss. "Okay."

It stood there all afternoon. The dog came and sniffed at it. He walked a slow circle round the edge, with his head down, and then, with the air of a creature that has made a considered judgment, he went into the middle and lay down in it, in the black and white, and turned round once, and put his nose on his paws.

The light went along the floor. It was a clear day, perfectly still. In the office the stove ticked, and over it, in the cold, a thread of steam went up from the kettle and hung and thinned; and it came to her that every single part of this was going to end. The hair would be swept. The kettle would be dry. The light on the boards would go along and be gone before the hour. The two of them, in this room, with the dog, with these hands. She stood with the broom, quite still, and saw the whole of it laid out in the sun, like a coat on a bed, and felt neither grief nor its opposite; only a great, still, rather startled attention.

*Breath,* she thought. It was what the old word meant, she had been told. The Preacher's. *Vanity.* It had meant a breath, a puff of steam on a cold morning. It had never meant that nothing was worth anything. It had only meant: *look quickly.*

She leaned the broom against the wall.

"Staying?" said Blake, from the window, not turning round.

It was the afternoon. She had never asked it in the afternoon.

"Today," said Weiss.
