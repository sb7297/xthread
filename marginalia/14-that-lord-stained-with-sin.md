# 14. that lord, stained with sin

> *If there exists some Lord all powerful to fulfil*
> *In every creature bliss or woe, and action good or ill,*
> *That lord is stained with sin. Man does but work his will.*
>
> — Mahabodhi Jataka

It came on the breakfast salver on the sixty-first day, under a napkin, with Klein's face above it arranged in complete neutrality.

She saw the corner of it. She looked at him. He looked at the wall above her head, and went on pouring tea. After he had gone she lifted the napkin with the point of her knife.

A plain envelope. A Mantle postmark. *Miss W. Schnee, Schnee Manor, Atlas,* in pencil, in a square rough hand that pressed hard on the capitals.

She read it standing at the window.

> *Miss Schnee,*
>
> *Yours of the 3rd received. It is a neat hand.*
>
> *The second page says my claim has been reviewed and is without standing. I would be glad to be told what standing would be. My husband was Halvard Dahl, Miner Grade II, Number 4, twenty-two years.*
>
> *I do not ask for the money. I ask for the words.*
>
> *Tove Dahl*
> *Hollin Gap*

She read it a second time. Then she folded it along its creases and put it in the pocket of her skirt, and sat through breakfast, and a lesson on the harmonic series, and luncheon, and a fitting. She spoke when spoken to. She was, she thought, entirely correct.

At a quarter to six she went down to dinner.

* * *

The long room had been laid for five, as it always was, with the fifth place bare. The soup was clear. The silver was Atlesian, plain, heavy, cold to the lip. At the head of the table, her father spread his napkin across his knee in a single movement and said "Good evening," and she said "Good evening, Father," and her brother said it, and her mother, in a lower voice, who had taken her chair before the rest and had a glass at her elbow already, half full, and held to the light.

Klein stood at the sideboard. Orla poured. The clock in the hall chimed the quarter.

She waited through the soup. She waited for the fish. It came poached, in a white sauce, with small potatoes, and she took her knife and fork and waited until her father had had his first mouthful, and then she put them down.

"Father. I received a letter this morning."

"From?"

"From a Mrs. Dahl. At Hollin Gap."

Whitley's fork stopped. It stopped in the air, with a piece of potato on it, and stayed there. Across the table her mother's glass, which had been rising, paused at the level of her chin.

Her father wiped his mouth. He did it slowly. He set down the napkin.

"I see. That ought to have gone to Mr. Dacre's office. Klein?"

"I regret the error, sir."

"It will not occur again." He turned his eyes on her. They were blue and level and not unkind. "You needn't trouble yourself with it, Weiss. The office will reply."

"She writes that the second page of my letter says that her claim has been found without standing."

"Yes."

"I did not know what the second page said."

"No."

"You did."

"Of course," said her father.

He said it as one says *the sun rose.*

* * *

She had imagined this. She had lain in the dark for nights and imagined it in several forms, and in none of them had he said *of course.* She had imagined denial, or anger; she had imagined him rising from his chair. She had not thought that he would simply agree.

"The letters I signed were not condolences."

"They were that as well." He took up his glass of water. "The Company extended its sympathy to the families, and notified them at the same time that their claims had been reviewed and denied. It is customary to do both in the one letter. It is thought more humane."

"I was told I was signing a letter of sympathy."

"You were. It is also what you signed."

"I did not read the second page."

"No. You were not asked to."

She sat still. Her hands were in her lap. She could feel them there, folded, one over the other, in the way she had been taught, and she had the odd, clear thought that she would not be able to take them apart.

"Why me?"

"Because it is easier," said her father, "to receive a refusal from a girl who is sorry than from a lawyer."

Nobody spoke. Whitley was staring at his plate with an expression of fascinated terror, like a boy watching a house burn from a safe distance. The clock ticked in the hall.

"Jacques," said her mother.

"Willow."

That was all. It was, she thought, the whole of their marriage. It had been so since she was a child. It was a thing that said *please,* and was answered *no,* in two words, each way, with their names.

"Why were the claims denied?"

"Because the Company was not liable. The roof failed. Mining is a dangerous occupation, and the contracts are very clear. A report was commissioned, and the engineers found no negligence."

"Who commissioned the report?"

"The Company."

"The engineers?"

"The Company's."

"There was a request for shoring on Level 3."

He did not answer immediately. It was a small pause. It might have been a man swallowing.

"You've been talking to someone."

"I have read the Q2 summary. The support budget for Level 3 was reduced by forty per cent last autumn." She had not raised her voice. "It's on page nineteen. In the third table. I noticed it because the line before it had gone up."

Her father looked at her for some time.

He looked at her with an expression she had not seen on him before. It was a kind of attention, direct, undivided, and entirely without hostility; it was the way he looked at a ledger when the figures balanced. A man might have looked at a fine instrument that had turned out to work.

"You read it twice," he said.

"Yes."

"Good."

She felt it go through her like a cold hand. It was *pleasure.* He was pleased. It was the first time in years that he had been pleased with her, and she saw, with great clarity, that she had wanted it, and that she would go on wanting it, all her life, whatever else she did.

"Then you will also have seen," said her father, "that the reduction was made by the Board, on the recommendation of the Director of Operations, on the basis of the Council's allocation, which was reduced in the previous quarter in response to the Vacuan tariff. Each of those persons acted correctly within his office. If you wish to find the man who killed the men at Hollin Gap, you will find that you have a great many to choose from, and that each of them did exactly as he was asked."

"And you?"

"I signed."

"Then—"

"I am the part that signs." He said it gently. "As, in time, will you be. I do not say it to distress you. A company is a method by which a great many persons are enabled to do a small thing each, so that none of them need do a large one. It is a considerable moral achievement, and it has made your life possible. You will find it very restful."

* * *

She sat and looked at it.

It was a complete thing. It had no gap in it. She turned it over, as she turned all problems, searching for the edge. If the order came from one man's will, and the rest only worked it, then the man was answerable and the rest were instruments; and she was one. She had signed. She had a neat hand. *Man does but work his will,* the old tutor had said once, of some other matter, with his small dry cough. It was meant to be a consolation.

And if it came from no one's will, but from the arrangement, the whole slow arithmetic of Board and Council and tariff, then no one was answerable, and she was not, and nor was he; and thirty-one men were dead in a hill with a wall across the door.

She went round it twice. There was no place in it to stand and say *I.*

She saw that this was the point.

"I would like to be excused."

"You haven't finished your fish."

"I would like to be excused."

Her father regarded her. It went on a long time. She watched him decide something, and put it away, and take up his fork.

"You may."

"Thank you, Father."

She rose. She pushed in her chair, squarely. She did not look at her mother, or at Whitley, or at Klein, who was standing at the sideboard with his hands behind him and his long grey face turned, slightly, away.

She went out through the door at the end of the room. It was a long door, and it closed on a spring, and in the hall the light from the chandelier fell on the black and white tiles in great even squares. She walked across them. She heard, faintly, her own heels.

It was not until she was nearly at the stair that she found she was humming.

She did not know what it was she was humming. She did not know when she had begun. It came up out of her chest and into the air of the hall, three notes, a rest, the phrase that climbed; she heard it go up under the chandelier and spread.

"Weiss."

He had come to the dining-room door. He stood in it with the light behind him. He had not raised his voice.

"That tune."

"Yes?"

"Please don't."

She stopped.

Something crossed her father's face. She would think about it a good deal, afterward. It was not anger. It was not anything that she had a name for. It was as though he'd heard a sound from the next room, in a house he had been assured was empty, and was waiting, very patiently, for the sound to come again.

"Not in this house," said her father.

* * *

She did not weep. She went up to her room and shut the door and stood in the middle of it for some while.

Then she knelt by the wardrobe and took down the hatbox.

The glove was on top. She put it on her left hand and took it off. Under it lay the sheet, folded in three; under that the marbled notebook, with its thirty-one names, in her best hand; under that the book with its green cloth spine. She turned it over. It was still warped from the damp. It fell open, as it always did, at page 112.

She took the pencil from the desk.

She had not written in it before. She had read it through four times; she had read the margins, and the long unanswered line on page 14 with its hook at the end. She sat on the floor with her back to the bed. She looked for a long while at the strip of white at the edge of the page.

Then she wrote it, small, in her steep light hand, under the last of Blake's notes. She did not write a date. She did not write a name.

*I signed.*

She put the pencil down. She closed the book. She sat there until it was quite dark, and outside the window, in the east garden, the lamps came on one after another along the paths.
