# 26. afterimage

She came out at five.

She had not slept. She had lain on the cot in the second room, in her clothes, with the lamp out, and said it over to the ceiling until the words wore smooth and began to mean nothing, and then gone back to the start and said it again. It had been a long night. It was possible, she thought, to rehearse a thing so many times that the rehearsal became the thing; and she had decided that this was not a danger, because she did not intend to say it well.

She opened the door.

The office was dark but for the stove. Yang lay on the floor beside it in a nest of blankets with the sheepskin over her head, snoring in long gentle swells. The dog lay at the foot of the desk with his chin on his paws. And at the stove, with her back to the room, in the grey coat, with the kettle in her hand, someone was making tea.

Weiss stopped.

She stood with her hand on the door frame. The shape in front of her was very still. The coat hung from the shoulders in its long familiar folds. The hair was black, cut square at the nape; the ears were up, bare, two dark upright shapes against the glow of the grille.

She had not thought that Blake would be here.

She had thought of a number of things, in the night, and none of them was this. She stood and felt her heart go once, hard, against her ribs; and she did what she had said she would do, and did not stop to ask herself why. She hummed.

Three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

The figure did not turn.

It did not move at all. At the first bar the ear on the left should have swung back; it always did; it was one of the few things in the world that Weiss had learned to depend upon. The ear stayed where it was. She finished the eight bars. The silence afterward was long.

*She is angry,* she thought. *She is ignoring me.* It was a reasonable conclusion. It was a conclusion she would have drawn about herself.

"Blake."

Nothing.

"I should like to say something." Her voice came out quite level. "I should like you not to answer until I have finished. You needn't answer at all."

She walked into the room. She did not go close. She stopped at the desk, six feet off, with one hand on the corner of it, and looked at the back of the grey coat and the black hair, and she said it.

"You were right."

The kettle ticked in the dark hand.

"I decided for you. I wrote *rest* on a sheet and gave it to you with both hands and I thought that was the same as care. I was taught that it was. I was taught it by a man at the head of a table, who did it to everyone in the house, and I did not know I had learnt it until you said so." She breathed. "I'm not saying that to excuse it. I'm telling you where I found it."

The stove ticked. Behind her Yang turned over in her sleep and said something to a goose.

"I'm not Adam."

It cost her something to say. It came out whole, and heavy, like a stone she had carried a long way and set down.

"I know you did not say I was. I understand why it felt the same. I would have felt it too."

She had been looking at the shoulders, at the place where the coat met the collar. It was somewhere about here, she realised, that she had begun to listen.

She was a singer. She had been trained to the breath. She could have told you, across a hall, who in the second row was afraid, by the way the air went in and out; she could hear a lie in the shoulders. She stood in the dim room and listened, as she had listened once in a bright arena, to a single instrument, and there was no breath.

There was the stove. There was the dog's. There was Yang's slow one, snoring through a blanket.

There was her own, a little quick.

Nothing else.

She had known it for some time, she thought. She had known since the ear. She stood with her hand on the corner of the desk and found she had known all along, in the place below the lecture, where the earlier teachers had kept their things; and she found that it made no difference to what came next.

"I don't want to do it," said Weiss. "I would like to ask you, instead. Before. About the stove, and the river, and everything, I expect. I'll be bad at it."

The figure stood in the firelight.

"I should like to be bad at it near you."

That was all she had. She had prepared more, but it had gone. She stood and let the quiet come back into the room, and it came, and was full of the small noises of a house at dawn: the stovepipe, the window, a drip in the lean-to. She finished the speech. She said the last line aloud to a back that could not hear it.

"You may say whatever you like. You don't have to say anything."

She waited.

It was the right thing to do. She had always been good at the right thing. She stood for perhaps a minute in the half dark with her hands at her sides, and the kettle in the other hand did not move, and the steam did not come from its spout.

"Blake?"

Nothing.

"May I?"

She crossed the six feet. It was no distance at all. She put out her hand, the left, in its black glove, and laid it very lightly on the grey wool, at the shoulder, where the coat was warm from the fire.

It came away under her palm.

There was no resistance. There was a faint cool sliding, as of a sheet of smoke; the coat, the shoulders, the black hair, the upright ears, all of it lifted off the air in one piece and thinned and went out, into the dim, into the stove light, as a breath goes out on a pane. It left a smell, or no smell. It left a shape in the corner of her eye, and then not that.

The kettle fell.

It struck the floor with a ring, and rolled, and stopped against the dog, who lifted his head. It was cold. It had been empty. She stood in front of the stove with her hand still held out, and the glove on it, and nothing under it.

On the other side of the room the door of the hut stood a hand's width open, and the cold was coming in.

* * *

"What?" said Yang.

She had come up out of the blankets with the sheepskin round her shoulders and her hair in a bird's nest. She stared at the kettle. She stared at Weiss, standing very straight in the middle of the floor with an arm out, and at the open door, and at the white light in it, and her face went through three expressions in a second and a half.

"She—"

"Yes."

Yang was at the door. She was through it, in her bare feet, and out onto the step, and Weiss heard her breath go in; and she heard the voice, from the yard, raw and cracked, as it had been once before, in a different place.

"There's tracks."

"Yes."

"They go— She's two hours gone. *Weiss.*" She was back in the door. "She's— I can catch her. I've got the machine. She can't have gone more than—"

"Don't."

It was not loud. It was said with perfect politeness. Weiss had not turned her head. She was looking at the kettle on the floor.

"I'm asking you not to."

"You're—"

"I'm *asking.*" She heard her voice shake, a very little, and let it. "She's allowed."

Yang stood in the doorway in her bare feet with the dawn behind her. The wind moved her hair. She looked at Weiss for some time with an expression that had in it, among a number of other things, a great deal of the old fury, and something else underneath it, like the line of a coast under a fog.

"She's *allowed,*" she said.

"Yes."

"She left a *shadow.*"

"Yes."

"And you're— you're just going to—"

"I'd like her to be able to," said Weiss. "I'd like to be a place where one is able to. Do you understand? I'd like the door to be—" She stopped. "I don't know how to do the rest of it. But I've found out what I mustn't. And that's one."

Yang said nothing for a good while.

Then she shut the door, quietly, with her back, and the cold stopped. She came across the floor. She bent and picked up the kettle, and turned it over in her good hand, and looked into the spout.

"You two," she said.

She did not finish. She shook her head. She went to the pump in the corner and filled the kettle, and set it on the stove, and the stove began to hiss.

* * *

The book was on the desk.

It was closed. It had been on the shelf the night before; she had put it there herself, at the end of the shelf, square to the edge. She stood and looked at it. Then she picked it up and it fell open in her hand at the place where it always did, though it was not page 112.

Page 14.

She read the margin.

The old argument. The hook. Her own *Whoever reads it.* Below that a word, *Reading,* in a small clear upright hand, which she had found on the tenth evening and had not forgotten. And below that, in the same pencil, new, hasty, a little slanted to the right:

*Rule 12: notice given. —B.*

She read it twice.

For a time she did not move. She stood with the book open in both hands and the pencil line gleaming a little in the light from the stove, and it was perfectly plain and perfectly absurd. It was legal. It was a shadow in a kitchen and a note in a book and a person on a mountain in the dark, and it said, in so many words: *I have complied.*

A sound came out of her.

It was not the snort. It was not any sound she had made before. It was dry, and short, and came up from somewhere under the breastbone like a cough, and it made Yang turn from the stove and stare.

"It's a rule," said Weiss.

"A what?"

"Twelve. No leaving without notice." She held the book out. "It's in ink."

Yang took it. She read. Her brows went together and then slowly came apart.

"I wrote the zero," she said, after a moment, in a peculiar voice. "On that sheet. Rule zero. It overrides the rest."

"Does it?"

"I wrote it. I get to say." She looked at the page. She looked up. "It says I can go after her."

"I've asked you not to."

"I know," said Yang. "That's why it's weird."

The kettle began to sing.

She turned and lifted it and made tea in the pot, with the blue-and-white strainer, and put the lid on. She stood over it. Weiss sat down in the chair with one arm. She did not know when she had sat. She held the book in her lap, shut now, with her thumb in the place.

Yang set a cup in front of her.

It was strong, and black. It smelled of smoke. She picked it up in both hands and the heat went into the bones of them, and she looked into it, and saw it was dark with sugar. Three at least. She had never in her life taken sugar.

She drank it.

It was dreadful. It was sweet to the point of being unrecognisable as tea. She drank all of it, slowly, to the bottom, and put the cup down; and the dog came and put his head on her knee, and looked up at her, and neither of them said anything for a long time.
