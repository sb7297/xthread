# 4. a reasonable position

The screen above the serving line was supposed to show the schedule for the week's matches. Someone had changed it to the news.

Blake saw it a half second after the others, because she was cutting her fish. A train on a siding. Steam, or smoke, and a reporter with a microphone held tight to her chin, and behind her the long boxcars with their doors torn off. The picture was good. She could see the stencil on the nearest car. A snowflake in a ring.

"—the fourth such attack on a Schnee Dust Company freight this quarter. A company spokesman said losses were—"

"Huh," said Yang.

"Do they say who?" said Ruby.

Weiss didn't turn around. She was sitting straight with her back to the screen, and her tea in front of her, and she had not stopped eating; she cut a piece of chicken and put it in her mouth and chewed it, and it was clear from the sound of her jaw that she had heard every word.

"It's the White Fang."

"They didn't say that," said Ruby.

"They never say. It's always the White Fang."

"Always?" said Yang.

"Four trains. The same method. The same cut on the couplings. Does anyone at this table honestly think it's pirates?"

"I mean," said Ruby, "it could—"

"It isn't."

Blake finished cutting the fish. She laid the knife across the edge of the plate. It was quiet, the knife, on the china.

"I'd like to say something before somebody says it for me," said Weiss. She put her fork down. "I am not saying anything against Faunus. My family employs more than four thousand of them. I know a great many. Some of them looked after—" She stopped. She went around it, in a small neat arc, the way you go around a puddle. "I know them. They are perfectly decent people. What I'm saying is that an organisation that sets a charge on a train with guards inside it is not a political movement. It is a gang with a slogan."

"There were guards on it?" said Yang.

"Two in the second car. One of them is still in hospital."

Nobody said anything. Over by the windows a first-year dropped a tray and the whole room clapped, the way a room does.

"Maybe they've got reasons," said Yang. She said it lightly, looking at her bread.

"Everyone has reasons," said Weiss. "Having a reason isn't the same as being right. It's a perfectly reasonable position."

Blake had put both hands in her lap. She noticed it, in the way you notice something you are doing at the far end of the table. Under the ribbon her ears were lying flat to her skull. They did it without being told, as they had always done, and they ached at the root with the effort of it, and in them she could hear her own pulse, slow and loud, like a clock on the other side of a wall.

She knew the number. Four thousand two hundred and twelve, as of the spring report. She could have given the average weekly wage, and the housing deduction, and what was left. She had once read it aloud to forty people in a cellar in Kuo Kuana, and they had listened to the last figure in silence, and then a man in the back had laughed and kept laughing for some time.

She could say it now. All of it. It would be easy to say, and it would be correct, and it would cost her the room.

"May I be excused," she said.

Weiss looked at her for the first time. "You haven't finished."

"I'm not hungry."

She stood, and picked up her tray. She walked at an even pace to the return, and set the tray in the slot, and did not run.

* * *

The woman at the dish window had small, round ears, set high, dark with a fringe of pale fur. They stuck out from her hairnet like two buttons. She took the tray and looked at the fish, and then at the ribbon, and then at the fish.

"Not hungry?"

"No."

"It's good fish."

"I know."

The woman said nothing else. She wrapped the fillet in a square of waxed paper, folded the ends as neatly as a letter, and slid it across the steel. Blake took it. It was warm in her hand, like something alive.

"Thank you."

"Mm," said the woman, and was already turning to the next tray.

* * *

She ate it on the east stair, in the dark, where the steps ran out against the wall and there was a ledge no wider than a book. It was cold. She ate with her fingers. It was good fish.

She tried to think about the train, and thought instead about the stencil on the car. It was a clean stencil. Whoever had made it had cut it carefully, with a steady hand, and it had been used a great many times without the edges getting soft. Someone had been paid to do that. Someone had a job making that mark.

*Four thousand,* she thought. *I know a great many.*

It was true, probably. The woman was not a liar. She would have met the housekeeper and the cook and the man who polished the cars. She would have learned their names. She would have gone on thinking they were what she had met.

And the other thing was also true, which was that Blake had stood on a roof like that one, in the dark, with a cutter in one hand and a lantern in the other, and had watched the same sort of train come in. She had never done a thing to a guard that she could not have defended in the moment. Whether she could defend it now was something she kept in a drawer.

So she did not argue. She was not sure that was cowardice. She was not sure that it wasn't.

She sat on the ledge until the bells rang eleven and then twelve.

* * *

At ten past three, the door opened. Blake was on her bunk, with the lamp on, and the book on her knees, not read. Yang and Ruby slept. In the doorway, Weiss stood in her coat with her shoes in her hand, carried by the heels so as not to make a noise.

They both stopped.

"You're awake," said Weiss.

"The lamp's on."

"It's low."

"Yes."

Weiss crossed the room and put her shoes in the wardrobe, heels to the back wall. She hung up her coat. She stood with her back to the room and her hand flat on the wardrobe door, for what was longer than it took to close a wardrobe door.

"At dinner," she said.

"Mm."

"Did I say something?"

Blake looked at the back of her head. The tie had come loose, and a white-gold thread of hair lay along the nape of her neck. There was a smudge of ink on the collar.

"No."

"Because it seemed—"

"It's fine."

Weiss's hand moved a little on the door. It looked as if she had a sentence ready and had run it through, and found it wanting, and was now looking for a different one in the wardrobe.

"All right," she said.

She got into bed. She lay on her side, turned toward the wall. After a time she said, in a small clear voice, to the plaster, "Goodnight, Blake."

"Goodnight."

The lamp stayed on. Blake lay on her back and listened to the other heartbeat across the room until it slowed, which took forty minutes. She had never listened to anyone's so closely. She did not know whether it was a kindness or a theft.

She did not turn the lamp off.
