# 29. problem of evil

*The office. The stove banked. The lamp out. Two blankets on the floor in front of it, and a dog between them.*

— Staying?

— Today.

— Good.

— That was this morning.

— I know. I'm checking it holds.

— It holds.

*The stove ticks.*

— Are you awake?

— Obviously.

— I wasn't going to ask.

— You're asking.

— I'm asking if you would like me to leave you alone.

— No.

— Right.

— Stay.

— Yes.

*A long time.*

— I thought it would feel like something.

— Yes. You said. To your mother.

— I told you that?

— On the third night. You were asleep. I don't think you were there.

— What else did I say?

— "Drink."

— That was her.

— You said it back.

— Mm.

*The dog sighs.*

— What was it like?

— You can say you'd rather not.

— No. I'd — I want to say it to someone who won't make a face.

— I don't think I know how to make one.

— You've made several.

— They were for other things.

— It was like being told to put something down.

— Yes?

— A plate. I'd been carrying a plate for six years, in both hands, very level. And someone said, you can put it on the table. And I couldn't. My arms wouldn't. They'd forgotten the other way.

— Yes.

— You can't just open them.

— No.

— It's been months and I still go round the room as if there's soup on it.

— I've noticed.

— Of course you have.

*Water, in the basin, a long way off. A cup against enamel.*

— I did it with wanting.

— Yes.

— That's the part. It was necessary. I'd do it again. I did it clean enough. But in the middle I — I wanted. I could feel it. It wasn't a duty. It was the best thing I'd ever felt.

— Yes.

— You're not shocked.

— No.

— Why?

— Because I have wanted.

— Who?

— My father. At dinner. In the middle of a sentence. He was very reasonable and I wanted to take the table and put it through the window.

— You didn't.

— I said I'd like to be excused.

— That's not the same.

— No. But wanting isn't rare. I found that quite — I'd always thought it was a sort of stain on a few people. It's not. It's a weather.

— He's dead and I'm glad.

— Yes.

— I'm not supposed to be.

— Who says?

— I do. And a list.

— Whose?

— Everyone I ever — I don't know. My father. The ones who went into the Fang for the right reasons. I stood on the sand with his — and I thought, *there are people who would say he was a man.* And he was. He had a mother. He — it doesn't make a difference.

— No.

— I'm glad. It doesn't cancel the other thing.

— What other thing?

— That I'd have rather not had to.

— Both.

— It's always both with you.

— It's usually both.

*The cup, set down. Water draining.*

— I keep wanting a reason.

— There's a reason. He wanted a certain thing and was willing to pay for it in other people.

— That's a cause.

— What's the difference?

— A reason would make it fit. It would go in a — you'd put it on a shelf and it'd be the right size. A cause is just: this, then this.

— Nothing makes it fit.

— That's what I can't stand.

— Yes.

*Silence. A branch of the wind on the roof.*

— When I was small I thought things were arranged.

— Arranged.

— That somebody was keeping the books. That if you were good the entries came out even. I thought it very hard. I was extremely good. I folded my blanket in thirds.

— I know.

— And then I learned to read the books.

— And?

— There's a Board.

*A sound, short, dry, in the dark: a breath that was nearly a laugh.*

— Don't.

— I'm not.

— You are.

— "There's a Board."

— I'm not joking.

— I know. That's why.

— It was a very upsetting discovery.

— I'm sure.

— There's an argument. I had it from a tutor. In the old language, with a cough. If there is someone who could stop it, and does not—

— Then he's either not good, or not able.

— Or not there.

— That's three.

— It's always three.

— Which one's Ozpin?

— All of them. On different days.

— That's not an answer.

— It's the best I have.

*The stove ticks.*

— If someone were in charge, he'd have to answer.

— Yes.

— And if no one is, it's us.

— Yes.

— I don't know which is worse.

— The second.

— Why?

— Because in the first there's someone to be furious at.

— You'd rather there was a lord.

— I'd rather there was someone I could hand it back to. It would be restful.

— Restful.

— I know.

— You said it.

— I know. I heard.

*A pause. Blake's voice, when it comes, is lower.*

— I'm sorry about the book.

— Which?

— Using it. Page fifty-eight. You asked me not to read it when you were in the room, and I read it, and then I — I held it up. In front of Yang.

— Yes.

— I wanted to hit you and it was the nearest thing.

— It was well chosen.

— I know.

— It was extremely accurate.

— I know.

— I minded it more than the other. If you want to know.

— More than Adam?

— Yes. Adam was a — you were frightened, and you said what frightened you. The book was something you picked. I'd given you it with both hands.

— Yes.

— It's all right.

— It's not.

— No. It isn't. Hold on. I'll say it better. It's not all right and I'd like to go on.

— Okay.

— Okay.

*A long time.*

— He was never wrong.

— Who?

— Adam. Not once. In six years. I'm trying to think. If he was ever wrong, I'd know. I can't find it.

— Mm.

— That was how he got to be what he was. He couldn't stop. If you're never wrong there's nowhere to turn round.

— My father either.

— And Ozpin.

— And the General. And Winter, a little.

— Everyone who's got a plan.

— Everyone who's certain.

— I think I'd rather be wrong.

— You're extremely good at it, lately.

— I'm getting better.

— You are.

— I'm working at it.

— I've seen the list.

— You haven't.

— It was on the desk.

— Oh.

— I didn't read it.

— You read it.

— I read the title.

— That's — yes. Fair.

— It was a good title.

— It was an honest one. I thought you'd be annoyed.

— I was. For about a line. Then I was — I don't know what the word is for it.

— Neither do I.

*The dog turns over, with a thump.*

— Blake.

— Mm.

— May I hold your hand?

— Why?

— I'd like to.

*Nothing for a while.*

— Okay.

*A rustle in the blankets. Nothing else.*

— That's cold.

— You're one to talk.

— You've no gloves on.

— I'm indoors.

— Yes.

— It's all right. It's — yes. It's fine.

— Is it?

— It's good. It's quite good.

— You're shaking.

— So are you.

— It's the cold.

— It's warm in here.

— I know.

*The stove ticks. Outside, far off, on the hill, something small goes through the snow and stops.*

— Weiss.

— Yes.

— I don't think I'll sleep.

— No.

— Stay anyway.

— Yes.

— Tell me something.

— What?

— Anything. Something true. It can be small.

— The tea is too sweet.

— That's not a true thing.

— It is. Yang put three in. I've been drinking it for a week. I didn't want to say.

— Why not?

— It would have been ungrateful.

— To Yang?

— To her having done it.

— You can say.

— It's too sweet.

— Thank you.

— Your turn.

— I — hum, when I'm not thinking. In the bunkhouse. At night.

— I know.

— You *know*?

— It's the same eight bars.

— Oh.

— You do it at the end. You hold the top too long.

— That's the way it's written.

— I like it.

— It's the way it's written, you know.

— I know.

— Tell me another.

— It's your turn.

— I'm — I find I've forgotten how to do it. Sleep.

— Then we'll sit.

— Yes.

*The stove ticks. The dog breathes. Neither of them says anything else for a long time, and when the window goes grey, they are both still sitting up, and one of them has been asleep for an hour, with her head on the other's shoulder, and the other has not moved.*
