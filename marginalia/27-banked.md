# 27. banked

Yang left on the second day.

She did not want to. It was clear in everything she did: the length of time it took to pack a bag that had been packed since the day before, the way she stood with the key in the snow machine and her thumb on it and did not turn it. She had eaten a good breakfast. She had split wood for an hour without being asked, in a sort of fury, and stacked it, and stacked it again. At ten she stood in the yard in the sheepskin and said, "I could stay."

"You could."

"I'd like to."

"I know." Weiss was on the step with the dog. "I'd like you to tell Ruby something."

"Anything."

"That I'm alive. That I'm sorry about the letters. That I'll write."

"You said that last time."

"Yes," said Weiss. "I'm saying it again. It's a rule."

Yang looked at her. She had her goggles up on her cap and her breath going white, and she stood with her feet apart on the packed snow like a woman about to go through a door.

"Ruby's gonna be so mad at me," she said. "For coming back without you."

"Tell her I'm stubborn."

"She knows."

"Tell her anyway."

Yang came up the step and put both arms round her. The cold one went across her shoulders; the warm one held the back of her head. It lasted rather longer this time. When it was done she pulled away, and sniffed, and said gruffly, "A month. If she's not back in a month, I'm coming up here and getting you both."

"That would be very welcome."

She heard it. She heard the word go out. It was the same word, and it went out into the air and was something different, a small clear plain thing, and Yang, who had a good ear, grinned.

"Rule zero," she said, and went.

The machine whined down the track, and dwindled, and was gone. On the desk behind Weiss, in the hut, in a net bag, five oranges lay in the lamplight. They had been left. She had not asked.

* * *

The radio spoke on the third day.

She had left it on. She had not known that she had. It had been a dull click and a hiss in the corner for two days, a sound she had stopped hearing, like a clock; and at eleven in the morning, across the hiss, a voice cut in. It was cracked and flat and extremely loud, and it said:

"Tove here."

Weiss crossed the room in two strides. She had the handset off its hook before she knew it. "Go ahead."

"Your cat's in my kitchen."

The room went out of true.

"She's eating." The voice hissed, and steadied. "She's rude. She's been in the corner by the stove for twenty hours. She's had two bowls of soup, and the heel of a loaf. She's said nine words. Four were *thank you.*"

"Is she—"

"She's eating."

"Is she—"

"I said." There was a pause. "She isn't hurt. She's cold. She's a person that's gone a long way and stopped. Don't come."

"I won't."

"Don't send that blonde one."

"I won't."

"I'll say when. Over." And the line clicked, and hissed, and was a clock again.

Weiss stood in the middle of the floor with the handset in her fist. It had a smell: cold bakelite and oil. After a time she became aware that she was holding it against her forehead, with her eyes shut, as one holds a hand there to check for a fever. She did not know how long.

The dog had come and sat down against her knee.

"She's eating," she said.

He looked up.

"Nine words." She put the handset on its hook. It took three tries. "Four were *thank you.*"

* * *

On the fourth day she made a list.

It was what she did. She had never done anything else in her life with a feeling that had no name; she took a clean sheet and ruled it, and wrote at the top, in her best hand, with a date.

*Things I Have Decided For Blake Without Asking.*

She looked at it. It was not the title she had intended. It had come out of the pen by itself. She did not cross it out.

*1. That she should rest. (Days 1–3.) [Schedule. Written. Handed over.]*

*2. That she would not want to talk about the door.*

*3. That she would prefer the stove lit.*

*4. That she slept badly. (She does. This is not the point.)*

*5. That the river was not safe.*

*6. That she wanted to be spared lamp day.*

*7. That she wished to be spared.*

*8. That I knew what she meant by "Okay."*

*9. That I should answer when Tove asked her a question.*

*10. That I should answer when Yang did.*

*11. That her silence meant yes.*

*12. That her silence meant no.*

*13. That she was tired, when she was tired. That she was tired, when she was not.*

*14. That I knew what she wanted.*

*15. That I was the person to say.*

She read it down. It was a good list. It was exact. She could find nothing in it to correct. She saw that it went on, below, in invisible ink, for a long way, and she saw how far. There was the whole of a house in it. There was a long table, and a man at the head of it, and a woman at the foot with a glass; there were children who learned to cut meat into pieces of the same size.

*16. That it was love.*

She sat looking at that one for some time.

She did not cross it out. It was probably true. It was the worst thing on the page.

She took a second sheet.

*Things I Have Not Asked.*

*What she would like for breakfast.*

*Whether she would like the window seat.*

*Whether she would like to talk about the door.*

*Whether she would like to talk about him.*

*What the dog is to her.*

*What she wants.*

She wrote the last in a smaller hand. She looked at it. Then, below it, after a long minute, she wrote the line she had known she would write from the start.

*Whether she wants to stay.*

She put down the pen.

* * *

It was the dog who got the first of them.

She had not planned it. He was lying across the foot of the cot, and she wished to move him, and put out her hand, and stopped. It was a ridiculous thing. It was a stupid, shaming, small thing; and she sat on the edge of the bed with her hand in the air over his head and her face getting hot.

"May I?"

The dog opened one eye.

"I should like to move you," said Weiss. "You're on my feet. Would you mind?"

He considered. He sighed through his nose, with immense reluctance, and removed himself an inch and a half.

"Thank you."

It was the first time that day she had felt anything but cold. She tried it on him again, at supper. *Would you like the fish skin?* He would. *Is it all right if I open the window?* He did not care. *May I sit here?* He moved, and she sat, and he lay down again with his back against her calf and his chin on her shoe.

She was aware that this was ridiculous. She was aware that she was a girl of eighteen, with an education, in an inspector's hut in a gale, asking a sled dog if he would mind. She did it anyway. She did it until the asking stopped feeling like a wall, with her forehead up against it, and began to feel like a door with a handle: a thing one could try, and have said no to, and survive.

"Is it all right," she said, "if I'm not very good at this?"

The dog's tail moved, once, on the boards.

* * *

She went over each evening to bank the stove.

It was a quarter of a minute across the yard. She went at eleven, in the dark, with the lamp. And at the door of the bunkhouse she stopped, in the snow, with her hand on the thong, and hummed.

Three notes. A rest. The phrase that climbed.

She did the full eight bars. She did them clearly, facing the door, in the cold, to an empty building. Then she waited. She waited for a count of five, as one does for a person to answer, and there was nothing, and she untied the thong and went in.

It was dark. It smelled of old wool and cold. She lit the lamp on the rail, and went down the aisle past the posts with their names, and knelt at the barrel and laid the fire.

On the pillow of the stove-end bunk she left a note.

She put it there each night, folded once. She wrote them in ink, in her good hand, in the office, after the bunkhouse was dark; and each time she was careful with the wording, and each time she wrote it three times before she was satisfied.

*Stove lit. If you would rather it were not, say so, and I shan't. —W.*

*Stove lit again. Same offer.*

*I should like to leave an orange on this pillow. May I? (If not, I'll eat it.)*

*I have eaten the orange.*

*I did not light the stove tonight. I wasn't sure. It is cold. I will light it if you would like to find it warm. Leave word.*

She lay awake that night in the cot in the second room with the dog on her feet and listened to the wind on the roof, and at four she got up in the dark, and put on her boots and crossed the yard in her nightdress and the coachman's coat, and lit it.

She stood on the step of the bunkhouse afterward, in the cold, breathing hard.

*That was not asking,* she thought. *That was deciding with a note.*

She went back in. She stood a long while at the washstand with her hands in the basin. Then she took a clean sheet and wrote the last.

*I lit the stove. I said I wouldn't, and then I did. I'm sorry. I find that I would rather you came back to a warm room than a clean principle, and I'm aware that this is exactly the kind of thing I'm trying not to do. I am doing it anyway. You may tell me off.*

She looked at it. It was not good. It was too long, and it rambled.

She left it as it was.

* * *

She had expected to be angry.

She had it ready. It was in a drawer, a clean bright cold thing, the way she had had it ready once in a dormitory at five in the morning. She took it out and looked at it. It would not come. It lay in her hand like a thing from a shop.

What came instead was a long grey quiet.

It was like the quiet of the camp. She knew it now. She had thought, when she came, that it was the quiet of a place that had been left; it was not. It was the quiet of a place that was waiting. She sat in the chair with one arm, with the lamp out and the dog on her feet and the stove ticking, and found that in the quiet she could see all the way to the bottom of what she had done.

It was not that she had been cruel. She would have known what to do with cruelty. It was that she had been so careful. She had been so careful, for so long, to be kind in the only way she had been shown, and the only way she had been shown was a hand over a mouth.

She did not know who she was without it.

She sat in the dark and thought about that, quite calmly, with the whole of her attention, as she had once thought about a problem in a difficult score. It was entirely possible, she thought, that she was going to find out.

* * *

On the seventh night she wrote one more, short and steady, and folded it in four, and laid it on top of the others.

On the eighth night she did not leave a note.

She had nothing more to say. She had run through the supply. She laid the fire and lit it, and stood up from the barrel, and wiped her hands on her skirt, and it was only as she turned to go that she saw that the pillow of the stove-end bunk was not as she had left it.

It had a hollow in the middle, the shape of a head. The blanket had been drawn up and folded back. And the notes were gone: all seven, the whole small stack, which she had left squared on the tick. She looked on the floor. She looked under the bunk. The dog, who had come with her, stood at the end of it with his nose in the straw, shuddering all over, making a high thin sound.

Weiss stood and did not move.

There was a smell in the room. It was faint. It was the smell of a cold coat, and of woodsmoke, and of a long way off, and under it something like salt.

She looked at the bunk for a long time. She did not call out. She did not go to the door. Then she did the only thing she could think of that was neither a command nor a flinch: she put the lamp down on the rail, carefully, where the light would fall, and went out, and shut the door softly behind her.

She stood on the step in the snow.

The sky was perfectly clear. The stars stood hard and close over the roofs, and the whole camp lay under them in a blue stillness, every pole and rail and chimney drawn in fine black, as on a plate. At the edge of the yard, by the woodpile, thirty yards off, there was a shape that she had been taking, for the last minute, for a stack of logs.

It was not a stack of logs.

It was grey. It stood quite upright. It had two points at the top of its head, and it was not moving, and it was looking at her.

She did not go toward it. She had learnt that, at least.

She hummed.

It was for the form of the thing. She did the eight bars, clearly, across the thirty yards, in the dark, with her breath going up. Her voice shook on the climb. She held the top a little too long, as one does, and let it fall.

She waited.

"May I come over?" said Weiss.

The yard was quiet. A long way off on the hill something small went through the snow and stopped. The figure at the woodpile did not move at all, and she stood, and counted five, and then, because she was not finished, counted five again.

"Yes," said Blake.

It was hoarse. It was low. It crossed the snow and arrived, whole.
