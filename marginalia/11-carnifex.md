# 11. carnifex

*Atlas.*

The house smelled of floor wax and cold stone. It had smelled that way all her life and she had not noticed until she was away from it.

They took her scroll on the first day. It was for security, said her father. She had been through a great deal. There were people who would want to speak to her, and it was better that she were not spoken to; and he put his hand on her shoulder for exactly as long as it took to say it, and took it away again.

She had her old room. Someone had changed the coverlet. The window looked out onto the east garden, where a gardener she did not know was cutting back the roses in straight lines. There was a guard at the end of the corridor, who was a young man with a good haircut, and who called her *miss* in the voice of a person who had been told what the word was for.

Breakfast was at seven. Luncheon was at one. Dinner was at six, in the long room, with the table laid for four and a fifth place set and left. Her mother came down for it, mostly. Her brother came down for it always. He had grown. He cut his meat into pieces of identical size.

On the ninth day her father had a binder brought up to her room.

It was black leather with a ribbon in it, and a label that said *Q2 — Operations Summary.* "You ought to begin to see how it's done," he said. "In case. You are the eldest present."

She read it that afternoon, at the desk, with the window open.

It was an excellent document. It was laid out in columns. There were sections for production and shipping, for tariffs, for the price of Dust per ton in each of the four kingdoms. There was a section called *Incidents.* It was a table, with seven columns, and it ran for eleven pages: *Date. Site. Nature. Injured. Fatal. Disposition. Notes.*

She read it from the top. She read a line that said *Derailment, Line 9, 2 injured, 0 fatal. Settled.* She read *Fire, warehouse, Mantle, 4 injured. Settled.* She read the word *Settled* some forty times.

On page seven it said:

*Mine No. 4 (Hollin Gap). Roof failure, Level 3. 29 Faunus, 2 other. 31 fatal. Settled.*

She read it twice, as she did.

Outside, a gardener dropped his shears. The sound came through the open window like a stone going down a well, and she found she had not turned a page in some time.

* * *

*Menagerie.*

The sun was the first thing.

It came in at six through a slatted shutter and lay across the bed in bars. Her ears were bare in it. She had not covered them since the boat. It was strange, and then it was not strange, and then she began to notice that people looked.

They looked at the ears, and then at her face, and then at the ground. They had a way of doing it that was almost courtesy. In the market a woman put down a basket of limes and stood and watched her go by. At the harbour a boy of about ten said, to no one, "That's Ghira's," and was hushed. An old man spat in the dust at her feet, not with violence, only as one clears a throat.

She mended nets. A man called Tobbe, who was past seventy and had a bad hip and no teeth, had shown her where to sit, in the shade of a hull, and had put a needle in her hand, and had not asked a single question. She mended with him for six hours a day. She was slow at it. He let her be slow.

At night she sat on the back step of her parents' house with a notebook.

It was a cheap one, bought at the harbour. She had held it for four days before she wrote in it. On the fifth she opened it and ruled it in columns, with the edge of a book. *Date. Place. What. Who. After.* At the head of the first page she wrote a word she had found once in the glossary of a history of the old war, and had never been able to put down. *Carnifex.* It was an old word. It meant the one who does the cutting.

She began.

*Rail yard, third quay, Mistral. Retrieved cargo.*

She looked at *retrieved.* It was what the report had said. She had written the report herself, in a clear hand, for Adam, on thin blue paper. She struck it out, and wrote *stole.*

*Night watchman. Neutralised.*

*Hit him.*

*After:* She did not know. She had not looked back. She wrote, *man, grey uniform.* In the *Who* column she wrote it again. *Man, grey uniform.* She had known his face. She sat for a long time, with the pencil over it, and could not make it come.

She went on. She did it for three hours. By the end the page was three-quarters full, and the last quarter was a column of the same word.

*Unknown.*

At some point her mother came out, with a tea tray, and set a cup down on the step beside her.

Kali was good at not looking. She did it as some people play the piano. She stood, a moment, in the doorway, with the light from the kitchen behind her, and her tail moved once against the frame; then she lowered herself onto the step. Blake put her hand over the page. She did not look up.

"The tide's out at six," said her mother.

"Okay."

"Drink that."

She drank it. It was hot, with honey in it. Her mother sat with her hands on her knees and looked at the dark and the sea, and did not say anything about the notebook, and did not move, and stayed.

*Atlas.*

On the fourteenth day she asked Klein for the names.

She did not do it directly. She asked him to bring her, from the personnel office, "a copy of the Camp Four roster," in the voice of a person requesting more tea. He stood in the doorway with the tray. He had been with the family since before she was born, and his face, which was long and soft and grey-moustached, did not do anything at all for such a time that she knew he was deciding.

"Miss Weiss," he said.

"It's a copy."

"The office will want to know what it is for."

"Then you may tell them it's for me."

He went. He was gone an hour. He came back with a folder, which he laid on the desk, and stood a moment, and then went out and shut the door with the care of a man putting down something that might go off.

Thirty-one names.

They were typed on the company's paper, with a number beside each, and a *Position,* and a column marked *Next of Kin.* She turned the sheet. Two of them were on a second sheet. *Harrow, T., Foreman. Voss, E., Surveyor.* The others were *Miner, Grade II.* She read the list through. She had never seen a list so quiet.

She got out a notebook. It was one of the good ones, from her school days, with a marbled cover. She ruled it in margins. She began at the top, and copied, in the best of her hands, the first.

She did not think about why. She had a feeling that it was a thing a person ought to do, with her own hand, before she did anything else. Every name took a minute. It was like practising a signature. *Anselm Voe.* *Dara Quill.* *Pettigrew Oakes.* By the end of the evening she had written eleven, and her wrist hurt, and she had begun, without meaning to, to hear them in her head as she wrote, in a low murmur, like a roll.

She did the other twenty over three nights. She kept the book in the hatbox, under the glove and the sheet and the green cloth paperback, with the lid on.

* * *

*Menagerie.*

"You've been a great help with the nets," said her father.

He did not look at her. He was sitting on an upturned crate by the water, with a pipe he never lit, and his enormous hands lay on his knees. He was looking at the boats. He spoke as he did everything, slowly, as if each word had been carried a long way.

"Tobbe says you are slow."

"I am."

"He says you do not drop a stitch."

She did not answer. Her father nodded at the boats. The sea made a sound against the quay like someone folding a sheet.

"I did not like to ask," he said, "what you did."

"I know."

"I would like you to know that I will, if you want to say it. And I will listen. And I will not look away from you."

"I know."

"That is all."

He put the pipe in his mouth, and took it out. He did not turn his head. It was the whole of the conversation, and it had cost him, she saw, a very great deal; and she sat beside him on the quay with her ruled notebook in her bag and did not take it out.

That night she added a line.

*Cut the couplings (taught it to others). After: ?*

She sat and looked at the question mark. Then she added another, below it. And then a third.

* * *

*Atlas.*

"Miss Schnee. A pleasure."

Mr. Dacre was from Public Relations. He was a pleasant man of about forty, in a good grey suit, with the kind of face that always looks as if it has just come from a funeral where it had been of use. He came into the study on the twenty-fifth day with a leather folder under one arm and a boy behind him with a case. He did not sit until she sat.

"Your father tells me you've taken an interest in the Hollin Gap business."

"I've read the report."

"Then you will know it was a tragedy." He said it as a physician says *a pity.* "The Company feels it very deeply. We have a duty to the families, and it is the Company's wish to express that duty in the most personal way available." He tapped the folder. "Thirty-one letters of condolence. They would mean a great deal more, in our judgement, if they were signed by the family. By one of the family. Someone young. Your father thought of you at once."

She looked at the folder.

She had a feeling like having a hand put into hers. She had not expected it, and she was ashamed of how quickly it came. She had been sitting in this house for twenty-five days with a notebook she could not mention, and nothing in the world to do with her hands.

"May I see one?"

"Of course."

He took out the master. It was a single sheet on the Company's cream paper, and she took it and read it. She read it twice. *The Schnee Dust Company wishes to express its profound sorrow at the loss of your husband / wife / son / daughter / brother / sister... He will be remembered by all who worked beside him... the Company's thoughts are with your family at this time...*

It was a decent letter. It was a little stiff. It was true, so far as she could tell, in each of its parts.

"These are the cover," said Mr. Dacre. He turned the folder. In it, the letters lay in a stack, each squared with the next, and each folded back at the foot; and from every one there stood out, like a flag, a narrow tab of yellow paper, flagging a line on the lower half of the sheet. *Weiss Schnee, on behalf of the family.* "It's only the signature we need. The rest is set. We would be most grateful if you would sign where marked."

"I should read the other page."

"It is the same page, Miss Schnee. They've been proofed." He smiled. "I can have the boy bring the originals by tomorrow, if you'd rather. Though I'm afraid the post will have gone by then. The families have been waiting some time."

"Waiting."

"A long time."

She thought about the roster. She thought of the eleven names she had written, and the twenty she had not, and the family at the end of each, in a row.

"Give me the pen," she said.

It took two hours. He sat opposite her and turned the folder, one at a time, so that each flagged line came to her hand, and she signed it, and he drew it away. The boy blotted. She did not hurry. She wrote each name as she signed it, in her head, in the voice she had begun to hear. *Anselm Voe.* *Dara Quill.* By the fifteenth her wrist had begun to ache, and she changed her grip and went on. By the twentieth she had found a rhythm, a swing from the shoulder, the pen going down on the yellow tab and rising from it. It was a good hand. It was a hand her tutor had taught her. It was clear, and legible, and entirely her own.

When she finished, she laid down the pen.

Her fingers had cramped. She opened and shut them. Mr. Dacre was squaring the stack.

"Thirty-one," he said. "Thank you, Miss Schnee. You have been a great help."

She thought about it afterward. She thought about the way he had said it. She sat at the desk, with the window open, and flexed her hand, and let herself be glad of it.

She had been, she thought, of some use.
