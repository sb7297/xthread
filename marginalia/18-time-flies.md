# 18. time flies

**Day 13**

> SW light. −17. Dog on cot. Removed. —W.S.

*Kuo Kuana.* She mends nets. Tobbe sets a cup of water by her knee and looks, not at her, but at where she is looking, which is the horizon. He has noticed. He says nothing. He is a man of long practice.

**Day 14**

> W. −19. Dog on cot. —W.S.

**Day 15**

> W. −18. Dog on cot. Dog fed. This is not a decision. —W.S.

**Day 18**

> Calm. −22. Learned to bank the stove. Wood use down by a third (T.D.). —W.S.

*Kuo Kuana.* Her father sets a roll of notes on the table by her bowl. She pushes it back. He pushes it forward.

"It is a loan," says Ghira. "You may repay it by writing."

Her mother, at the stove, does not turn round. "Don't look at me. It was his idea."

**Day 21**

> NE. −24. Chopped wood. Forty-one logs. Blister, left palm, where the glove is cut. Wear gloves. —W.S.

*The ferry.* Third class, which is the deck. She sleeps among the net-bags with her back against a bollard and the grey coat over her head. The sea is flat. She watches it for six hours, and is sick, and then isn't.

**Day 24**

> Snow. −20. Read the others' initials through. A.V. writes the best. Corrected the date on the 14th. Not my place. Left it corrected. —W.S.

*Vale.* From the window of the northbound train, at dusk, there is a long grey ridge against the sky, and on it a stump.

She does not look. She looks. The train does not stop.

**Day 27**

> NNE. −28. Humming. No objection. —W.S.

*The train.* It gets colder one station at a time. At the fifth a woman with a child gets on. The child stares at the ears, with wide and perfect candour; the mother's hand comes down on its shoulder and turns it. Blake looks at the window. After a minute she finds she is humming. A man across the aisle looks up from his paper.

She stops.

**Day 29**

> Thursday. T.D. with fish. She says my stove is "less loud." Spelled Voe two ways in my own notes. Corrected. —W.S.

**Day 31**

> W. −22. Began a letter. Did not finish. —W.S.

*Atlas.* She walks three miles round the wall to find the back. The door is green. It is opened by a broad woman in an apron and a cap, who stands in it a long time with a ladle in her hand and looks at Blake's head, and then at her face, and then at her boots.

"Sit," says the woman.

It is soup. It is hot and thick and has barley in it. Blake eats it at the end of a scrubbed table with her coat on and the woman's back to her at the range.

"She isn't here," says the cook. "I haven't seen you."

"I only—"

"Up past Mantle. There's an old camp at the Gap. Number Four." A spoon scrapes. "Mind the ice by the second post."

Blake sets down the bowl.

"She wears it, you know," says the cook, to the pot. "The glove. Black. On the left, with the other white. Nobody's said a word."

There is a long silence in the kitchen. The range ticks. Blake sits with both hands round the empty bowl, and does not move her ears, because she does not trust them, and the woman does not turn round, and after a time hands her a loaf wrapped in a cloth.

**Day 33**

> Overcast. −23. Letter. Did not finish. —W.S.

*Mantle.* The depot is a shed. A man with a face the colour of a boot says there is nothing up there, that the line was cut in the spring, that they have a wall on it.

"I know," says Blake.

He takes her to the foot of the gap for forty lien. She pays twenty and says she will pay twenty on the other end. He looks at her for some time and does not ask which other end.

**Day 36**

> Wind. −30. Said one name aloud, to the dog. Anselm. The dog was unmoved. —W.S.

*The foot of the gap.* He sets her down where the track gives out. "If you're not back by the thirtieth I'll not come looking," he says, with a certain bleakness, and goes.

**Day 38**

> Clear. −31. Cold enough to hear the river. —W.S.

*The gap.* On the first night her ears freeze. She wakes with them stiff as paper, and ties her scarf over them, round and round, and lies in the dark in the snow hole she has cut, thinking about a ribbon. The thought does not seem to belong to her. It comes from a long way off, from a room with four beds and a lamp.

**Day 41**

> Calm. −33. Cleaned the radio. It gives out a hum if I turn the dial. No one on any channel. Turned it off. —W.S.

*The gap.* The track is not a track. She follows it by other things: a scat, a scrape, the line where the snow has been lightly packed. At noon she finds the print of a dog. It is a good clear print, one foot to the one before, travelling up.

She follows the dog.

**Day 44**

> Snow. −27. Re-read p. 14. Wrote nothing. —W.S.

*A hay shed.* A storm. She sits it out in a lean-to at the side of a deserted farm with a tin of fish and the grey coat and her knees against her chest. It lasts three days. She keeps the count by the number of times her ears hurt. She reaches nineteen, and stops counting, and takes out of her bag a cheap notebook, ruled in columns, and does not open it.

She holds it on her knee.

After some time she takes the pencil out of her pocket and writes on the inside of the cover, in letters so small she can barely see them herself, a line she has been carrying since the ferry.

*Rule 12.*

She doesn't know what comes next. She leaves it there.

**Day 47**

> Gale, NW. −34. Third attempt at the letter. I know the address. I do not know the sentence. —W.S.

**Day 49**

> Storm. No sun. Light for perhaps a quarter of the day. Dog restless. —W.S.

*The gap.* She goes up in it. There is nothing to see. She walks with her hands out in front of her, like a blind woman, and the wind comes down off the hill in sheets, and the snow goes in at her collar and her cuffs and the corner of her eye. She walks by sound.

She can hear the river, under the ice, talking to itself.

She can hear her own pulse.

She can hear, a long way up and to the left, so faint that she thinks at first it is the wind's own invention, a thin hollow note, repeated at intervals, like someone knocking on a door.

A dog barking.

**Day 50**

> Storm. −35. Dog at the door since noon. Lit the lamp in the window. (No reason.) —W.S.

*The camp.* It is nearly dark. She has been climbing for two hours with her head down. She comes up over the lip of the shelf on her hands, and straightens, and cannot see anything: not a roof, not a wall; there is only white, moving, the width of her arm.

Then, low down and to the right, a little yellow square.

It hangs in the white. It does not flicker. It is very small and quite steady, and it is the only thing in the world.
