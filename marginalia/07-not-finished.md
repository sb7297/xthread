# 7. not finished

"Hendry died this morning," said Weiss.

She said it to her desk. She was reading something on her scroll, a company bulletin, one of the ones her father's office sent every day at seven, and she read the line once and then said it aloud in the voice of a person reading it back to herself, to check.

Blake was on her bunk with a book. Ruby was in the shower. Yang was out.

"Cornelius Hendry. Forty-one. He had three children." Weiss turned the scroll face down. "I didn't know his name until Tuesday. I looked it up. He drove a regional line for eleven years."

There was nothing to say to that and so Blake didn't say it. She turned a page she had not read.

That night, at the washbasin, she took the pencil from her pocket and tore a leaf from the back of the book. She wrote four words and a letter. She looked at it for a time. Then she folded it once and left it on Weiss's desk, under the lamp, where it would be found.

*Roof, after lights. If you're willing. —B.*

* * *

The roof of the east wing was reached by a ladder, a hatch, and a long cold stretch of gravel with a lip at the edge. The moon was broken, as it always was, a big pale disc with a bite out of one side, and the cliff was somewhere past the lip, and past that the lights of Vale in a spill.

She had taken off the ribbon. Her ears were out in the wind and the wind was the worst of it. It was unbearable and it was clean. She sat with her back against the housing of the ventilation shaft and her knees up and her hands clasped around them, and she waited, and did not know if she was waiting for a person.

The hatch opened at a quarter past midnight.

Weiss climbed out in her coat. She stood. She had her hands in her pockets and her chin up, and in the half-light she looked like a person who had come to inspect a property. She did not come any nearer than the hatch.

"I got your note."

"Thank you for coming."

"I'm not sure that—" She stopped. "Go on."

Blake looked at the lights. It was easier than looking at the person.

"I should tell you something. Before you're kind to me anymore."

"I was not aware that I—"

"The books."

"Ah."

"I was in the White Fang."

The wind took it, and brought it back.

She had thought about this for three years, off and on, and had run through it in the dark of other rooms in other cities, and in none of the versions had it been this short. It was one sentence. It was all there was.

"Six years. Since I was twelve. My father started it and he left when it changed, and I stayed. I was—" She heard her own voice go level, the way it did, the way it had in the cellars. "I was good at it."

"At what?"

"Getting in. First through a door. I could do a lock in under a minute." She held her hands out in front of her in the moonlight. They were quite still. "The cut on the couplings. You said it was always the same."

"Yes."

"It's a particular cut. It's called a – it's got a name. I taught it to people."

Weiss did not speak.

"I didn't do the one on the news. I'd left by then. But I know who did." She lowered her hands. "I did others. I don't think I killed anyone. I don't *know*. I held doors. I carried things. I stood where I was told. I knew what we were for and I stayed. I stayed for a long time after I knew."

"Hendry," said Weiss.

"I know the name."

"Why did you leave?"

"Because of a train."

It was the whole answer. She had no other.

She had expected something. Shouting; or a hand going to a weapon; or a sound like the sound of the cafeteria when the tray went down. What she got was Weiss standing at the hatch with her hands in her pockets. Her face in the moonlight was very white. She was looking at the middle distance between Blake and the sky.

She said, "Excuse me."

She went down through the hatch, and the hatch came down after her, and shut with a small flat noise.

Blake sat on the roof until she could not feel her hands.

* * *

It took four minutes.

She had always been able to do it in four. The duffel from under the bunk; the second pair of boots; the blanket, rolled; the book; the tin with the knife-oil. Not the lamp. Not the ribbon. She left the ribbon on the pillow, and then she thought, *with what, then,* and took it again.

Yang was asleep on her back with her mouth open. Ruby had a sock on her face. Weiss's bunk was empty and made.

She sat on her own bed with the bag at her feet and her coat on and the light off.

She thought it through. It was a skill. Weiss would go to the Headmaster, or to the Dean, or to her father's lawyers; it would be a night, or a day. There would be a woman with a clipboard. They would not arrest her at Beacon. They would put her on a train. She did not think they would put her in a cell. She did not know where the train would go. She tried to think of a city she had not already left.

She listened.

Every sound in the building came to her through the floor. Someone on the third floor with a tap running. A heel in the corridor. When she heard heels her ears went flat against her skull with a small clicking effort, because the one heel she was waiting for would be Glynda's, and it did not come. Every bell she counted. Two. Three.

At four she began to think about the door. She had, over the weeks, come to know the sound of it: the way it stuck at the top, how it gave with a small suck of air. She thought, *She will open it, and that will be the end.* And she thought, *She won't open it,* and her ears went flat again.

At ten past five, someone came up the stairs.

She knew the tread. It was a very upright tread, even and light, a person who walked as if the stairs were being watched. It came along the corridor. It stopped at the door, and stayed stopped for a time, and she thought *Now.*

The door opened. It stuck at the top.

Weiss stood in the doorway.

She was in her coat. The coat was dark at the shoulders and there was frost in her hair, which was down; she had taken it out of its tie, and it hung loose over one shoulder, white and a little wild. Her cheeks were red with the cold. She had been walking for hours and it was written all over her. She was wearing one glove and holding the other.

She looked at the bag.

She looked at it for what felt like a long time. Her face did not change. Then she looked at Blake, who had not moved, and said, quite clearly, in the voice she used to open a recitation:

"I am not finished being angry."

Blake said nothing.

"I should like you to remain until I am."

* * *

She crossed the room and sat on her own bunk, across from Blake, with her hands between her knees.

"I've been walking," she said. "I went to the cliff and back. Twice. I composed several speeches."

"Okay."

"None of them were adequate. They had a good deal of *whereas.*" She was looking at the floor. "I want to be very clear that I am angry. That is the first thing. I'd like it noted."

"Noted."

"I am angry that you didn't tell me. I am angry that I sat there—" She stopped. "I sat there at that table and said *a gang with a slogan* with you eating fish. I'm angry about the train. I'm angry that a man called Hendry had three children and I'd never heard of him. I am very angry that I can't tell you which of those it is. I thought if I walked far enough it would sort itself."

"Did it?"

"No."

"How long?"

"What?"

"How long will you be."

"I don't know. I've never done this before."

She said it with such a lack of humour that Blake did not know, for a moment, whether it was a joke. Then she saw that it was not. It was a plain fact. It was a thing a person said when she had been taught that there was a way of doing it, and had been taught it wrong.

Weiss lifted her head.

"Tell me one thing you did."

"What?"

"One. A specific. I can't be angry at a category. I've been trying since midnight."

Blake looked at the bag.

She had meant to say something general. She had a kind of statement, ready, that she gave to people: *I was involved in activities.* But there was the face in front of her, with its frost-burned cheeks and the red-rimmed eye and the scar, and the pale hair loose, and it was waiting, and it was not going to take the statement.

"In Mistral," she said. "There was a man carrying a crate. It was Dust. We were moving it, and there was a door, and I was nearest. I held the door for him. I knew where the crate was going. It was going to a shop. The man had hired a – it doesn't matter. It was going to be used on him." Her voice stopped, and found itself again. "I held the door. He said, *thanks.*"

"He thanked you."

"I said, *sure.*"

The radiator knocked. It was warm in the room, for once. Yang turned over.

"Thank you," said Weiss.

"For what?"

"For not saying you were sorry."

Blake stared at her.

"If you had," said Weiss, "I believe I should have said something I couldn't take back. I don't want it yet."

"Yet."

"Yes."

She stood, and began to put her gloves on, and found she was already wearing one and took it off again.

"I am going to the library to be angry in there," she said. "You may come, if you like. I'd prefer you didn't speak."

* * *

The library opened at six. The night librarian was still there; he had been there since ten the evening before and showed no sign of leaving. He looked up when they came in. He looked at the clock, and at the duffel Blake had forgotten to put down, and at Weiss's hair. He did not ask.

Weiss took the east table. Blake sat on the other side, two chairs along, with the bag at her feet. Neither of them opened anything.

It was a long time. The light came in at the window, grey, then white. The pipes started up. A boy came in, saw them, and went out again. Blake watched Weiss not read. She did it with great concentration, with a pencil against her lip and her eyes fixed on a point two inches above the page. At some point she began to hum, three notes, a rest, and a phrase, and stopped, and began again, and stopped, and her mouth went thin.

Blake's ears moved. She let them.

At eight, a saucer was set down on the table between them.

It had an orange on it, peeled, and divided into its segments, and laid out in a ring. The librarian did not speak. He went back to his desk. The smell came up from the plate, very strong in the cold room, and Weiss looked at it as if it had been an official document.

After a while she took a segment. She ate it with her whole attention. She wiped her fingers on her handkerchief.

Blake took one. She licked her thumb.

Weiss looked at the wall.

* * *

It was noon before they went back. Yang opened one eye when they came in and said, "Where've you been?" and Weiss said "Library," and Yang said, "Both of you?" and Weiss said "Yes," and Yang said, "At six in the morning," and nobody answered. Blake lay down on her bunk, in her coat, with the bag still at her feet, and was asleep before she had finished thinking that she would not be.

She woke to the sound of a pen.

It was a small sound, one she knew. A nib, on paper, going steadily. She lay still and kept her eyes shut and listened to it go. It went for some time. Then it stopped, and the cap clicked on.

When she opened her eyes, Weiss was standing at the door with the pen in her hand. She was not looking at Blake. She was looking at the sheet on the wall.

Blake got up and went to read it.

*12. No leaving without notice.*

It was beneath the eleventh in the same neat black ink. It was in ink.

"That's not a rule you can enforce," said Blake.

"It is in ink."

"That's not what makes a—"

"It's in ink."

Blake looked at the pen. After a moment, she took the pencil stub from her pocket and wrote, beside the twelfth, in small upright letters: *(Emergencies excepted.)*

Weiss looked at it. She took the cap off the pen. She struck out the parenthesis with a single line, neat as a ruler, and capped the pen.

"I don't want a promise," she said, to the wall.

"That's all?"

"It is a great deal." Her voice was very dry and very careful. "I do not want to be told you'll never go. I wouldn't believe it, and if I did, it would be worse. I'd like notice. If you're going. Tell me you're going."

Blake read the line again. She read the strike-through.

She put the pencil back in her pocket.

"Okay," she said.

Weiss nodded. She put the pen on the desk, squared with the edge, and went out to find breakfast, and it was not until the door had closed that Blake understood she had left her scroll behind, face up, on the desk, with the bulletin still open on it, where anyone could read it.

She did not read it. She turned it face down.

Then she unpacked the bag.
